## What This Is

The logging architecture the memo asks for, checked against what the corpus already holds and against what the storage layer will actually do: **the memo proposes answering the question of what is going on with a vault by having instrumented clients send records into a data vault over the vault-to-vault append channel with its pre-agreed secret, accepting the same class of abuse that any client-side analytics accepts, landing records in temporal folders in a raw vault, then consolidating completed hours and days once and never again, with a separate visualisation and code vault holding the pipeline that reads the data vaults, and with rotation of data vaults as volume grows; two of those ideas are older than the memo remembers, since the transformation pattern is documented as LETS rather than LEPS in four project-lead briefs beginning 13 February and the process-once rule is specified far more precisely than the memo states it, as a finality model in which a bucket is partial while its period is open and final once closed, cascading fractally so a day is partial until its twenty-four hours are final, with the rule that you only calculate what is missing, and both were deferred by a recorded decision on the day they were proposed and neither has ever been built; what is genuinely new is the subject, because per-vault observability has never been specified anywhere, and there is a deliberate decision on record not to instrument vault reads on the ground that they are high frequency and would create noise, in a review that names the exact function to change if vault analytics ever become desired, so this memo is the moment that condition is met; the correction that changes the design is that the memo's central performance intuition, that a write is just a fast S3 put, is true of one lane and false of the other, since the append lane is deliberately outside the commit DAG and costs one flat put with no tree rewrite and no commit, whereas the raw vault the memo wants to git-commit into pays, for every record, a full rewrite of its folder's tree object and of every ancestor tree up to the root, which for a folder holding a thousand records of two hundred bytes is on the order of a thousand times the record in rewritten tree bytes, and this compounds because there is no deduplication, no packing, no compaction and no garbage collection anywhere in the system; four further cliffs follow in a known order, being history walks that truncate silently at one hundred, two hundred and five hundred commits so that a busy log vault stops reconciling cleanly rather than reporting an error, an append lane that refuses at a thousand pending files and pays a full listing of that folder on every single write, a clone path measured at two hundred and two seconds for a vault of forty-two commits and two thousand three hundred and seventy-five objects with the fix specified and unshipped, and a listing endpoint whose own architect recorded it as acceptable below a thousand files while the zip endpoint reads every object ever written to the vault twice inside a thirty-second function; the shape that survives all of this is already written down in an architect review as an append-only event log with a tiny marker on the hot path and a folding job behind it, and the rotation instinct in the memo arrives there too as time-sharded append tokens, which shards the cheap thing rather than the expensive one; and the question the memo actually asks, when does a vault become slow, has never been measured, although a performance harness with a burst-save scenario and buttons to create a hundred small files already ships in the vault interface and has never had a published run.** New contributions: **the two lanes separated by cost with the amplification derived, the seven cliffs ordered by when this workload meets them, the four-tier store mapped onto the lane each tier can afford, rotation resolved as token sharding before vault sharding, the deliberate no-instrumentation decision located as the thing this memo overturns, two naming collisions caught, and the unrun harness identified as the cheapest next action in the document.**

## The Two Claims, Checked

**The transformation pattern is real and the memo has the name wrong.** It is LETS, and there is no LEPS anywhere in the corpus. Under its real name it is well documented: the project lead's own brief of 13 February states it as "Load, Extract, Transform, Save" with the operative rule that "when you query, save the result", a dev brief of 22 April gives the fullest stage-by-stage definition with a worked example and the property that "each LETS pass is idempotent", and an architecture brief of 1 May generalises it beyond analytics.

**One inconsistency should be settled before it is cited.** The dedicated reference file in the CloudFront pack expands the S as **Store**, and it is the only document that does; every brief by the project lead says **Save**. If this architecture is going to name the pattern, it should pick one and correct the other, because both are currently canonical-looking.

**The process-once rule is documented more precisely than the memo states it**, and the extra precision is worth adopting. The daily brief of 16 February specifies it as a **finality model**: a time bucket is either partial, meaning its period is still open and its aggregation is provisional and recomputed on request, or final, meaning the period has completed and the aggregation will never change and is cached permanently. It cascades fractally, so a day is partial until all twenty-four of its hours are final. And the operative optimisation is stated as **only calculate what is missing**, with a manifest tracking what has been computed and what is final.

**Neither has been built.** A decision recorded on 13 February deferred the pipeline on the ground that aggregation was not needed yet, the reference pack names four services to write and none of them exists, and the only trace in shipped code is a comment on a schema that nothing computes. **So this is the tenth and eleventh instance of the corpus proposing already-specified work, both in one memo**, and the useful response is to inherit the finality vocabulary rather than to restate it in new words.

## Nobody Has Specified What Is Going On With A Vault

This part of the memo is genuinely new, and there is one document that makes it more pointed than it looks.

Vault reads are **deliberately not instrumented today**. A dev review of 11 March records that the vault read functions do not record analytics events, unlike the transfer download endpoints which do, and states the reason: vault reads are high frequency, since a clone or pull reads dozens of objects, and recording each would create noise. It then ends with the sentence that makes this memo the moment the decision gets revisited: **"However, if vault analytics become desired, this is where to add them."**

**So the first thing this architecture inherits is a reason to be careful.** The objection was volume, and volume is exactly what the cost analysis below is about. An architecture that answers it by writing every read into a git-committed vault would be building the noise the earlier decision refused.

Two adjacent things exist and neither is this. Server analytics ships in part and is about the site rather than about vaults. And a write-only monitoring vault was specified on 2 August, quoting the project lead on one vault sending messages and logs to another and on batching because most data is only processed when the monitoring vault is opened; it is catalogued as proposed, scoped to a field demo, and its transport is the append lane this memo also uses.

## The Correction: Two Lanes, Two Costs

The memo's performance claim is that "every time we have a write, we have a simple S3 file" and that this is "very fast to add data". **That is exactly true of the append lane and it is not true of a git commit**, and the whole architecture turns on the difference.

**The append lane is outside the commit DAG by design.** Its storage lives under a separate prefix, managed by the tooling but deliberately not in the version-controlled commit tree. A record written there is one flat put. There is no tree rewrite, no commit object, no ref advance, and no history.

**A committed write is not one put.** Writing one file into a folder produces a new blob, a new tree object for that folder, **a new tree object for every ancestor folder up to the root**, a new commit object, and a ref write. These are batched into a single request, so it is one round trip, but it is not one object and it is not a constant number of bytes.

**The amplification is in the tree rewrite, and it scales with how many files are already in the folder.** A tree entry carries the file name, size and content hash each encrypted separately and base64 encoded, plus the blob identifier, which comes to roughly two hundred and eighty bytes per entry. This figure is derived from the entry schema rather than measured, and one measured data point in the corpus is consistent with it: a subtree of thirteen encrypted entries observed at about eight kilobytes.

| Records already in the folder | Tree rewritten per new record | To store a 200 byte record |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | about 28 KB | about 140 times the record |
| 1,000 | about 280 KB | **about 1,400 times the record** |
| 5,000 | about 1.4 MB | about 7,000 times the record |
| 10,000 | about 2.8 MB | **exceeds the request budget; commits into that folder begin to fail** |

**And nothing reclaims any of it.** There is no deduplication, because the same plaintext encrypted twice produces different ciphertext and therefore a different object, so even rewriting an identical record creates a new object. There is no delta compression, no packing, and **no garbage collection**: every version of every tree object ever written is still there. A vault of forty-two commits was measured holding two thousand three hundred and seventy-five tree objects, which is about fifty-six objects per commit.

**This is why the memo's own instinct that the raw vault will produce "a lot of git commits" and will therefore need another vault is right, and why the reason is worse than volume alone.** It is not that there are many commits. It is that each one rewrites the folder it lands in.

## The Cliffs, In The Order This Workload Meets Them

| # | Cliff | Threshold | How known |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Tree rewrite amplification** on every committed record | Immediate, and painful in the low thousands per folder | **Derived** from the entry schema and the ancestor rebuild |
| 2 | **History walks truncate silently.** Ahead and behind counts stop at 100, ancestry at 200, common ancestor at 500. Not errors: wrong answers | Four-figure commit counts | **In code** |
| 3 | **Append lane refuses at 1,000 pending files** per token, and **every append lists that folder before writing**, so the hot path slows as the lane fills | 1,000 pending, per token | **In code** |
| 4 | **Clone and cold open.** Measured at 202 seconds for 42 commits and 2,375 objects, at roughly 50 ms per object server side. The fix is specified and **has not shipped** | Thousands of objects, and it never improves | **Measured**, in a document |
| 5 | **Listing and zip.** The list endpoint enumerates every object; the zip endpoint reads every object **twice** inside a 30 second function. The architect's own note says acceptable below 1,000 files | About 1,000 files | **In code**, with the design point stated in a review |
| 6 | **A single folder's tree exceeds the request budget** and commits into it fail with an opaque error | About 10,000 entries in one folder | **Derived** |
| 7 | **Object identifiers are 48 bits**, being a truncated hash, and a collision silently overwrites | Roughly 0.2 per cent at a million objects, 18 per cent at ten million | **Derived, and undiscussed anywhere in the corpus** |

**Cliff 2 is the one to internalise**, because it is the only one that fails quietly and in the wrong direction. A log vault past a few hundred unreconciled commits does not report a limit; it reports that it is diverged when it is merely behind.

**Cliff 7 deserves a decision rather than a note.** An append-forever store with no collection is precisely the workload that walks into a birthday bound, and nothing in the corpus has ever raised it. It is not urgent at the scale this starts at, and it is much cheaper to widen an identifier before there are ten million objects than after.

## The Architecture That Survives This

Four tiers, and the tier boundary that matters is which lane each can afford.

```
   INSTRUMENTED CLIENT
        |
        |  append token, one per channel and per time shard
        |  a flat put, no commit, blind writer
        v
   TIER 1  QUEUE           append lane, outside the commit DAG
        |                  cost: one put. ceiling: 1,000 pending
        |
        |  drain: load, sanitise, mark processed
        |  batch many records into ONE commit
        v
   TIER 2  RAW             committed, temporal folders
        |                  type / year / month / day / hour
        |                  one file per BATCH, never per record
        |
        |  fold completed windows once. finality: partial vs final
        v
   TIER 3  CONSOLIDATED    committed, one file per closed window
        |                  hour folds to day folds to month
        |
        |  when a shard is spent
        v
   TIER 4  ARCHIVE         retired vault, read key published,
                           write key escrowed or deliberately dropped

   VISUALISATION AND CODE VAULT
        reads tiers 2, 3 and 4 as linked vaults, holds the pipeline
```

**Tier 1 stays in the append lane for as long as possible**, because that is the only tier where the memo's fast-write intuition holds. The lane's properties are also better than the memo credits: the token names the folder, so **the token is the channel** rather than merely identifying who sent; a token holder can write and cannot list, read, purge or configure, those being gated by two other credentials; and the writer is blind, since the filename is assigned by the server and never returned, with a test asserting exactly that. One correction of vocabulary: it authorises on a **bearer secret rather than a signature**, so the memo's "pre-agreed secret" is the right description and any document calling it a signature will mislead.

**Tier 2 is where the memo's open question gets a numeric answer.** The memo treats one file per record against batching against zipping as a business decision. In the append lane it genuinely is one. **In the committed raw vault it is not**, because one file per record is the case that pays the amplification in the table above. The primitive that fixes this already exists: an add-many call that writes N files in one commit and one request. It is not wired to any timer, and it should be what the drain step calls.

**Tier 3 inherits finality rather than reinventing it.** A window is partial while open and final once closed, a final window is never recomputed, and the cascade is fractal. The one thing to add for this store, which the February specification did not need, is that **the consolidated file should record which raw commits it folded**, so a reprocess is verifiable rather than a matter of trust.

**Tier 4 is the memo's rotation idea and it has a conceptual ancestor rather than a mechanism.** A brief of 20 March states that a vault exists to meet an objective and that "when the objective is met, the vault becomes an archive". There is no verb for this, no state, no endpoint, and nothing in the catalogue of proposed vault capabilities. Two adjacent concepts exist and **must not be mistaken for it**, because ephemeral vaults and paid time-limited vaults both **delete** rather than archive, which is the opposite outcome.

## Rotate The Token Before The Vault

The memo reaches for vault rotation, a vault per day or week or month, retired to an archive. The corpus reaches the same conclusion from the payments side and lands one level cheaper: an architect review recommends **time-sharded append tokens, one per day**, as the answer to a single token's namespace filling.

**That is the better first move and it is available today.** A token per day gives a fresh thousand-file allowance, makes the drain job's unit of work obvious, makes retention a matter of purging a token's folder, and requires no new vault, no new key to escrow and no archive state that does not exist yet. **Rotate the vault when the vault is the thing that is full**, meaning when tier 2's committed history rather than tier 1's queue is what has grown, and that is a much later problem.

There is a real consequence to record. **A vault whose write key is lost is frozen**: readable forever and never updatable, which the corpus already documents as a failure mode with escrow as the mitigation. **Retirement is that state chosen on purpose**, which means it is reachable today by dropping a write key, and that is a decision to take deliberately with the read key published first, rather than a feature to wait for.

## Who Does The Processing, And What That Costs Now

The memo answers this and the answer is a constraint rather than a preference: the processing runs in the browser as a vault app for now, which is why the linked-vault write path had to be solved first, and it moves later to a scheduled or real-time runner.

**The morning's brief is the dependency.** A processor vault reading and writing several data vaults is the read-write linked mount that ships on the app lane and not on the tree lane, and the credential and attribution gaps recorded there apply directly here: nothing in a data vault's history will record that the processor vault authored a commit, which for a log store is the wrong way round.

**Two things about a browser processor should be stated now.** The batching that tier 2 needs is not available to it, because each write through the app bridge is still its own commit; the commit queue was specified on 26 May and **has not shipped**, what ships is a two and a half second debounce on the push rather than on the commit, and the batching call the flush controller wants does not exist outside a mock. **Until that lands, a browser-based drain job is the amplification case rather than the fix**, and the honest options are to keep the drain in the append lane, to write consolidated output only, or to ship the batch call.

**And the eventual move off the browser is a change of trust, not only of location.** A scheduled runner holds a write key continuously, which the browser does not, and that is the point at which this stops being client-side analytics with client-side analytics' accepted abuse and becomes a service with a credential to protect.

## The Measurement Nobody Has Taken

**There is no measured performance data anywhere in the corpus for a many-records, many-commits vault.** The two large-vault numbers that exist are a 251 megabyte vault of 155 large files, which is the easy direction, and the 202 second clone, which is the closest analogue and is the discouraging one.

**The harness to answer it already ships.** A performance harness lives in the vault interface with instrumentation, scenarios and setup pages, its scenario list includes a burst of ten saves back to back described as an autosave loop, and its setup page has buttons to create one, ten or a hundred small files. **No run of it has ever been published**: there is no results file, no debrief and no number.

So the memo's question has a cheap answer available. **Run it.** Three curves are worth having before any of this is built: commit time against files already in the folder, which is cliff 1 and can be read directly; clone and open time against commit count, which is cliffs 2 and 4; and append time against pending count, which is cliff 3 and should show the listing cost. That is an afternoon on existing code, and it converts every derived figure in this document into a measured one.

## Two Naming Collisions

Both are cheap now and expensive after a schema ships. This is the seventh and eighth caught in this corpus.

**Manifest already means something.** In the storage layer it is a three-field authentication record per vault. The February finality specification uses the same word for the structure tracking which windows have been computed and which are final. **Those are different objects at different layers and one of them must be renamed**; the finality one is the newer and should move.

**Raw already means something too.** The append lane's own folders are pending and processed, and the memo's tier 2 is called raw. A drained record is not raw in the sense of untouched, since it has been sanitised on the way through. **Call tier 2 what it is**, which is the first committed form.

## Build Order

| # | Step | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Run the harness and publish three curves** | It exists, it is unused, and it turns derived numbers into measured ones before anything is designed around them |
| 2 | Instrument into the append lane only, one token per channel per day | The one lane where the memo's fast-write assumption holds, and it needs nothing new |
| 3 | The drain job, marking processed, using the add-many call | The single place that decides whether tier 2 is affordable |
| 4 | Temporal layout and the first committed form, one file per batch | And record the batch's record count, so the folder's growth is visible |
| 5 | Finality, adopting the February vocabulary unchanged | Including which raw commits each consolidated file folded |
| 6 | The visualisation vault reading the data vaults | Depends on the morning's brief, and on nothing else here |
| 7 | Retirement as a deliberate act, read key published and write key dropped or escrowed | It is reachable today; what is missing is the decision and the record of it, not a feature |
| 8 | A decision on the 48 bit object identifier | Cheap before ten million objects, and not afterwards |

**Steps 1 to 3 are the whole of the risk.** If the drain job batches, the rest is ordinary work. If it does not, tiers 2 and 3 are built on the amplification case and the vault gets slow for a reason nobody will see in a code review.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a schema for a log record.** It maps the tiers and their costs and says nothing about what a record contains, which is business specific as the memo says.
- **Not a replacement for the February pipeline specification.** LETS and the finality model are inherited from it; this locates them, corrects one name and adds the cost model they never had.
- **Not a design for the server-side variant.** The memo names a cloud function as a later option, and the trust change that implies is noted rather than designed.
- **Not a privacy or abuse analysis.** The memo accepts the client-side abuse surface by analogy with any client-side analytics, and this brief takes that as given.
- **Not a measurement.** Every number here is either read from code, quoted from a document, or derived and labelled as such, and the document's own first build step is to go and measure.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Keeping records in the append lane | It is the only affordable write, and it is outside version control, so the thing this estate sells is absent from the tier that receives the data |
| Batching for the raw tier | It is the difference between viable and not, and a batch is a coarser unit than an event, so a partial batch is a lost window |
| The 20 March objective-driven framing | It makes retirement natural, and the mechanism does not exist, so the honest version today is a deliberate freeze rather than a feature |
| Processing in the browser | It forces the linked-vault work to be finished properly, and it is the environment with the worst commit behaviour of the three candidates |
| Publishing the derived amplification figure | It is the most decision-changing number here, and it is arithmetic on a schema rather than an observation, which is why step one is to measure it |
| Instrumenting vault reads at all | The memo's question needs them, and a review already refused them for the volume reason this document then confirms |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| Save or Store? | Four documents say Save and the reference file says Store. **Pick one and correct the other before citing the pattern** |
| What is the unit of a batch? | Time, count or size, and the drain job cannot be written until this is chosen |
| Are vault reads instrumented or only writes? | The reads are the interesting signal and the expensive one, and the 11 March decision refused them on exactly that ground |
| Does a consolidated file record what it folded? | Proposed here and absent from the February specification, and it is what makes a reprocess checkable |
| When is the vault the thing that is full, rather than the token? | Token sharding defers vault rotation and does not remove it, and nobody has the curve that says when |
| Is retirement a state or an act? | It is reachable today by dropping a write key, and whether that should be named, recorded and made reversible is a product decision |
| What widens the object identifier, and when? | Undiscussed anywhere. The workload proposed here is the one that reaches the bound |
