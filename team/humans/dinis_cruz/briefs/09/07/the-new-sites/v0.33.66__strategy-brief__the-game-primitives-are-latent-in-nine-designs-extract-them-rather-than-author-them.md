## What This Is

The games site the memo asks for, and the authoring structure underneath it, both grounded in what already exists: **the memo proposes a parent site at a games subdomain that links to individual game sites, explains why games are being used at all, carries the building blocks and the guidance, holds reference implementations for the agents that will build later games, and archives the experiments already done, then asks the real question, which is what primitives a game needs once a topic is chosen, naming storylines, materials, players, characters and narratives as candidates; the answer should not be authored, because nine game designs already exist across this corpus and the repository, four of them written in June and July as concept briefs and five in the last twelve days as full designs, and reading them side by side yields a structure they all share plus three slots that recur as failures rather than as features, which is a stronger foundation than a list invented from the outside and has the property that it can be used immediately to score the nine and find which slots are empty; the count is also the argument for the site, since of nine designs exactly one is built, the parked item recording that games are specified and not coded has been carried since 26 August and has grown rather than shrunk, and a site whose first job is publishing the ones that exist would convert a backlog into an asset; the memo's reason for doing this at all is right and the corpus supplies the constraint that governs it, because a brief of 20 August establishes from the published evidence that a strong threat message paired with a weak efficacy message produces defensive denial rather than behaviour change, which means a game that dramatises danger without handing the player something that works performs worse than no game, and that single rule decides more about these designs than any question of engine or framework; the site's own job is composition, and composition is precisely where this family has a recorded defect, since the site-access report of 20 August found three sites holding three thirds of one answer with every cross-link pointing at a domain rather than at the page that answers the question, and stated that a domain link is a referral rather than a composition, so a hub that links to domains would reproduce the exact failure it exists to fix; the archive the memo wants was specified on 14 August as a submission queue rather than a catalogue of written pages, on the explicit ground that a design requiring a written page per vault stalls at the third entry, with an asymmetry between the four things a human supplies and everything an agent derives from the read key, and it was never built; and the reference-implementation pattern the memo asks for exists in four established forms in this corpus, one of which was already produced for a game two days ago, so what is missing is the place to put it rather than the format.** New contributions: **the nine designs counted and scored against a structure extracted from them, twelve primitives with the three that recur as failures separated out, the efficacy rule promoted from a page-design constraint to the governing rule of the whole programme, the composition defect named as the hub's first acceptance test, the archive located as already specified, and a naming collision caught on the word this site will most want to use.**

## The Primitives, Extracted Rather Than Invented

The memo offers storylines, materials, players, characters and narratives as candidates. Reading the nine, those are not the load-bearing parts. **Twelve primitives recur, and they divide into three groups.**

### The five that define the game

| # | Primitive | What it is | Worked example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The claim** | One finding the game exists to transmit. Not a topic | *An insurer that profits too much has failed as surely as one that makes a loss* |
| 2 | **The seat** | Who the player is, and specifically **what they cannot do** | *You never act. You only decide what may happen* |
| 3 | **The loop** | The repeating unit of play, in named beats | *A working day in four beats: allocate, watch, decide, settle* |
| 4 | **The tension** | The reason no single dial wins | *Three ways to lose and two of them are opposite* |
| 5 | **The output** | What leaves the game and is worth keeping | *The gap between what you predicted and what was found* |

**Primitive 2 is the one most often got wrong by writing what the player can do.** Every strong design in the set is defined by a restriction: never acting, seeing only three inputs, being the subject rather than the questioner. **The constraint is the design.**

**Primitive 5 is what separates a game from a demo.** A demo ends; a game produces something. In four of the five recent designs the output is a measurement about the player rather than a score, and the corpus's own phrasing is that the product is the gap rather than the guess.

### The four that make it work

| # | Primitive | What it is | Worked example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | **The content set** | The items, questions or scenarios, authored and fixed | *Forty items with their bins, their explanations and their levels* |
| 7 | **The weather** | Exogenous events that carry findings without explaining them | *Every event encodes a finding, and none of them is explained in text* |
| 8 | **The engine contract** | A state block, a resolution table and a turn protocol | *Or the engine improvises and the game has no integrity* |
| 9 | **The seed** | Determinism, so a run can be reproduced | *An unreproducible game cannot be balanced* |

**Primitive 6 carries a rule discovered on 5 September**: when the game is a prompt handed to a model, the content set must travel **inside** the prompt, or comparing models measures who invented better examples rather than who built a better game.

**Primitive 8 is the reason two of these designs are playable today.** EXCESS and CAPTIVE each shipped an engine-rules document that can be pasted into a session, which means a design becomes testable before anything is coded. **That artefact should be mandatory rather than optional**, and it is the cheapest thing on this list.

### The three that recur as failures

These are the ones nobody writes down and everybody gets wrong, and each has been caught at least once in this corpus.

| # | Primitive | The failure | Where it was caught |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | **The scoring rule** | The naive rule rewards a lazy strategy, so the score measures nothing | **Twice in one day.** A yes-biased player would have won two of the 5 September games until the datasets and the rule were changed |
| 11 | **The anti-lesson** | The game teaches the opposite of the intended finding | Named explicitly in every strong design: *must not teach that bounding everything is the answer*; *must not teach that the most dangerous subsidiary is the bad one* |
| 12 | **The feedback path** | A player who knows better than the dataset has nowhere to say so | The disagreement button of 5 September, which had no transport until this morning |

**Primitive 10 should be a required section with a required test.** The rule to adopt is the one the corrections produced: **name every lazy strategy and show it losing, with numbers.** Answering yes to everything, answering no to everything, and the single most obvious heuristic. If any of them scores well, the game measures nothing and the content rather than the rule is usually what needs to change.

**Primitive 11 should be a required section too**, because every design that has one arrived at a sharper game, and it is the section that forces an author to say what the game is actually claiming.

### Scoring the nine against this

Applying the twelve to the existing designs finds the gaps immediately, which is the point of extracting them.

- The **four repository concepts** have a claim and a seat and mostly stop there. None has an engine contract, a scoring rule or an anti-lesson. **They are premises rather than designs**, which is the honest label to put on them on the site.
- **EXCESS and CAPTIVE** have all twelve except a feedback path, which did not exist when they were written.
- **Guess The Agent, What Can It Do and Let It Through** have all twelve, with the last two having acquired their scoring rules by correction rather than by design.
- **No design in the set has a stated seed discipline for a coded implementation**, only the rule that one is required.

## Why Games, And The Rule That Governs Every One Of Them

The memo's reasons are good and the corpus adds a third and a hard constraint.

The memo says games explain a concept and guide a user into an experience. The corpus's own levels brief of 3 September supplies the mechanism: **we built an endgame solution and every audience is on level one**, and concepts have to be introduced in order of attrition. **A game is the cheapest way to move somebody from level one**, because it does not require them to accept a premise before they can begin.

And the June brief adds the discipline that keeps this from becoming decoration: **the game framing serves a faithful model of reality**, and the point is to represent that reality so the risks can be applied on top of it. **Not entertainment**, in its own words.

**Now the constraint, and it should govern the whole programme.** The brief of 20 August establishes, from the published meta-analysis it cites, that a strong threat message combined with a low-efficacy message produces the greatest defensive response, while a strong threat combined with high efficacy produces the greatest behaviour change. Its conclusion is stated plainly: **a frightening page with no credible answer performs worse than a page that says nothing.**

**Applied to games this is severe, because games are unusually good at threat and unusually bad at efficacy.** A game that dramatises an agent doing something terrifying and then ends has produced denial, not learning. **So every game on this site owes the player something that works**, and the acceptance test is the one the same brief names: **the measure is visitors who take a named action afterwards**, and explicitly not whether the game was startling.

Two more inherited rules belong on the authoring page. **An analogy must survive an honest holder**, because any framing that only lands when the listener imagines a bad actor teaches the wrong controls. And **humour lowers the defensiveness that risk conversations raise**, which the July brief records and which is the one stylistic recommendation in the corpus.

## The Site's Job Is Composition, Which Is Where This Family Fails

**The most useful thing to give the site agent is not a structure but a defect to avoid**, because the memo describes a hub, and this family already has a recorded hub problem.

The site-access report of 20 August found that the composition gap **has not been solved but relocated**, because three sites now hold three thirds of one answer while no page joins them, and **every cross-link between them points at a domain rather than at the page that answers the question**. Its verdict is quotable and should be the games site's first acceptance test: **a domain link is a referral, not a composition.**

**So the hub's rule is: link page to page, never domain to domain, and give every game one sentence saying which third it holds.** The three recent games were designed to measure the same thing by different routes, so this is not a stylistic preference; a hub that lists them without saying that would lose the only interesting thing about the set.

Two more inheritances from the same family.

**The path convention should be stated rather than left to be discovered.** Every page on the parent site is available as markdown at the same path with the extension swapped, and links inside the markdown point at markdown, so a traversing agent never parses HTML. The report found this working and undocumented, and recommended saying so. **A games site whose whole premise is that agents will build the next games should state it on the first page.**

**And there is a placement oddity worth resolving in passing.** The first game currently sits on the registry site, which the same report found shipped and empty, being a design specification rather than an operating registry with no entries. **Moving the game to its own home fixes two things at once.**

## The Archive Was Specified On 14 August

The memo wants to capture the experiments and vaults already done, and calls the site the perfect place. **The design for that exists and was never built**, which makes it the fourteenth instance of proposing settled work.

Its central decision is the one that saves this from stalling: **a design that requires a written page per vault will stall at the third entry.** So it is a submission queue rather than a catalogue, built on an asymmetry.

```
   WHAT A PERSON SUPPLIES            WHAT THE AGENT DERIVES
   the read key                      file listing and structure
   one line: what it is for          object and commit counts, size
   shape                             last updated
   evidence status                   a rendered preview
   copy or reference                 whether an app is present
   write-key status                  whether it renders in a browser
```

**Getting a game into the archive should take under a minute**, and four fields cannot be derived and must be captured: its shape rather than its subject, whether it is production or demonstration or sketch, whether the entry is a copy or a live reference, and the write-key status, because a frozen entry should say so publicly since a reader deserves to know it will never be corrected.

**Two rules carry over unchanged.** Read keys yes, write keys never, because a central list of write keys is a single artefact whose compromise is unbounded. And escrow the write key **before** publishing rather than after, since a vault whose write key is lost is readable forever and never correctable.

**And topic pages are views rather than documents**: adding a game to the archive with a tag is what makes it appear on a page, so nothing is written twice and nothing drifts. For this site that means the series page, the by-concept page and the by-mechanic page are all one dataset.

## The Reference Implementation Pattern Already Exists

The memo asks the site to carry reference implementations for agents that will build later games. **The format question is answered; four established forms exist**, and the strongest statement of why is the project lead's own, that a page should serve as documentation, as a live demo, and as guidance for agents implementing similar things, with the observation that **the third is the one most sites omit** and the one that removes the recurring cost of explaining an existing vault to an agent so it can extract a pattern.

The four forms, in the order they suit this site:

**The pasteable build prompt.** A complete game specified as a prompt with its dataset inside and an acceptance checklist attached, so several models can be scored identically. **One already exists**, written on 5 September, and it is the single best proof of the memo's own claim that we are a prompt away from building these.

**The engine-rules document.** Playable in a chat session before anything is coded. Two exist.

**The annotated source recipe.** Production code with the bugs marked, aimed explicitly at developers and agents who want to replicate without reinventing.

**The dev pack.** The house format for handing a build to an agent, with a copy-and-paste session bootstrap naming the exact files to read in order.

**The rule to inherit with them is the honesty one**: a pack is more credible for naming what is genuinely absent than for presenting absences as roadmap items.

## One Naming Collision, And It Is On The Word This Site Wants Most

**Level is already carrying two meanings and is about to take a third.** In this corpus, altitude describes a document's depth for a stakeholder, level describes where an audience sits on the attrition ladder, and a parked item from 3 September says one of those two frames should be retired. **A games site will use level for a stage of play**, which is the third sense and the most colloquial.

**Settle it before the first page.** The recommendation is to let the games site keep level for a stage of play, since fighting a word that universal is not winnable, and to retire one of the other two as the parked item already asks. The related caution stands: persona already means the compiled view generator, so a game's cast needs a different word.

**And the 20 August design grammar should be inherited rather than rediscovered**: levels and variants are two axes, so the design is a grid rather than a ladder, **everybody starts at level one** because the advanced user holds the biggest gap and the strongest prior that there is nothing to learn, and a variant may change emphasis, ordering, vocabulary and format and may never change what is being claimed, which is mechanically checkable because a diff of the facts across variants must be empty.

## How To Test A Game Before Building It

The corpus already specified this and half of it ships. Two agents: one drives a browser and captures screens, the other plays a persona and **receives pixels and nothing else**, with no page text, no source and no knowledge of what the page was trying to achieve.

**Two ways it silently voids itself**, both recorded: the same model must not both generate the thing and react to it, and **patience must be set exogenously**, as a fixed number of screens decided before the run.

**And its honest limit maps exactly onto the grid above**: synthetic readers reliably find defects and cannot report preferences, so **synthetic readers should clear the levels and humans should judge the variants.**

| 2 | **The authoring page: twelve primitives, with the scoring rule and anti-lesson as required sections** | It is what the memo asked for, and it is what lets an agent build the tenth game without a conversation |
| 3 | The composition rule enforced: page to page, one sentence per game saying which third it holds | The family's known defect, and the first acceptance test |
| 4 | The archive as a submission queue, in a vault, with the four captured fields | Already specified, and the design that does not stall |
| 5 | The pasteable build prompt published as the reference implementation | It exists. It needs a home |
| 6 | The engine-rules documents published, playable in a session | Two exist, and they make the site usable before any game is coded |
| 7 | The efficacy rule as a stated authoring constraint, with the named-action measure | The rule that decides whether any of this works |
| 8 | The second site, once one game has outgrown the hub | See below |

**On the memo's two sites.** Only one is named, and the structural answer is that the hub holds every game until one earns its own domain. **The test for earning one is that a game has its own dataset, its own audience and its own update cadence**, which of the current set only the triage game plausibly reaches. Until then a second site is a second thing to maintain and a second place for a link to point at a domain.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a design for any game.** It gives the structure a game needs and scores the existing ones against it.
- **Not a technology choice.** Engines, frameworks and animation approaches are deliberately absent, because the primitives above are engine-independent and every design in the set predates any such choice.
- **Not a site build specification.** It names the structure, the defect to avoid and the artefacts to publish, and leaves the build to the site agent in the pack format the corpus already uses.
- **Not a claim that games are the right medium for everything.** The July brief's own honest tension stands: a visualisation often suffices and is much cheaper.
- **Not a plan to code the backlog.** Publishing nine designs is the proposal; building them is a separate decision the site would make easier.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Publishing nine designs with one built | It is a substantial launch made of finished work, and it advertises a backlog |
| Extracting primitives from our own designs | They are grounded and tested by use, and they encode our habits, so the tenth game may inherit our blind spots rather than escape them |
| A required anti-lesson section | It sharpens every design that has one, and it asks an author to state a claim before they have played anything |
| The efficacy rule | It is the best-evidenced constraint here, and it rules out the most dramatic version of almost every game in the set |
| Games as the medium | They move somebody from level one better than a page, and a visualisation is far cheaper and often enough |
| A hub before a second site | It avoids a second thing to maintain, and it means the first game keeps living somewhere that is not its own |
