# Browser Extensions as a Risk-Card Case Study: The Read-Content Closure

**version** v0.33.42
**date** 4 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Architect, @Dev, Strategy
**type** Arch brief

## What This Is

A case study and a risk-card series on browser extensions, chosen because almost everyone has them and because a benign-looking permission hides a large authorization closure: **accepting that an extension can read the content of the pages you visit, which many extensions genuinely need, quietly means it can read and act on your behalf on every site you are logged into, so if you are in the AWS console it can do what you can do there, and the same for your email, Salesforce, and the rest, and even without capturing secrets, injecting scripts and reading content is enough for serious compromise; the risk compounds the moment an agent, even a local one, is connected to the extension, because prompt injection from the very web content the extension reads turns a safe extension into an actor taking instructions from attacker-controlled pages; and the case study is bidirectional in the graph, you can walk from the extension out to the AWS or email account it reaches, and back from the email account in to the extension that exposes it, so a user asking how their email can be attacked is shown the extensions that put it at risk.** It is the third brief of 4 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 agent-authorization brief, the v0.33.40 scenario-series brief, and the v0.33.35 directed-edges brief). New contributions: **the read-content closure, the agent-connected-extension escalation, and bidirectional exposure queries.**

## Why Browser Extensions

Extensions are the ideal card subject because they are universal and their risk is invisible. The project lead: **"it is worth doing a whole series of cards just on browser extensions, because a lot of people use them, and it connects to attack-surface multiplication when you accept privileges, and to supply-chain compromise of source code and deployments."** Everyone has a few, each one added privilege by privilege, and each is a piece of third-party code that can be compromised upstream and pushed to every user at once. That makes them both relatable and genuinely dangerous.

## Part One: The Risk You Accept Straight Away

The core reveal is a permission that looks harmless. The project lead: **"when you accept that the extension can read content, which looks reasonably benign and many extensions genuinely need, what is not explicit is that you now can read and act on behalf of every website you are on."** The consequences are concrete. The project lead: **"if you happen to be logged into the AWS console, now you can do everything you can do in the AWS console; if you are logged in, it has access to all your emails, and Salesforce."** And it does not depend on stealing credentials. The project lead: **"even if it cannot capture secrets, and a lot of them can, just being able to inject scripts and see the content is enough to do a lot of compromise."** Because the extension runs inside your authenticated sessions, it inherits your access to every site at once, without ever needing a password.

## Part Two: What Comes Next With an Agent

The second slice is what happens once an agent is wired to the extension. The project lead: **"once you add an agent, even a local one, you get prompt injection; even if the extension is safe in itself, once it can act on prompts from websites and read content the user does not control, you have these problems."** This is the lethal trifecta realised in a browser: the extension reads untrusted content, it can act across every logged-in site, and the agent will follow instructions embedded in the very pages it reads. A perfectly honest extension becomes an actor taking its orders from attacker-controlled web content.

## The Read-Content Closure, Rendered

```
  NOMINAL PERMISSION                 REACHABLE CLOSURE (what you actually granted)
  -----------------                  --------------------------------------------
  "read content of pages     -->     act as you on EVERY logged-in site:
   you visit"                          - AWS console      -> whatever you can do there
                                        - email            -> read/send, and password resets
                                        - Salesforce, etc. -> read/export CRM data
                                     + inject scripts and read content, no secrets needed
  + a connected agent          -->   prompt injection from page content -> it acts on those pages

  DELTA = (every authenticated session) minus (the one benign-looking permission)
```

## Bidirectional Evidence: Extension to Account, and Back

The case study is a clean demonstration of the graph's directed inward and outward paths. Outward, the extension reaches its targets. The project lead: **"we go from the extension all the way to the AWS or email account."** Inward, the same edges run the other way, and that is what makes it useful to a worried user. The project lead: **"it is cool to do the reverse, so from the email account we arrive back at the Chrome extension, so if somebody asks how can my emails be attacked, or what exposure do I have, you can say here it is, do you have any of these."** A user does not have to understand extensions to benefit; they ask about the asset they care about, their email, and the graph walks inward to every extension that puts it at risk. As the body of evidence grows from many angles, the same node can be reached and explained from any starting point.

## Building the Card Series

Each card follows the scenario pattern the cards already use: you accepted this benign-looking thing, here is what you actually granted, and how long will you accept it. The read-content permission is the first card; the agent-connected extension is the next; the supply-chain compromise of a trusted extension is a third. A little humour and the sheer relatability, everyone has these, carry the point, and each card is backed by the real closure so it survives scrutiny rather than reading as scaremongering.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not anti-extension.** Many extensions genuinely need to read content; the point is the unstated closure.
- **Not dependent on secret theft.** Script injection and content reading are enough.
- **Not a single card.** It is a series, built from real closures, from multiple angles.
- **Not one-directional.** The value is walking the graph inward from the asset, not only outward.

## Honest Notes

- **The permission is legitimate.** The reveal is the gap between the grant and its reach, not malice.
- **Bidirectional is the unlock.** Asset-first queries are what make this land for a non-expert.
- **Supply chain multiplies it.** A compromised update reaches every user of a trusted extension at once.
- **Evidence must be real.** Each card grounds in the actual closure, not a worst-case story.
