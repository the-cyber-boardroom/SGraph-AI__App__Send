# In Scope Is A Red Herring: Rate Every Agent Against The Act Whether Or Not It Applies, And Let The Buyer Ask

**version** v0.33.54
**date** 31 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Commercial, Board

**type** Strategy brief

*Tenth of 31 July, and the business case its companion supplies the questions for. Legal and regulatory points are factual and are not legal advice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A position that separates two questions the market treats as one, and a route to market that follows from the separation: **whether you are in scope of the instrument determines whether you can be fined, and whether you meet its principles determines whether your system is any good, and conflating them means an organisation outside the scope concludes it has nothing to learn, which is the wrong conclusion because the Act contains a genuinely good set of principles, primitives and checks that any system running an agent would benefit from meeting; the scope question is a red herring for the purposes of assessment and matters enormously for the purposes of liability, and keeping both truths in view at once is the whole of the argument; the position has a sharp risk consequence, because an organisation that is out of scope and not compliant is carrying a latent exposure rather than none, since a change to its own use case or an amendment to the instrument can move it into scope with no compliance behind it, which is not hypothetical given that the instrument was amended four days ago and the amendment added new prohibited practices taking effect in December; the market dynamic the memo describes is real, with sectors and firms working to stay outside the high-risk classification, and the honest reading is that the regulator is calibrating a first attempt rather than that the participants are acting in bad faith, but the consequence for a buyer is that classification is a poor proxy for quality; the proposal is therefore a rating that applies to every system whether or not the instrument binds it, with in-scope status as one attribute of the rating rather than its precondition, so that being in scope and non-compliant is simply the worst cell rather than the only one that exists; and the route to market is the strongest idea in the memo, because this does not require regulatory adoption or vendor consent, it requires buyers to add one line to the due diligence they already perform, alongside the certification questions they already ask, and buyers have the leverage to make that stick.** It is the tenth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the companion question-set research brief, the v0.33.54 standards-as-a-service brief, the v0.33.53 accepted-is-not-acceptable brief, the v0.33.51 OSMM brief, and the v0.33.52 outbound maturity model). New contributions: **the decoupling of in-scope from in-breach stated as a product principle, out-of-scope non-compliance named as a latent exposure that a use-case change or an amendment converts into a live one, in-scope status recast as an attribute of a rating rather than its gate, buyer-driven due diligence as a route to market requiring neither regulatory nor vendor consent, and the boundary that a maturity rating must never imply presumption of conformity.**

## Two Questions, Not One

The distinction the memo draws is simple to state and consequential once stated. The project lead: **"I want to decouple the idea that you are in scope for the act or not in scope, because I think that's a red herring."**

**Am I in scope?** A legal question. It determines whether an authority can fine you, and the companion brief sets out what that costs.

**Do I meet the standard?** An engineering and governance question. It determines whether a human can oversee the system, whether anyone would know if it drifted, whether the logs would survive an investigation.

An organisation outside the scope has a complete answer to the first and no answer at all to the second, and today it usually concludes that the second does not apply. That conclusion is what this brief argues against, and the reason is the quality of the instrument itself. The project lead: **"what I like about it is it actually has a really good set of principles, and it has a really good set of primitives and a really good set of checks that I would argue every single AI system should map itself to."**

That assessment is corroborated by an independent observation the corpus made on 28 July, when the human oversight provision turned out to be the closest thing in any published instrument to the plug profile the corpus had been developing on its own. The Act is worth reading as engineering guidance whether or not it binds you.

## Out Of Scope And Non-Compliant Is A Latent Risk

This is the argument that turns a philosophical point into a rateable one, and it is the memo's sharpest move. The project lead: **"the idea that you're not compliant with the act but you're not in scope means that if you change anything in your use case, or if the EU amends the act to now put you in scope, then you have a massive business risk, because you're not compliant to start with."**

Two triggers, both outside the organisation's normal risk radar.

**Your own change.** Scope depends on use case and deployment context, so a product decision taken for commercial reasons can alter regulatory status without anyone treating it as a regulatory decision. The corpus already identified this on 28 July, when the amendment's grandfathering provisions meant a substantial modification resets the clock, so an ordinary engineering choice becomes the thing that moves you into scope.

**Their change.** This is no longer hypothetical. The instrument was amended on 27 July, four days ago. The amendment deferred the high-risk obligations by sixteen months and **added new prohibited practices taking effect on 2 December 2026**. So the boundary moved in both directions within one instrument, and an organisation that decided it was out of scope in June may be wrong today without having done anything at all.

That produces a clean risk statement in the corpus's own terms: **being out of scope and non-compliant is an accepted risk with an unowned trigger.** The exposure is real, the probability is governed by decisions taken elsewhere, and nobody has signed for it. Which is exactly the shape the register exists to surface.

## The Whack-A-Mole Is Real And The Conclusion Is Not Cynicism

The memo describes the market dynamic candidly. The project lead: **"there's a lot of companies, a lot of industries playing a bit of the whack-a-mole, trying to make sure that they're not in scope for the high risk."** And it declines the cynical reading. The project lead: **"I view that as the EU trying to at least set a bar, because if they're not careful they'll make every single system out there in scope for high risk, and then that would probably be too much too soon."**

That balance is right and worth preserving in anything published. Boundary-drawing under lobbying pressure is how every regulation gets made, and the sixteen-month deferral is evidence of exactly the calibration the memo describes rather than of capture.

The commercial consequence, though, is unavoidable: **if classification is contested, negotiated and moving, it is a poor proxy for whether a system is well built.** A buyer who asks only whether a supplier is in scope has learned something about the supplier's lawyers rather than about the supplier's engineering.

## The Rating Applies To Everyone

The proposal follows. The project lead: **"every single service that I use, and companies need to do this internally and externally, they need to have a rating on where they are on the EU AI Act, whether or not they're in scope."**

The design consequence is that in-scope status becomes an attribute of the rating rather than a gate on it.

```
                        IN SCOPE?
                    no                yes
                +------------------+------------------+
   meets the    | good practice,   | compliant and    |
   principles   | no obligation    | can prove it     |
                +------------------+------------------+
   does not     | LATENT: a use    | live exposure to |
                | case change or   | fines and market |
                | an amendment     | withdrawal       |
                | makes this live  |                  |
                +------------------+------------------+
```

Every cell is occupiable and each means something different. The bottom-right is the emergency and is well understood. The bottom-left is the one nobody currently measures, and it is where most of the market sits.

That structure also mirrors the accepted-versus-acceptable model from earlier this week, where two properties usually collapsed into one turn out to be orthogonal and the interesting cases are the off-diagonal ones. It is the same analytical move applied to regulatory status.

## Should We Build A Maturity Model On The Act?

The memo asks directly. The project lead: **"an interesting question here is: should we build a maturity model on top of the AI Act?"**

The answer from the research is yes, with a boundary that must be explicit.

**Yes, because the gap is real and time-limited.** The official conformity route exists and is not ready: harmonised standards have been under development since 2023, the quality management standard is closest to citation, and as of mid-2026 none grants presumption of conformity because none has been cited in the Official Journal. Meanwhile the obligations they support have moved out to December 2027. Buyers need something to ask now.

**Yes, because a maturity model decouples from scope naturally.** A legal status is binary and jurisdictional. A maturity level is neither, so it can describe an out-of-scope system without implying anything false about its legal position. The instrument's own structure supports this, since the corpus established on 28 July that some provisions are satisfied by degree rather than by state, and that an organisation has a shading of compliance per provision, per business unit and per product rather than one level.

**And it fits the corpus's existing pattern**, which already has three ordered, conjunctive, computed-not-claimed maturity models. A fourth on the Act's principles would be the same instrument pointed at an external standard rather than an internal property.

**The boundary is legally sharp and non-negotiable.** Presumption of conformity is reserved to harmonised standards cited in the Official Journal. A private maturity rating confers no legal status, is not a certification, and must say so prominently rather than in a footnote. That is the difference between a defensible product and a misrepresentation, and it is also the honest position, because a rating that claimed legal effect would be claiming something the Union has explicitly reserved to itself.

## The Route To Market Is The Buyer

This is the strongest idea in the memo and it deserves to be the headline of any external version. The project lead: **"this can be implemented client side, because all you need is for the companies who are doing due diligence to basically, in the same way that they go, can I have the due diligence, are you SOC compliant, all you have to do is put an extra requirement in there to say what's your level of compliance using this particular metric of the EU AI Act, and because they're the buyers, they control the power."**

Why this is a genuinely good distribution strategy, rather than merely a nice thought:

**It requires no permission.** Not from the regulator, who is years from citing a harmonised standard. Not from suppliers, who would have to be persuaded. It requires one buyer to add one line to a questionnaire they already send.

**It uses machinery that already exists.** Security questionnaires, vendor assessments and due diligence packs are established, staffed and expected. This is a new row in a document everybody already fills in, not a new process anybody has to adopt.

**The asymmetry favours adoption.** A buyer asking costs nothing. A supplier not answering looks worse than a supplier answering badly. That is how certification frameworks reached ubiquity, and it works without any supplier ever choosing to participate.

**It generates the data.** Every questionnaire answered is a rated system, which builds precisely the corpus of assessments that makes the rating meaningful. The distribution mechanism and the data acquisition mechanism are the same act.

**And it inverts the sales motion.** The corpus has spent weeks on how to persuade an organisation to assess itself. This does not require that. It requires persuading their customers to ask, and the supplier then arrives already motivated.

It also connects directly to the commercial model set out yesterday in the standards-as-a-service brief, where firms fund the mapping of standards they need to understand. A buyer-driven rating creates demand for exactly that mapping, from the side of the market that has the leverage to make it happen.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim that scope is unimportant.** It determines liability entirely; it is a poor proxy for quality.
- **Not a certification.** No presumption of conformity, no legal status, stated prominently.
- **Not a criticism of the regulator.** Boundary calibration under pressure is how regulation is made.
- **Not an argument that everyone should be in scope.** The argument is that everyone benefits from the checks.
- **Not dependent on regulatory or vendor adoption.** It needs buyers to add one question.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Rating out-of-scope systems versus implying legal status | The most serious risk in the whole proposal, since a rating against a law reads as a compliance verdict however it is labelled, and the labelling has to carry real weight |
| The buyer-driven route versus who pays | Buyers have the leverage and suppliers have the budget, and a mechanism where the party who benefits is not the party who pays needs its commercial path stated |
| Gamification versus gaming | Any rating buyers ask for becomes a target, and a self-reported level with nothing behind it will be inflated within a quarter |
| Voluntary adoption of deferred obligations versus the deferral itself | Recommending Tier 3 as good practice invites the answer that the regulator judged it not yet necessary, which is a fair point that needs a substantive reply |
| A fourth maturity model versus coherence | The corpus now has three and is proposing a fourth, and at some point the models need a shared frame rather than accumulating |
| The time-limited gap versus building on it | The conformity route will eventually close this gap, and the durable asset has to be the graph and the assessment corpus rather than the absence of an alternative |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What are the levels? | The ordered, conjunctive predicates of an Act-based maturity model, in the pattern of the existing three |
| How is a self-reported rating made credible? | Evidence requirements, or a verification step, or acceptance that the first version is self-reported and improved later |
| Who is the first buyer? | The distribution strategy needs one organisation willing to add the line, and that is a specific, findable person |
| What does the questionnaire line actually say? | The exact wording a buyer would paste, which is the whole product surface in this motion |
| How is the no-legal-status boundary expressed? | Prominently enough to be honest, without undermining the value of the rating |
| Does the shading structure become the rating? | The per-provision, per-unit, per-product model from 28 July is a candidate representation |
| What happens when harmonised standards are cited? | Whether the rating maps onto them, is superseded by them, or continues alongside |
