# The Registrar We Should Not Build: Evidence Produced Once, Act Coverage As A Test Of Whether You Are Really Doing AI, And Why A Participant Cannot Own The Register

**version** v0.33.54
**date** 31 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Commercial, Partners, Board

**type** Strategy brief

*Eleventh of 31 July. A business opportunity identified and deliberately declined, offered publicly so that somebody else builds it. Regulatory and enforcement claims are grounded and cited. Legal points are factual and are not legal advice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A market gap that follows from using the instrument as a measure of agent deployments, and an explicit decision not to fill it: **the underlying activity is mapping risks, with the Act supplying the evidence, the foundation and a vocabulary that makes the conversation pragmatic rather than abstract, and once assessments of that kind are being produced at any volume the market needs somewhere to put them, which is a registrar of evidence rather than a marketplace, something every government and every organisation would benefit from having, and whose economic point is that evidence should be produced once and consumed many times instead of every buyer independently re-establishing the same facts about the same supplier; part of it already exists in statute, since the Act obliges the Commission to run a public, navigable, machine-readable database of high-risk systems, including systems whose providers claim the derogation and must publicly document their reasoning for doing so, which means the escape route from high-risk classification is itself a matter of public record, but that database is scope-gated by construction and therefore covers only the population the previous brief argued is the wrong population to limit yourself to; the sharpest idea in the memo is an inversion, that coverage of the Act is a test of whether an organisation is really doing AI at all, because the instrument's obligations attach to autonomous action, data handling, oversight and permissions, so a company claiming to be an AI business against which almost none of the Act bites has either not assessed itself or is not doing what it says; the memo's premise that there is currently no downside to overstating AI needs updating, because enforcement has moved from signalling to action with more than a dozen cases in two years, and crucially the standard being applied is substantiation rather than mere falsity, which means the market for documented evidence is being created by regulators as this is written; and the reason to publish rather than build is that a register owned by a participant is not a register, since the corpus is producing exactly the kind of assessment such a body would hold and cannot credibly also be its custodian, so the right position is contributor rather than operator.** It is the eleventh document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.54 in-scope-versus-in-breach brief, the v0.33.54 question-set research brief, the v0.33.54 standards-as-a-service brief, the v0.33.51 OSMM brief, and the v0.33.48 governance-layer brief). New contributions: **the registrar of evidence as a distinct market gap, the produce-once-consume-many economics, the identification that the statutory database is scope-gated and that derogation reasoning is public, Act coverage proposed as an AI-washing detector, the correction that enforcement now applies a substantiation standard, and the argument that a participant cannot own the register.**

## The Frame: We Map Risks, The Act Supplies The Evidence

Worth restating because it governs everything after. The project lead: **"ultimately, what we're doing is mapping risks; in a way, the act just provides the evidence and the foundation, and the ability to have that conversation in a pragmatic way."**

That is the corpus position rather than a new one, and it is the reason the regulation work has never been a compliance product. The instrument is useful because it is an external authority a board already respects, which turns an assertion into a citation. What is being built on top of it is a risk register, and what a registrar would hold is the evidence underneath one.

## Evidence Is Produced Once And Paid For Many Times

The economic argument is the strongest part of the memo. The project lead: **"there's a case for sharing this data, so that the cost of reviewing and the cost of providing evidence is only done once."**

The current state is straightforwardly wasteful. A supplier answers substantially the same due diligence questions for every customer. Every customer independently evaluates the same evidence about the same system. Nobody's assessment is reusable, comparable, or visible to anybody else, and the total cost across the market is the number of suppliers multiplied by the number of buyers rather than the number of suppliers.

```

   supplier -> buyer 1            supplier -> registrar

   cost: suppliers x buyers       cost: suppliers, plus verification
```

And the scope the memo proposes is deliberately wide. The project lead: **"every government should have one of these, and every organisation should have one of these."** That is the fractal pattern the corpus applies to registers generally: a register per accepting entity, connected rather than centralised, which suggests the right architecture is federated rather than a single global body.

## Part Of It Already Exists, And Its Limit Is The Point

The Act itself mandates a registry, which materially changes what a builder would be starting from.

Article 71 obliges the Commission, with the Member States, to set up and maintain an EU database of high-risk systems. Providers or their authorised representatives enter the information specified in Annex VIII, public-authority deployers enter their own section, and the registered information must be publicly accessible in a user-friendly, easily navigable and **machine-readable** form. Substantial modification triggers an update obligation, so a registration frozen at the point of first entry becomes a non-conformity. Certain areas are excluded from public view, with law enforcement, migration and border management held in a secure non-public section and critical infrastructure registered nationally.

Two consequences follow, and they point in opposite directions.

**It is a genuine asset for any builder.** A public, machine-readable registry of high-risk systems, maintained by the Commission, is a free authoritative data source. Nobody building a registrar of evidence should be re-collecting what the Union already publishes.

**It is scope-gated by construction, which is exactly the wrong boundary.** It covers high-risk systems and nothing else, so the population it describes is the one the previous brief argued is the least interesting to limit yourself to. Everything out of scope, which is most of the market, appears nowhere.

There is a third detail that deserves attention on its own, because it is quietly remarkable. Systems claiming the derogation under Article 6(3), meaning providers asserting that an Annex III system is **not** high-risk, must still register and publicly document the reasoning behind that assessment. So the escape route from high-risk classification is itself a matter of public record. The whack-a-mole described yesterday leaves footprints, and a registrar of evidence could make those footprints comparable across an industry, which no one currently does.

## Act Coverage As A Test Of Whether You Are Really Doing AI

This is the most original idea in the memo and it deserves to be the headline of anything published from it. The project lead: **"a good way to define if a company is actually using AI effectively, or even in their flows, is how much of the AI Act is relevant to them."** With the mechanism. The project lead: **"in a weird way, the Act also captures all of the extra actions and all the extra capabilities of the agents."**

The logic holds because the instrument's obligations attach to the things that make AI consequential rather than to the label. Autonomous action, data governance, human oversight, logging, transparency about machine-generated content, permissions and blast radius. A system doing none of those things triggers almost nothing in the Act.

So coverage becomes a diagnostic with three readings, and the third is the interesting one:

| Coverage | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| Substantial, with evidence | You are doing meaningful AI and you know what you are carrying |
| Substantial, without evidence | You are doing meaningful AI and have not assessed it |
| Minimal, despite the claim | Either you are not doing what your marketing says, or you have not looked |

That third row is the AI-washing detector, and it is unusually elegant because it requires no judgement about what counts as real AI. It just asks which obligations attach, and lets the instrument answer.

## The Downside The Memo Says Does Not Exist Is Arriving

One correction, and it strengthens the case rather than weakening it. The project lead: **"at the moment there is no side effect; there's only positive advantage for companies to slap AI in their offering, even when most of them are not really doing AI."**

That was true and is becoming less true. Enforcement has moved from guidance to action. The United States competition regulator has brought on the order of a dozen or more AI-washing cases since 2024, with more than fifty investigations opened between 2022 and 2025, including a May 2026 action against three marketing companies carrying a collective monetary total of around nine hundred and thirty thousand dollars, and a case resolved in January 2026. The securities regulator penalised two investment advisers in March 2024 for claiming to use AI without the underlying systems, totalling four hundred thousand dollars. Criminal fraud statutes have been applied to AI-related misrepresentation.

Two features of that pattern matter more than the amounts.

**The standard is substantiation, not merely falsity.** Several recent actions allege that claims lacked sufficient evidentiary support rather than that they were outright false. That is a documentation standard, and it means companies making AI claims increasingly need evidence they can produce on demand.

**Most recent cases are business-to-business.** Seven of the last eight reported actions concern claims made to other businesses rather than to consumers, which is precisely the due-diligence context the previous brief identified as the route to market.

Put together, **the regulator is manufacturing demand for exactly what a registrar of evidence would hold.** A company that can point to a dated, independent, structured assessment is in a materially better position than one relying on its own marketing copy. The enforcement environment does not undermine the memo's business case; it supplies the urgency the memo assumed was missing.

An honest caveat: enforcement posture is politically contested, with at least one prior order reopened and set aside in December 2025 with reference to a change in federal AI policy. The direction of travel is real and its persistence is not guaranteed.

## Who Should Be In It

The memo identifies the natural participants. The project lead: **"the companies that today provide metrics and provide scorecards and provide ratings and provide analysis on agents and solutions should basically be mapped here, which I think is going to be quite nice business."**

That is right, and it defines the shape of the thing. A registrar is not a competitor to the rating providers; it is the layer that makes their outputs comparable and reusable. Each contributes assessments in a common structure, buyers read across them, and the value of every individual assessment rises because it can be compared. That is the standard economics of an interoperability layer, and it is why no single rating provider can create it alone.

## Why It Makes The Market More Efficient, Carefully Stated

The memo reaches for a framing and then corrects itself, and the correction is worth keeping. The project lead: **"we're allowing the good companies and the companies not, sorry, this is not good versus bad."** And the accurate version. The project lead: **"we're allowing the companies who are investing in security, who are taking the time to really develop the product, and not just rushing it through the last phases."**

That distinction matters for how this is published. The claim is not that some firms are virtuous and others are not. It is that investment in the unglamorous parts, security, oversight, permissions, documentation, is currently invisible at the point of purchase, so a buyer cannot tell the difference and a supplier gets no return on having done it. Making it visible is a market-efficiency argument rather than a moral one, and it is considerably more persuasive.

The memo also names where the information asymmetry is worst. The project lead: **"especially from a permissions point of view, what's actually going on."** Which is the corpus's own central claim arriving from the buyer's side: what an agent can actually reach is the thing nobody can see and everybody needs to know.

## Why We Should Not Build It

The memo is unambiguous. The project lead: **"I hope somebody does it, because we will need to; we can contribute data to it, but this is not something that we should be building."**

The instinct is right and the reason is worth stating precisely, because it is the same principle the corpus applied to itself a week ago.

**A register owned by a participant is not a register.** The corpus is producing assessments of exactly the kind such a body would hold. An organisation that both produces the assessments and operates the registry that arbitrates them has a conflict no governance structure fully resolves, and every competing rating provider would be right to decline to participate. The OSMM work made the same argument in the other direction, that a maturity model published by a vendor that has not run it against itself is marketing rather than measurement.

**It is infrastructure, not product.** Registries win by neutrality, coverage and permanence, which are institutional properties rather than engineering ones. The natural operators are standards bodies, industry consortia, regulators or a neutral non-profit.

**And declining it is consistent.** The corpus has already refused to be the enforcement point, refused to be in the request path, and positioned itself as the layer above things it consumes. Refusing to be the registry is the same discipline applied to a tempting adjacency, and stating it publicly is how the position becomes credible.

## What We Would Contribute

If somebody builds it, the corpus is well placed to be an early and useful supplier rather than a bystander.

Structured assessments produced by the question set, in a form designed to be machine-readable and provenance-backed. The regulation graph itself, so that an assessment can cite a provision that resolves to a published paragraph, a file and a commit. The distinction between what is claimed, what is confirmed and what is validated, established this week, which is exactly the metadata a registry needs to avoid becoming a repository of unverified self-reports. And the maturity framing, which lets an assessment describe an out-of-scope system without implying a legal status it does not have.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a product we are building.** The opportunity is published so that somebody else takes it.
- **Not a marketplace.** A registrar of evidence, not a place where anything is bought and sold.
- **Not a replacement for the statutory database.** That exists, is machine-readable, and should be consumed rather than duplicated.
- **Not a moral ranking.** The argument is that invisible investment in security and oversight should become visible, which is efficiency rather than virtue.
- **Not a claim that AI-washing goes unpunished.** Enforcement is active, and the standard being applied is substantiation.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Publishing an opportunity versus keeping it | An open call is consistent with the corpus's posture and hands a well-researched idea to anyone, including a competitor better placed to execute it |
| Not building it versus needing it to exist | The strategy depends on somebody else doing something they have not committed to, and there is no fallback if nobody does |
| Contributor versus the same conflict at a smaller scale | Declining to operate the registry removes the sharpest conflict and not the softer one, since an early and prolific contributor shapes the schema in its own image |
| Coverage as an AI-washing test versus gaming | Once coverage signals seriousness, the incentive inverts and firms will claim more of the Act applies to them than actually does |
| Shared evidence versus what suppliers will disclose | The economics work only if suppliers publish, and the things buyers most want to know are the things suppliers least want public |
| A federated register versus a usable one | Every organisation having one is the right architecture and is much harder to make comparable than a single central body |
| Enforcement as the demand driver | It is real today and politically contested, and a business case resting on it inherits that volatility |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| Who is the natural operator? | A standards body, an industry consortium, a regulator, or a neutral non-profit, and which is approachable |
| What is the minimum schema? | The smallest structure that makes assessments from different providers comparable |
| How does an assessment carry its provenance? | Who made it, when, against which version of the instrument, and with what evidence |
| Does the statutory database offer an interface? | Machine-readable is stated; how it is actually consumed is a practical question for any builder |
| How is a self-report distinguished from a verified assessment? | The confirmed, validated and accepted distinction is a candidate, and without it the register fills with marketing |
| What would we publish first? | A worked assessment in the proposed structure would demonstrate the format better than a specification |
| Is there an existing body already close to this? | Worth checking before proposing something new, since an adjacent organisation may only need extending |
