# Talk To The Act: Grounded In Paragraphs Rather Than Chunks, The Question Only We Can Answer, And The Second Product On The Same Kit

**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Engineering, Product, Commercial

*Twelfth of 31 July. Adds a conversational layer to the published Act site. Prior art and platform capabilities are grounded and cited. Legal points are factual and are not legal advice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

## What This Is

A conversational layer over the published instrument, which is simultaneously a feature, a revenue experiment and a test of something the corpus committed to four days ago: **the site is live and the artefacts exist in several forms, the rendered pages, the downloadable documents, the individual provisions, the structured serialisations, and the proposal is to let a user talk to them, through two interfaces with genuinely different scopes, a sidebar present on every page whose context is whatever the reader is looking at, and a fuller interface of the kind already built elsewhere in the vault work, with both reusing components already built rather than starting fresh; the important architectural decision is that the several artefact forms are projections of one source rather than separate sources, so the chat should ground on the structured paragraphs and use the other forms as outputs, because indexing each projection separately would produce retrieval that contradicts itself and a citation that points at a rendering rather than at a provision; the technical bet that the graph improves the answers is not merely ours, since an academic team building the same kind of assistant compared naive chunk retrieval against a graph-structured representation and found the structured version better for the deeper interpretive queries, which is exactly the class of question this instrument attracts; the feature nobody else can offer is the one the memo lands on almost casually, because comparing what a provision said before the amendment against what it says now requires holding both versions in a form where the comparison is computable, which the two-hash model produces as a by-product and which no public source currently has, making the most valuable question askable only here; commercially this is the second product to ship on the metering kit built for the voice note tool, which makes it the live test of whether that kit was real, and it forces the key-provisioning problem that the first product deliberately deferred, since a shared key embedded in a public indexed site is not defensible in the way one handed to a known first cohort was; and there is a pleasing recursion in that a conversational interface published on this site falls under the transparency obligation of the instrument it explains, which begins to apply two days from now.** It is the twelfth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.52 voice-note architecture brief, the v0.33.53 voice-note build brief, the v0.33.54 document-ontology brief, the v0.33.54 no-canonical-Act research brief, and the v0.33.52 first-product commercial brief). New contributions: **the two chat scopes and why the sidebar is structurally cheaper, the rule that projections are outputs rather than retrieval sources, external evidence that graph structure beats chunking for interpretive queries, the version-difference question identified as the unique capability, the recognition that this is the second product on the commercialisation kit and therefore its test, the public-site trigger for key provisioning, and the recursion that the assistant is subject to the instrument.**

## The Milestone

Worth recording because it changes what is possible today. The project lead: **"now that we have the EU Act website working and deployed and it's actually looking pretty good."** And the intent. The project lead: **"it's a great opportunity to do there, and also is going to force us to develop a couple of core components that we need everywhere."**

That second clause is the strategic reason to do this rather than something else. A chat layer over a public artefact exercises components the corpus needs across every product: metered inference, per-user keys, grounded retrieval over a vault, and citation back to source. Building them here, against a public artefact with no confidentiality exposure, is a cheaper place to get them wrong than against a customer's register.

## Two Interfaces, Two Scopes

The memo proposes two, and the difference between them is scope rather than size. The project lead: **"one is a sort of chat session, almost on every page as a sidebar, which is the one where we can basically say talk to whatever we have in front."** And the second. The project lead: **"it would be good to create a sort of interface like the one we already created in the vaults, and using the tools."**

```
   SIDEBAR                          FULL INTERFACE
   scope: this provision            scope: the instrument, or several
   context: already known           context: must be retrieved
   retrieval: none needed           retrieval: required
   prompt: small, bounded           prompt: assembled, variable
   cost: low and predictable        cost: higher and variable
   question: "what does this mean"  question: "where does the Act say"
```

The asymmetry is worth exploiting rather than smoothing over. The sidebar needs **no retrieval at all**, because the reader is already standing on the provision and its graph neighbourhood is known: the definitions it depends on, the provisions it cross-references, the amendment history. That context can be assembled deterministically from the folder rather than searched for, which makes the sidebar cheap, fast, and immune to the failure mode where retrieval fetches the wrong passage.

So the sidebar should ship first. It is the smaller build, the lower cost per interaction, and the higher quality answer, and it is the one that fits the reading behaviour the site already produces.

## Ground On The Source, Use The Projections As Outputs

The memo lists what exists. The project lead: **"we have HTML, we have PDFs, we might have even infographics, we have the individual articles, the JSON-LD, the triplets."**

The important thing about that list is that these are not several sources. They are **projections of one source**, which the document ontology brief established earlier today: the folder tree is the master record and everything else is derived from it.

Indexing them separately would be an expensive mistake. The same provision would appear in several forms with different chunk boundaries, retrieval would return three versions of one passage, and the model would have to reconcile a rendering against a serialisation. Worse, a citation would point at a page or a document rather than at a provision, which throws away the addressability the two-hash model was built to provide.

The rule to adopt: **retrieval grounds on the structured paragraphs; the projections are what the answer offers afterwards.** A reader asks a question, receives an answer citing a provision, and is then offered the rendered article, the document to download, the diagram, or the machine-readable form. Grounding and delivery are different jobs.

## Graph Beats Chunks, And Somebody Has Measured It

The bet that structure improves the answers has external support, which is worth knowing before building.

An academic team published a self-assessment assistant for this exact instrument, using retrieval-augmented generation, and compared a naive chunk-based implementation against a graph-structured one. Their reported finding is that naive retrieval handles straightforward lookups effectively, while the graph-structured representation supports deeper regulatory interpretation and more complex queries.

That is the class of question this instrument attracts. Nobody asks a regulation what its words are; they ask whether an obligation applies to them, what it depends on, what it excludes, and what changed. Those are traversals rather than lookups, and they are what the structure is for.

It also means the corpus's approach is not eccentric. An independent group building the same thing reached the same architectural conclusion, and this build starts from a considerably richer graph than a retrofitted one.

## The Question Only We Can Answer

The memo mentions this last and it should be first. The project lead: **"in this particular case, you could even talk about the difference between one version and another; what changed, what didn't change."**

That is the differentiator, and it is not a chat feature. It is the whole architecture becoming askable.

Today, as this morning's research established, no consolidated version of the instrument exists publicly. The official database still carries the original. The best independent browsable version states that its text predates the amendment. One widely used source carries wording that was never law. A person wanting to know what changed must open two documents and perform the amendment themselves.

The corpus can answer it because of decisions already taken. The positional hash is stable while the content hash moves, so the changed provisions are identifiable by comparison rather than by parsing instructions. The folder model stores repealed and superseded text rather than discarding it. And consolidation is computed as a projection, so both states remain available.

Which means questions nobody else can currently answer become ordinary here:

- What changed in Article 10, and when did it take effect?
- What did this provision say before 27 July?
- Which provisions did the amendment touch at all?
- Does the change affect an obligation I already documented against?

Given the amendment came into force four days ago and the first transparency obligations apply in two, this is not a clever demonstration. It is the most commercially valuable question in the subject area right now, and it should be the launch feature rather than a later addition.

## This Is The Second Product On The Kit

The commercial framing is explicit. The project lead: **"it's about allowing the user to talk to the vault, and also allowing us to start exploring the revenue stream of us basically selling OpenRouter tokens, and connecting the dots and exposing that to the user, and trying to get that commercial flow going."**

Which makes this the second product to ship on the metering arrangement built for the voice note tool: inference and billing both through the router, a per-user key carrying a credit limit, the retained margin expressed as the difference. The commercial brief written for that product set an explicit test, that the real output was a reusable commercialisation kit and the honest measure was whether the second product shipped faster than the first.

**This is that second product, and the test is now live.** Two things follow. The build should reuse rather than reimplement, and any place it cannot is a defect in the kit rather than in this product. And somebody should record how long it takes, because that number is the answer to a question the corpus asked itself four days ago and has no other way to settle.

One difference from the first product is worth noting: the voice note tool charges for a discrete job with a predictable cost, while a conversation has no natural unit and a curious user can spend a lot without noticing. The live cost display designed for the field demo becomes more necessary here rather than less.

## A Public Site Forces The Provisioning Problem

This is the consequence the memo does not mention and it should be settled before launch.

The voice note build deliberately deferred key provisioning by seeding a key into the client for a known first cohort, with the conditions written down: hard spend cap, short life, easy revocation, treated as public from the moment it shipped. That was defensible for a handful of named beta users.

It is not defensible here. This site is public, indexed, and intended to attract strangers, which is exactly the trigger flagged three days ago when asking when the seeded key stops being acceptable. A shared key embedded in a public regulatory reference will be found, and its spend cap will be consumed by somebody other than the intended readers.

So this product forces the provisioning function the first one postponed. The good news is that the platform supports what is needed: keys can be created programmatically with credit limits and reset periods, and guardrails can restrict a key by model, by provider and by data policy, so an issued key can be narrowed to the two or three models this application actually calls.

A workable sequence: a small free allowance per visitor to make the feature discoverable, then a provisioned key on purchase. Whether the free tier is possible without an account is the open question, and it is the difference between a feature people try and a feature people read about.

## The Assistant Is Subject To The Act It Explains

A recursion worth building for rather than merely enjoying. The instrument requires that people be told when they are interacting with an AI system rather than a person, and that obligation begins to apply on 2 August 2026, two days from now, unaffected by the deferral that moved the high-risk obligations.

A conversational interface published on a site explaining the Act therefore falls within the Act. Handled well this is the most credible possible demonstration: the site can disclose plainly, cite the provision requiring it, and link to the paragraph. Handled badly it is the first thing a critic will point at.

It also makes the site its own worked example, which is worth more than any illustration. The tool that helps you assess your obligations visibly meeting one of its own is a better argument than a case study.

## Informing, Not Advising

The boundary the corpus has held all week applies with more force here, because a conversational interface invites exactly the question the product must not answer.

Three rules follow. The assistant answers what the instrument says and what a provision depends on, and declines to conclude whether a particular reader complies. Every substantive answer cites a provision that resolves to a published paragraph, so the reader can check rather than trust. And the artefact's own status is stated, since the corpus publishes a combined version that is derived and explicitly not canonical, which means an answer must never be presented as an authoritative statement of law.

That last point is not a disclaimer to bury. Yesterday's publishing brief made verification the differentiator, and a chat that shows its source and admits its status is the same argument in conversational form.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a legal adviser.** It explains what the instrument says; it does not conclude that anyone complies.
- **Not authoritative.** The underlying text is a derived combined version and says so.
- **Not retrieval over the projections.** Grounding is on the structured paragraphs; the renderings are outputs.
- **Not a new billing system.** It reuses the metering kit built for the first product, which is the point.
- **Not launched with a shared key.** A public site forces provisioning.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Conversation versus a bounded price | A discrete job has a predictable cost and a conversation does not, so a curious user can spend more than they expected, which makes the visible meter necessary rather than optional |
| Free allowance versus abuse | Anything usable without an account on a public site will be automated against, and anything requiring an account will stop most people trying it |
| Citing provisions versus appearing to advise | The more precisely an answer cites, the more authoritative it sounds, which works against the informing-not-advising boundary rather than with it |
| Sidebar first versus what people expect | Page-scoped chat is cheaper and better grounded, and users arriving with a general question will find it narrow and go elsewhere |
| The version diff versus its shelf life | It is the strongest differentiator today and it closes when an official consolidation appears, so the durable asset is the mechanism rather than the gap |
| Second product on the kit versus honest measurement | Whether the kit worked is only answerable if somebody records the effort, and nobody has been asked to |
