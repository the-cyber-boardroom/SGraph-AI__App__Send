# Why We Hold On To What We Made: The Triple Lock On Change, Why Marginal Improvement Is Not Enough, And Why Forcing An Upgrade Destroys The Evidence That It Was Better

## What This Is

The psychology behind resistance to change, and the design principles that follow from taking it seriously: **the memo's hypothesis is that creating something produces a sense of ownership that makes later change feel like loss, and asks for research to test it, and the research confirms it precisely, because the tendency to overvalue what you built yourself is a named and replicated effect in which people rated their own amateur creations as comparable in value to expert work and expected others to agree, and which operates over and above the ordinary tendency to overvalue what you merely own; three layers stack here rather than one, since people already prefer the current state as a default, the current state is additionally theirs, and they have already invested in it, so giving it up registers as a double loss of both the possession and the investment; the boundary conditions are the most useful part, because the effect depends on completion and disappears when a creation was never finished or was subsequently taken apart, which suggests that preserved history and reversibility change the emotional arithmetic rather than merely the practical one; on the design side the memo distinguishes three outcomes of a change, marginally better, dramatically better, and worse, and argues that because change carries real friction the bar must be significant improvement rather than parity, which is the same standard an entrant needs against an incumbent; reversibility is what makes experimentation safe, and running several options at once is what makes it measurable; and the sharpest observation is about forced adoption, because an organisation that compels an upgrade has destroyed the only clean evidence that the new version was better, since the people who moved had no choice, whereas voluntary migration is simultaneously the adoption mechanism and the measurement.** It is the twenty-third document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.54 design-for-non-cooperation brief, the v0.33.49 open-source-as-strategy brief, the v0.33.53 cognitive-load brief, the v0.33.54 project-manager brief, and the v0.33.49 consulting-model brief). New contributions: **the triple lock of status quo, ownership and sunk cost identified as a single stacked mechanism, the completion boundary condition read as a design instruction, forced upgrade identified as the destruction of evidence rather than merely a discourtesy, attrition reframed as a signal of fit, and open-source practice named as a cultural antidote to the ownership effect.**

## The Hypothesis, And It Is Correct

The memo states it as an intuition and asks for evidence. The project lead: **"when you create something and you have a sense of ownership on that, there's something to be said for a natural desire to hold to what was created."** With the specific fear. The project lead: **"if you keep refactoring, you're going to lose what you created."**

That is a named effect with a substantial literature. Research published in 2012 across four studies, in which participants assembled boxes, folded origami and built construction toys, found that people who built something valued it markedly more than identical items they had not built, rated their own amateurish output as comparable in value to expert creations, and expected other people to agree with that assessment. The effect held for experienced hobbyists and complete novices alike, and it was shown to operate over and above the ordinary tendency to overvalue what one merely possesses. The authors traced it to earlier work on effort justification, which found that the more effort someone invests in something, the more they value it.

So the intuition is not a hunch about human nature. It is one of the better-replicated findings in the field, and the memo's framing of it as unverbalised is consistent with how it operates.

## Three Locks, Not One

What makes organisational change hard is that these effects stack rather than compete, and separating them is useful because each has a different countermeasure.

```
   LAYER 1   status quo bias        the current state is the DEFAULT
   LAYER 2   ownership effect       the current state is MINE
   LAYER 3   sunk cost              and I ALREADY PAID for it
                                    -> giving it up is a double loss:
                                       the possession and the investment
```

The first is general inertia. The second adds that the thing belongs to somebody, and the ownership research suggests valuations can diverge substantially between what somebody would pay to acquire a thing and what they would accept to give it up. The third, which the memo also identifies, is the one the corpus has already met twice today, in project lock-in and in defensive justification of prior positions. The project lead states it exactly. The project lead: **"you don't want to change because you're already committed, you already invested, but you're going in the wrong direction, and instead of turning around or adjusting navigation, you keep going."**

Understood as three layers, the resistance stops looking like obstruction and starts looking like arithmetic, which is a considerably more useful frame for anyone trying to design around it.

## The Boundary Conditions Are The Design Instructions

The most practically valuable part of the research is where the effect stops.

The 2012 work found that **labour leads to attachment only when the labour results in successful completion.** When participants built something and then took it apart, the effect dissipated. When they failed to finish, it did not appear. A later replication identified psychological ownership as the underlying mechanism and confirmed the same dissolution on disassembly.

Two design readings follow, and they are both actionable.

**Completed things are the ones people defend.** So the moment to propose a change is earlier than feels natural, before something is finished enough to have become somebody's. Refactoring a half-built thing is not merely technically cheaper; it is emotionally cheaper.

**Preservation is not destruction.** The attachment is to the artefact continuing to exist as something the creator made. A change that deletes work triggers loss; a change that supersedes work while keeping it in history does not, in the same way. That is an argument for version control as an emotional instrument rather than only a technical one, which sits neatly alongside the argument made on 28 July that the ability to see what changed is what makes a document usable more than once.

## Three Outcomes, And Why Marginal Is Not Enough

The memo works out a taxonomy while speaking, and it lands somewhere useful. There are three outcomes of a change: marginally better, dramatically better, and worse. The project lead describes the marginal case precisely. The project lead: **"I had a way to do this with three clicks, now I have the same way to do it with maybe two and a half clicks, but it's just a small incremental benefit."** Against the transformative one. The project lead: **"you dramatically unlock either a better experience, a better workflow, less costs, you simplify the design."**

And the conclusion that follows from change having a cost. The project lead: **"because change has a cost, because change has an impact, it does have a friction, it's super important that that benefit is not purely marginal or equivalent, it's actually significantly better."**

This is the right standard and it has a second justification the memo supplies itself: it is the same bar an entrant faces against an incumbent. The project lead: **"that's how it should be when you're trying to enter markets, you have to be that much better than the previous one."** Given the triple lock above, the incumbent is not merely established; it is owned, defaulted to, and already paid for. A replacement that is marginally better is competing against three advantages it cannot match on merit.

**The practical rule: if the improvement is marginal, the change is a net loss.** The friction is real, the attachment is real, and the benefit is not large enough to clear either.

## Two-Way Doors, And Several At Once

The memo uses a familiar framing and then extends it usefully. The project lead: **"this doesn't work with one-way doors, using that analogy; it only works with two-way doors, and it works better when you have multiple doors available and you can try multiple doors at the same time, because then you can really test the whole thing."**

The extension is the interesting half. Reversibility makes a single experiment safe. **Parallelism makes it informative**, because running the old and new together produces a comparison rather than a before-and-after separated by everything else that changed. And it directly addresses the fear the memo identifies. The project lead: **"people are worried that it's working already, and you're going to make it worse."** That worry is entirely rational under a one-way door and largely dissolves under a two-way one, which means reversibility is not just an engineering property but the thing that makes the conversation possible at all.

## Forcing The Upgrade Destroys The Evidence

This is the sharpest observation in the memo and deserves to travel beyond it. The project lead: **"this is the mistake that other companies make when they force upgrades, because they aren't actually able to understand if what they did was actually better, since the people moving to the new version didn't have a choice."** With the standard that follows. The project lead: **"you should force the new product, the new versions, to be that good that everybody just upgrades, not because they're forced to, but because it's damn better, and that's measurable."**

The insight is that **voluntary migration is simultaneously the adoption mechanism and the measurement instrument, and compulsion destroys the second while appearing to solve the first.** An organisation that forces an upgrade gets its adoption numbers and learns nothing, because migration under compulsion carries no information about quality. It then has to discover whether the new thing was actually better through complaints, churn, and support load, which are slower, noisier and much more expensive signals.

Voluntary uptake is a clean measure precisely because it is costly to obtain. That is the same logic as several other things in the corpus: a paid engagement is better evidence than an enthusiastic conversation, and a customer asking what it costs to keep is better evidence than a compliment.

## Simplicity, And Knowing When To Stop

The memo's design direction is subtraction, with a well-known formulation refined in a way worth keeping. The project lead: **"good design is done when there's nothing else to take out."** And then the refinement, which is the part that makes it usable. The project lead: **"it's not when there's no stuff that you can remove, it's when there's no stuff you can remove without impacting the user experience, without impacting what you're actually trying to do in the first place."**

That converts an aesthetic principle into a stopping rule, which is the same move made this afternoon on word-level annotation. Remove until removing costs something. The memo also identifies where this is most counter-intuitive. The project lead: **"a lot of times the move from service to commodity is actually a path where you're removing features, because you're removing features that were not relevant to the user and were just adding complexity."** Which is right, and it is the harder half of commoditisation: the transition is usually described as adding scale and is often achieved by subtracting scope.

## Attrition As A Signal Of Fit

An unusual idea, and worth preserving because it inverts something the corpus has otherwise treated as a problem. The project lead: **"the definition of something that is just working is when it's painful to remove and to add stuff; it's painful to make a change, not because you can't make a change, but because you now have a built-in attrition that exists in the workflows."**

Read carefully, this is a claim about fit. When a design is loose, changes are cheap because nothing depends on anything. When it has converged on the problem, every part is load-bearing, so every change costs something. **The resistance is therefore evidence that the thing is doing its job**, provided it remains possible to try changes rather than impossible.

That last qualification is doing the work, and it is the difference between a well-fitted system and an ossified one. Both are painful to change; only one still permits the experiment.

## Open Source As An Antidote

The memo closes on something personal that is also a genuine mechanism. The project lead: **"because I come from an open source world, you don't have ownership on the code, so you don't put that much value in the code itself, you put value in the solution, or in what you're trying to do."**

The ownership effect attaches to the artefact. If professional identity attaches instead to the problem solved, the artefact becomes an instrument rather than a possession, and replacing it stops registering as loss. That is a cultural rather than technical countermeasure, and it is teachable: praise the outcome rather than the implementation, treat deletion as progress, and make the reuse of somebody else's better solution a visible good.

It is also consistent with the position taken on 17 July that openness is self-interest rather than charity. Reduced attachment to any particular implementation is another return on it, and one that brief did not name.

## What This Means For Our Own Product

The user's framing is the right one to end on: the entire model asks a company to change how it operates. Everything above therefore applies to the corpus itself, in two directions.

**Outward.** The existing spreadsheet, register or process is subject to all three locks: it is the default, it belongs to someone, and it cost them years. The replacement must therefore be significantly rather than marginally better, must run alongside rather than replacing on a one-way door, must preserve rather than delete what came before, and must never be mandated, because mandating it forfeits the only clean evidence that it worked. The person whose spreadsheet is being replaced is often its author, which was flagged in the mass-customisation brief today and is now grounded: they are not being obstructive, they are experiencing a documented effect.

**Inward.** The corpus is now a large body of models, frameworks and vocabulary built by the people who would have to refactor it. Every argument here applies to that too, and the completion boundary condition suggests the risk grows as things become finished. The stated willingness to change position, as happened twice today with a superseded draft and a revised publishing plan, is the practical form of the defence.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not an argument that attachment is irrational.** It is documented, general, and applies to everyone including us.
- **Not a case for constant change.** The bar is significant improvement, and marginal change is a net loss.
- **Not a claim that resistance is always bias.** Sometimes the existing thing is simply better, which is why reversibility matters.
- **Not a mandate.** Forced adoption destroys the evidence that the change was worth making.
- **Not only about software.** The memo's claim is that this applies to every kind of creation, and the research supports that.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Significantly better versus who judges | The person best placed to assess the improvement is the person most attached to the incumbent, which is the problem restated rather than solved |
| Voluntary adoption versus never adopting | Refusing to compel is right and some organisations will simply never move, and there is no answer here for the case where the better thing loses |
| Parallel running versus cost | Two systems at once is the cleanest evidence and the most expensive arrangement, and somebody has to fund the duplication |
| Attrition as fit versus ossification | Both look identical from outside, and the distinguishing test, whether experiments are still possible, is not observable from a distance |
| The completion boundary versus shipping | If attachment forms on completion, the advice is to change things before they are finished, which conflicts with finishing things |
| Open source as antidote versus attachment to the approach | Identity can attach to a way of working as firmly as to an artefact, and a strong methodological commitment is the same effect wearing different clothes |

## Open Questions

| How is parallel running funded? | The measurement argument requires it and the cost argument resists it |
