# The Asymmetry Of Proof: Dramatise The True Number Rather Than Inventing One, Because Comedy Polices Itself And Exaggeration Does Not

**version** v0.33.54
**date** 31 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Board, Risk, Communications

**type** Strategy brief

*Nineteenth of 31 July. The research the memo refers to is grounded and cited. This brief argues against one of the two strategies the memo proposes and for the other; that disagreement is stated openly rather than smoothed over. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A strategy for the situation where the other party does not have to be right: **the observation is an asymmetry in the burden of proof, where one party makes confident unevidenced claims to an audience that accepts what suits it and finds reasons to dismiss what does not, while the party making the careful evidenced case is judged on every sentence, so that a single error discredits everything, and the memo is correct that there is science behind this, since the foundational study on the topic found precisely that people accept confirming evidence at face value while subjecting disconfirming evidence to critical evaluation, with the striking consequence that presenting both sides with the same body of evidence can drive them further apart rather than closer together, and with the further finding that sophisticated audiences are more prone to this rather than less; there is a second asymmetry underneath the first, which the memo identifies and which no amount of evidence addresses, that some individuals face payoffs unrelated to the outcome, gaining if the bet pays and landing softly if it does not, so their willingness to carry risk is rational for them and irrational for the organisation; the memo then proposes two responses and they should be separated, because one of them would destroy the only asset this position has, since counter-exaggerating walks directly into the mechanism the memo itself identified, spends the accuracy that is the entire source of leverage, and in the version where a second entity is introduced to do the exaggerating becomes something that ends careers when discovered; and the second response is not only safer but stronger, because almost every example the memo gives is not exaggeration at all but the dramatisation of a true figure, so that being told you are comfortable with a third of customers affected, or that the plan requires three consecutive sixes, or that the strategy is to fly the plane with nobody who can fly it, are all vivid statements of things the register already contains, which is why comedy works here at all, since a joke that is not true is not funny and the audience's recognition is the accuracy check that exaggeration lacks.** It is the nineteenth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.44 risk-communication brief, the v0.33.54 register-maintains-itself brief, the v0.33.51 plug-profile series plan, the v0.33.54 mental-load brief, and the v0.33.50 no-judgement brief). New contributions: **the asymmetry named and grounded, the payoff asymmetry identified as immune to evidence, the argument that counter-exaggeration is self-defeating in precisely this situation, the reframing of the memo's own examples as dramatisation rather than fabrication, comedy identified as self-policing because untrue jokes fail, and the structural answer that a named dated acceptance succeeds where argument cannot.**

## The Asymmetry Is Real, And The Science Is Worse Than Expected

The memo describes the situation precisely. The project lead: **"you have a situation where there's an asymmetry of the requirement to be correct, an asymmetry of the requirement to be accurate."** With the mechanism on the receiving end. The project lead: **"the people listening to it are very biased, so they tend to support and grab hold on the things that they care about, and they find reasons to dismiss what they don't agree with."** And on the other side. The project lead: **"that body is judged by every single thing that he says, and if anything in his arguments is false, the whole thing loses credibility and is shut down."**

The science he refers to exists and matches almost word for word. The foundational 1979 study found that people holding strong opinions examine evidence in a biased manner, accepting confirming evidence at face value while subjecting disconfirming evidence to critical evaluation, and consequently drawing undue support for their existing position even from mixed or random findings.

Three further findings make the picture harder than the memo assumes.

**More evidence can make it worse.** The same study reports that exposing opposing factions to an identical body of evidence may produce polarisation rather than convergence. The instinct to respond to resistance with a better-evidenced case can therefore be actively counterproductive.

**Sophistication does not help.** Later reflections on this work note that each side tends to feel a neutral third party has subjected its own contentions to unduly harsh scrutiny while going easy on the other, and that this is particularly likely among sophisticated audiences. The boardroom is not a safer environment for this than anywhere else.

**The asymmetry is structural rather than personal.** The memo makes this point and it is worth preserving, because it changes the response. The project lead: **"I'm not saying that the bias is malicious; the bias is a natural piece of the puzzle that we have to take into account."**

## The Second Asymmetry: Payoff

Underneath the evidential asymmetry sits one that evidence cannot touch, and the memo names it directly. The project lead: **"there's a lot of execs who get massive payouts if things go wrong, so you sometimes think that the incentives are aligned with the company, and sometimes they're not."** With the logic laid out. The project lead: **"they're almost okay to roll the dice, because if what they're trying to do works out they get the benefits, and if it doesn't work out they still come out on top."**

This matters because it explains why better arguments keep failing. A person with an asymmetric payoff is not being irrational; they are being rational about a different objective function. No amount of accuracy changes a calculation whose downside somebody else carries, which means the response has to be structural rather than rhetorical. That is dealt with at the end.

## The No-Money Case

The memo raises a related situation. The project lead: **"if there is no money available to do anything and everything is cash-strapped, then what's the point of even having some of the conversations, because it's never going to happen."**

The corpus already has an answer and it is worth stating, because it converts a dead end into a recorded position. That situation is not a reason to stop; it is an acceptance. Accepted because we cannot fund a remediation, by the person who controls the budget, reviewed when the position changes. That is honest, it is cheap, it takes ten minutes, and it produces the one thing the conversation otherwise leaves behind: a record that the decision was made deliberately rather than by drift. The interval does the rest, because when money appears the review is already scheduled.

## The Fork

The memo proposes two responses and they are different in kind, so they should be assessed separately rather than as one strategy.

The first. The project lead: **"one of the ways to deal with that is to actually also make up stuff from our side, to exaggerate."** With a refinement intended to protect the position. The project lead: **"ideally you even have another entity behaving like this, so you don't burn your credibility angles."**

The second is everything that follows: humour, analogy, and making implications vivid.

I think the first is a mistake and the second is the strong version of what the memo actually wants, and the case for that is below rather than assumed.

## Why Counter-Exaggeration Is Self-Defeating Here Specifically

Not as a moral objection, but because it fails on its own terms in exactly this situation.

**It walks into the mechanism the memo identified.** The memo's own diagnosis is that the careful party is judged on every sentence and that one false statement discredits everything. Counter-exaggerating supplies that statement voluntarily. The asymmetry that is currently unfair becomes an asymmetry that is deserved, and it becomes permanent, because the next accurate warning arrives from a source with a demonstrated record of overstating.

**Accuracy is the only leverage this position has.** The party without the burden of proof already has authority, budget and the benefit of the doubt. The only thing the evidenced party holds is that its claims survive checking. That is a slow asset and a finite one, and it can be spent once.

**It is the corpus's own asset.** Everything built over these weeks is provenance: a claim resolves to a source, a source resolves to a commit, a risk cites a paragraph, a level is computed rather than asserted. A product whose entire differentiator is that its numbers can be checked cannot have an advocacy strategy that involves numbers which cannot.

**And the proxy version is worse rather than safer.** Introducing another entity to exaggerate on your behalf, so that your own credibility is insulated, is a describable practice with a name and a poor history. It is not insulation, because the connection is usually discoverable, and its discovery does not merely lose the argument. It retrospectively validates every dismissal ever made of the honest position, and it is the kind of thing that ends the careers of the people who authorised it. The corpus's 5 July position on grounded alarm rather than fear-selling was drawn precisely at this line.

## Look Again At The Examples: Almost None Require Inventing Anything

Here is why the disagreement above costs nothing. Take the memo's own illustrations and check them against what the corpus already produces.

| The memo's line | What it actually is |
|---|---|
| "So you're okay with a half a billion cost" | The modelled worst-case impact, stated as a question |
| "You're comfortable with thirty percent of our customers having this problem" | The blast radius, expressed in customers rather than systems |
| "You're okay for the company to go bust" | Recoverability at zero, stated plainly |
| "Not just our customers, another twenty million users" | The wider reach of the same exposure |
| "You're going to hit three sixes in a row with no mistakes" | Compound probability, made legible |
| "You'll pilot that plane with nobody who can fly it" | An analogy exposing an unstated assumption |
| "You'll need lucky charms, eighty percent of your strategy is luck" | Naming a dependency the plan does not acknowledge |

**None of these requires making anything up.** Every one is a true statement delivered vividly, and the corpus has spent weeks building the machinery that produces exactly these numbers: worst-case reach, blast radius, recoverability, the plug profile, the acceptance interval.

So the strategy is not exaggeration. It is **dramatisation of the true number**, and it is stronger for being true, because it is the version that survives the scrutiny the asymmetry guarantees. When somebody challenges the half a billion, the answer is a model with its assumptions listed. When somebody challenges an invented figure, there is no answer.

The memo's instinct that caution is a weakness here is right. The project lead: **"if you operate from a fact-based approach you tend to be a bit cautious; then don't be."** Being uncautious about *presentation* while remaining exact about *substance* is the whole move.

## Comedy Polices Itself, Which Is Why It Works

The memo arrives at humour and gives the reason without quite noticing that the reason is also the safeguard. The project lead: **"comedy works fundamentally when it's believable; it's not just about making stuff up, it's about having something that you can actually relate to."**

That is the key property. **A joke that is not true is not funny.** The laugh is a recognition response: the audience laughs because they see something they already half-knew and had not said. Which means humour has an accuracy check built into its delivery mechanism, and exaggeration does not. If the analogy overstates, it lands flat and the room knows, immediately and visibly, at no lasting cost. If a fabricated figure overstates, nobody notices for six months and then it is fatal.

Two further reasons humour is well suited to this specific problem.

**It does not present as an argument.** Biased assimilation is a defence against arguments. An analogy is not a claim to be evaluated; it is a frame, and it is inside before the defence engages.

**It travels.** The memo notes this. The project lead: **"the more comedy, the more interesting, the more meme-like the messaging."** A risk paper is read by the people already convinced. A line about needing three sixes in a row is repeated in the corridor by people who were not in the meeting, and repetition by peers does what a report cannot.

The corpus's own 5 July position is the boundary to hold: grounded alarm rather than fear. A vivid, true, funny statement of a real exposure is grounded alarm. The same sentence with an invented number is fear-selling, and the two look identical from outside until somebody checks.

## The Documented Corrective

There is an evidence-based technique for this and it is close to what the memo's better examples do. The same research group that identified biased assimilation published a corrective strategy under the heading of considering the opposite, which works by forcing explicit engagement with the alternative rather than by supplying more evidence for one side.

Read that way, "so you are comfortable with a third of customers affected" is not rhetoric at all. It is an instruction to consider the opposite, delivered as a question. It requires the other party to inhabit the position they are implicitly taking, which is the one thing biased assimilation does not defend against, because it is not a counter-argument to be dismissed but their own position restated.

That is probably the most useful framing for anyone being trained to do this: **you are not trying to win the argument, you are trying to make them say the thing out loud.**

## The Structural Answer, Which Is The Product

For the payoff asymmetry, none of the above works, and the corpus already contains the answer.

You cannot argue somebody out of a position that pays them either way. What you can do is make the position explicit, attributed and visible. An executive comfortable rolling the dice in a meeting is a different person when asked to accept a named risk, in writing, with a date, an interval, and a record their board will see. Nothing about their incentives has changed; what has changed is that the decision is now legible to people whose incentives differ.

This is exactly the mechanism described earlier today: accountability that is anticipated and reviewed changes behaviour, not because it persuades, but because it makes the decision visible to an audience whose reaction cannot be fully predicted. And it is why the register is a better instrument than any argument.

So the sequence for a genuinely irrational or misaligned counterpart is: **dramatise the true numbers so that what is being accepted is unmistakable, use humour to make the implication travel further than the meeting, and then ask them to sign it.** The first two get you to the third. The third is the one that works.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not an endorsement of counter-exaggeration.** It fails on its own terms in exactly this situation, and the brief says so.
- **Not an argument against vividness.** Precision about substance and boldness about presentation are compatible and should be combined.
- **Not a claim that better evidence wins.** The research says it can polarise, which is why the answer is framing and structure rather than volume.
- **Not a moral lecture.** The strategic case against fabrication is the stronger one and is the one made here.
- **Not applicable to the payoff asymmetry.** Rhetoric does not touch misaligned incentives; a named acceptance does.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Dramatisation versus the same accusation | A vivid true statement will still be called scaremongering by somebody who dislikes it, and the defence is the model behind the number rather than the tone |
| Humour versus seniority and culture | Comedy is powerful and situational, it fails badly in some rooms and some cultures, and a joke that misses can cost the relationship the argument needed |
| Refusing to counter-exaggerate versus losing | The honest position may simply lose to the confident one in the short term, and this brief offers no comfort about that beyond the long game |
| Considering the opposite versus feeling cornered | Making somebody say the implication out loud is effective and can read as a trap, which hardens rather than opens |
| The register as the answer versus who signs | It works when there is a functioning acceptance process, and the memo describes environments where there may not be one |
| Naming the payoff asymmetry versus saying it out loud | It is real and it is close to accusing a named person of self-interest, which is rarely survivable in the room |
