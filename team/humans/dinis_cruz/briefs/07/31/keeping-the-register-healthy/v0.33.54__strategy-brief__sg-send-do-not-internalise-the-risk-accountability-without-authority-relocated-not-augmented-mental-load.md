# Do Not Internalise The Risk: Misallocated Accountability Is The Named Driver Of Burnout, And This Workflow Moves It Rather Than Adding Authority

## What This Is

A property of the acceptance workflow that emerged in a conversation about positioning, and which the memo asks to have researched rather than assumed: **the observation is that risk work carries a mental load which the workflow reduces, because a risk professional who knows the business has explicitly accepted an exposure is carrying something different from one who privately believes they are the only person who understands it, and the project lead has watched his own teams take risks personally, almost owning them, at real personal cost; his advice to them was that risk decisions belong to the business, that the job is to ensure the business is well informed, and that internalising a risk somebody else decided to carry is both unnecessary and harmful; the research he asks for exists and is substantial, since surveys of security leaders report between sixty-three and seventy-six percent experiencing or witnessing burnout in a single year, with a quarter of leaders reported to be moving into entirely different roles because of work-related stress, and crucially the mechanism most frequently named as the defining pressure of the role is precisely the one the memo describes, accountability without the authority to match it, which maps onto the established finding that strain is produced by high demand combined with low control; the standard prescription in that literature is to give the security leader more authority, which is correct and almost never happens, because authority over every business unit's decisions is not something an organisation can hand to one function; the corpus solves the same equation from the other side, leaving authority where it already sits and moving the accountability to meet it, so the person who decides is the person who signs, which is a considerably more achievable rearrangement than the usual one; and the reason it works is that it replaces reassurance with evidence, because telling somebody not to own a risk is advice they must talk themselves into, while a dated record naming somebody else as the acceptor is a fact they can point at, which is also why the same mechanism improves decision quality, since a professional who is calibrated rather than alarmed makes better judgements, and a team that is not depleted in normal conditions is a team that can still think during an incident.** It is the sixteenth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.54 register-maintains-itself brief, the v0.33.46 risk-acceptance-redefined brief, the v0.33.50 no-judgement brief, the v0.33.49 fractal-registers brief, and the v0.33.53 scenarios-are-the-MVP brief). New contributions: **the mental load property named as a positioning argument, the identification that the memo's mechanism is the most-cited driver of burnout in this profession, the observation that the corpus relocates accountability rather than augmenting authority and that this is the more achievable of the two, and the distinction between reassurance and documented evidence as the source of the relief.**

## The Observation

It arose in a presentation about how the pieces fit together, which is worth noting because it means somebody outside the team saw it before the team articulated it. The project lead: **"one of the things that was interesting on the risk acceptance was how risk acceptance the way we're doing it, where you get underwritten and the confirmation of risk from the top, would basically reduce stress quite a lot."** And the effect. The project lead: **"members of the risk team and other parts of the business would actually have much less mental load, because they are comfortable that the business is supporting them."**

The pattern being described has a specific shape. The project lead: **"I would see the risk professionals almost own the risk; they almost take it personally with the risks that the business was taking, and it was a massive source of stress."**

That is a precise description of something familiar to anyone who has run a security or risk function. The person who understands an exposure best is frequently the person with the least ability to resolve it, and the gap between those two things is carried privately.

## The Advice, And Why It Is Hard To Take

The memo records what he told his teams, and it is good advice. The project lead: **"at the end of the day, it's the business's decision to make risk decisions; our job is to make sure that the business is well informed, and that risks are not taking a higher proportion, but it's very important that you don't own the risk, you don't internalise the risk, because ultimately that's the job of the business, that's the job of the executives."** With the corollary. The project lead: **"it's the executives that should either feel stressed or not, and if they are stressed, if they're not happy with it, they should be funding the risk reduction or not making those decisions."**

The advice is correct and it is difficult to act on, because it asks somebody to stop feeling responsible for something they can see clearly and cannot fix. Telling a conscientious professional not to internalise a risk is asking them to talk themselves out of a feeling, and the feeling usually wins, because in the absence of any record the private sense of sole custody is not obviously wrong. If nobody has written down that the business accepted this, then in a meaningful sense nobody has.

That is the gap the workflow closes, and it is why the structural version of the advice works better than the spoken version.

## This Is The Named Driver, Not A Side Effect

The memo asks for research and the research is unambiguous, both about the scale of the problem and about its cause.

On scale: a 2025 survey of security leaders reported that sixty-three percent had personally experienced or witnessed burnout among peers in the preceding year, while a separate study put the figure at seventy-six percent; two-thirds report feeling at risk of burnout, and more than half report losing sleep over threats and the pressure of accountability. Forecasts suggest that around a quarter of cybersecurity leaders will move into entirely different roles because of work-related stress, against a workforce gap reported at four point eight million unfilled positions. Median tenure in the role is frequently reported at under two years. The World Health Organization classified burnout in 2019 as an occupational phenomenon rather than a medical condition, characterised by exhaustion, negativism and reduced professional efficacy.

On cause, the convergence is striking. **The mechanism named most often, across independent sources, is accountability without the authority to match it.** Security leaders are held accountable for the security posture of an entire organisation while lacking the authority to ensure that the rest of it does what it is supposed to do. One study of leaders in the field states directly that burnout intensifies when responsibility is high and control is low, and connects it to the established occupational research on the same relationship. Another characterises the position as high-stakes accountability with low-level control, noting that leaders must depend on other teams, legacy systems, third parties and the everyday behaviour of employees, any one of which can produce the outcome they will answer for.

That is the memo's observation, arrived at independently from personal experience, and it turns out to be the single most-cited structural driver of distress in the profession.

## The Standard Remedy, And Why It Rarely Arrives

The prescription in that literature is consistent: align authority with accountability, give leaders the resources and decision rights to match what they are answerable for, and empower rather than merely hold responsible.

It is the right diagnosis and it is very hard to implement, because the authority in question is authority over other people's decisions. A security function cannot realistically be given the power to overrule a business unit's commercial judgement, a product team's release decision, or a supplier relationship owned elsewhere. Organisations therefore keep arriving at the correct conclusion and failing to act on it, which is part of why the numbers have not improved.

## The Same Equation, Solved From The Other Side

Here is where the corpus's approach differs, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the actual contribution.

```
   THE MISMATCH        accountability sits here    authority sits there
                              (risk function)        (business owner)

   USUAL REMEDY        move AUTHORITY leftward     rarely possible
   THIS WORKFLOW       move ACCOUNTABILITY right   already the truth
```

Rather than giving the risk professional authority they will not be given, the workflow puts the accountability where the authority already is. The person who can decide whether to carry an exposure is the person who accepts it, by name, with a date and an interval. Nothing has to be taken from anyone, because the arrangement simply records what was always the case: the business made the decision.

Two things follow. The risk professional's role becomes what it should always have been, which is to make sure the decision is well informed, and their contribution is measurable in terms of the quality of information rather than the absence of incidents. And the accountability lands somewhere it can act, which matters because the memo's own formulation is the right test: an executive who is uncomfortable with a risk has the option to fund its reduction, and an analyst who is uncomfortable has no option at all.

This is also the same distinction established earlier this week between confirming a fact and accepting an exposure. A technical stakeholder confirms that something is true. A business stakeholder accepts what it means. Separating them protects the person who cannot carry the consequence from being treated as though they can.

## Documentation Beats Reassurance

This is the mechanism, and it is why the workflow does something a conversation cannot.

Being told the risk is not yours is reassurance, and reassurance decays. It has to be re-administered, it is contradicted by the private evidence of being the only person who seems to care, and it collapses the moment something goes wrong and attention turns to whoever raised it.

A dated acceptance naming a specific person is not reassurance, it is a fact. It can be pointed at. It survives the incident, and it survives the conversation afterwards. The relief comes from the artefact rather than from the encouragement, which is why this property belongs to the system rather than to good management.

It also converts an unbounded worry into a bounded one. Without a record, a risk professional carries every unresolved exposure indefinitely and simultaneously. With one, each exposure has an owner, a date and a next review, and what remains genuinely theirs is a much smaller set: the things nobody has yet been asked to accept.

## Calibration And Decision Quality

The memo makes an argument that deserves separating from the wellbeing case, because it stands on its own. The project lead: **"good risk management is about making good risk decisions, and sometimes when we have not the correct calibration of the risks, and we become risk averse, or we become exaggerated and become stressed, you introduce areas where you're not going to make good decisions."**

A distressed professional does not merely suffer; they judge differently. The distortion runs in both directions, toward exaggeration of familiar threats and toward fatigue-driven dismissal of new ones, and neither is calibrated. Since the entire value of the function is the quality of its judgement, this is an operational argument rather than a humane one, and it is the version that will land in a boardroom.

The supporting data exists: in one study, thirty-nine percent of professionals reported that burnout had reduced their productivity and a third reported reduced engagement, which are described as leading indicators of organisational risk rather than as personnel matters.

## Sharp For The Incident

The memo's closing point is the most practically compelling and should be part of any external version. The project lead: **"you don't want your team to be very stressed at the best of times, in normal times, because you need them to be super sharp during incidents."**

Incident response is the highest-stakes cognitive work the function ever does, performed under time pressure, with incomplete information, at unsociable hours, by exactly the people who have been depleted by the preceding months. A team run at capacity in normal conditions has nothing left for the abnormal ones, and the moment that matters most is the moment they are least able to meet it.

Reserve capacity is therefore an operational requirement rather than a benefit, and anything that reduces sustained background load is buying performance at the moment of maximum consequence.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim that this addresses burnout.** Burnout in this profession is driven by workload, under-resourcing, always-on incidents, blame culture and a large skills gap. Misallocated accountability is one driver among several, and it is the only one this workflow touches.
- **Not clinical guidance.** Anyone experiencing serious or sustained distress needs proper support, and no process substitutes for that.
- **Not a wellbeing product.** This is a property of a risk workflow, and presenting it as a mental health intervention would be both inaccurate and distasteful.
- **Not a claim that stress disappears.** It is relocated to where it can produce action, which is a different claim.
- **Not a substitute for good management.** The artefact helps a good manager and will not rescue a bad one.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Relocating stress versus reducing it | Making accountability explicit moves discomfort onto the executive who accepts, which is defensible because they can act on it, and it is still moving a burden rather than removing one |
| A record of who accepted versus a blame instrument | The same artefact that relieves an analyst can be used after an incident to find someone to hold responsible, and if it is used that way it will make things worse than no record at all |
| Marketing a mental health benefit | This is a genuine property and a distasteful pitch, and the line between describing it honestly and exploiting it is one the corpus should be careful about |
| One driver among many | Misallocated accountability is the most-cited driver and it is not the only one, and a workflow that claims more than it delivers will be resented by the people it claims to help |
| Explicit acceptance versus what people will actually sign | Some executives will decline to accept in writing what they were previously happy to tolerate informally, which is the mechanism working and will feel like obstruction |
| Documented relief versus organisational culture | In an organisation that punishes the messenger, a record naming the acceptor may not protect the person who raised it at all |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| Is the effect measurable? | Whether teams using the workflow report lower load, which would need a before-and-after rather than an assertion |
| How is the record protected from becoming a blame tool? | The governance around post-incident use, which determines whether the property holds or inverts |
| Does it work below the executive level? | The relief described is for the risk function; whether an engineer carrying an unaccepted exposure feels the same benefit is untested |
| Should this be said out loud in positioning? | It is real and it is delicate, and the framing that describes it without exploiting it needs writing carefully |
| What happens to the unaccepted set? | The residue that remains genuinely the professional's own, which is where the load concentrates once everything else has an owner |
| Is the calibration argument separable? | The decision-quality case may travel further in a boardroom than the wellbeing case, and may be the better external framing |
