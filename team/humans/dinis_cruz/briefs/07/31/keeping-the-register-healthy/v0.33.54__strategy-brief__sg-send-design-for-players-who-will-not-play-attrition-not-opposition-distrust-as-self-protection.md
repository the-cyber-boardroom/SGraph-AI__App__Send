# Design For Players Who Will Not Play: Attrition Beats Opposition, And Mutual Distrust Turns Out To Be The Safety Mechanism

## What This Is

A design principle for systems that span parties who did not choose to be in them, and an inversion that turns the principal obstacle into a safeguard: **the principle is that a multi-party system must work when the players do not want to play, do not like it, do not see the point, or actively resist, and the memo's sharpest observation is that outright refusal is not the failure mode worth designing for, because the thing that actually kills these systems is attrition, the quiet non-doing of people who never refused anything and simply had other work, which comes with permanent plausible deniability since doing one thing requires stopping another and being busy is always true; the literature names this directly, distinguishing active resistance, meaning open criticism and explicit refusal, from passive resistance, meaning inertia, withdrawal, procrastination and disengagement, and notes that the passive forms are less apparent and slow things down substantially, with one practitioner formulation putting the underlying error precisely, that organisations stop at installation rather than implementation and adoption thereby becomes optional; the corpus already has the countermeasure and it has not previously been described this way, because a system in which not deciding produces a visible escalating item with somebody's name on it is a system in which non-participation is no longer indistinguishable from busyness, which removes the deniability that makes attrition work; and then the inversion, which is the most original idea in the memo, that parties who distrust each other, dislike being accountable for things outside their domain, and would each prefer the responsibility landed elsewhere, will not collectively agree to a badly calibrated decision, because challenging the other party and pushing responsibility away are exactly the behaviours that produce scrutiny, so the misalignment that looks like the obstacle is the mechanism, and this matters more than it first appears, because it repairs the specific failure identified earlier today, that anticipated review stops improving judgement once the reviewer's preference becomes guessable, and a person answering simultaneously to a manager, a supplier, a customer, a regulator and their own professional duties faces an audience that cannot be guessed by construction.** It is the twentieth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.54 register-maintains-itself brief, the v0.33.49 fractal-registers brief, the v0.33.46 risk-acceptance-redefined brief, the v0.33.54 asymmetry-of-proof brief, and the v0.33.53 scenarios-are-the-MVP brief). New contributions: **non-cooperation stated as a design premise rather than an adoption problem, attrition with plausible deniability identified as the operative failure mode, the roll-up mechanism reframed as the anti-attrition property, mutual distrust identified as adversarial verification, the observation that multi-party accountability defeats the guessable-audience failure, and the honest tension that the safeguard requires the participation the premise says cannot be assumed.**

## Design For Players Who Will Not Play

The premise is stated as a design requirement rather than a complaint, which is what makes it useful. The project lead: **"it's very important that you create a system that will work even when the players don't want to play, don't like it, don't see the point, or actively resist."**

Most governance systems are designed for the cooperative case and then deployed into the real one. They assume that if the workflow is sensible and the value is explained, participation follows. The memo inverts the default: assume it does not, and ask what the system does then.

The corpus has an additional reason to take this seriously, which the memo names. The project lead: **"especially with risk acceptance, we are bringing accountability to places where a lot of accountability doesn't want to be there."** This is not a neutral tool being adopted. It attaches names to exposures that were previously ambient, which means resistance is the expected response rather than a surprise.

## Attrition, Not Opposition

The most useful observation in the memo is about which failure mode actually matters. The project lead: **"a lot of systems fail because there's this sort of passive aggressiveness, where people are not helping the other team, or they're not doing something, but they're not going out of their way to do it anyway, so attrition ends up being the one that kills everything."**

And the reason it is so effective. The project lead: **"a lot of the times, in order to do something, you do need to stop doing something else, so suddenly there's also a lot of plausible deniability; hey look, I was doing all the other stuff, I didn't have time to do that."**

That is the crux. **Open refusal is manageable because it is visible**: it can be escalated, argued with, or overruled. Quiet non-participation is not, because the excuse is always true. Everybody genuinely does have other work, so the non-participant never has to lie, never presents a target, and cannot be distinguished from someone who is simply busy. A system that requires effort to adopt and produces nothing when ignored loses to this every time.

## The Literature Names It

The distinction the memo draws is established. Research on organisational change separates **active resistance**, meaning open criticism, conflict, sabotage and explicit refusal, from **passive resistance**, meaning inertia, withdrawal, procrastination, absenteeism and declining commitment. Both aim at preserving the status quo, and the literature notes that the passive forms, while less apparent, can significantly slow a change process. The two are also reported to arise from different psychological causes, which matters because it means they need different responses.

Scale figures circulate widely and should be treated carefully: the frequently repeated claim that around seventy percent of change initiatives fail is heavily recycled and its provenance is weak, though multiple studies do report that between sixty and seventy percent of large-scale initiatives fall short of their goals. Resistance is commonly cited as the leading factor, in one summary behind seventy-two percent of failures and in a separate study forty-three percent, with one large practitioner survey reporting that seventy-six percent of initiatives encounter resistance at some level.

One source disputes the common diagnosis and is worth recording because it strengthens rather than weakens the memo's position: it argues that failures are driven primarily by poor design, weak sponsorship and insufficient reinforcement rather than by employee resistance, and offers a formulation that lands exactly on the memo's point. **Organisations confuse installation with implementation, and when they stop at installation, adoption becomes optional.**

Optional adoption is precisely the condition attrition exploits.

## The Countermeasure Is Already Built

The corpus has an answer to this and has not previously described it in these terms.

In an ordinary governance system, not participating produces nothing. Silence looks like silence, and silence is indistinguishable from workload. That is what gives attrition its power.

In this model, not participating produces something. An unaccepted risk does not sit quietly; it becomes critical by default and rolls upward until somebody with standing accepts it. So the person who declines to engage does not achieve invisibility. They generate a visible item, attributed, escalating, that their manager and their manager's manager will see.

```
   ORDINARY SYSTEM              THIS MODEL
   ignore it -> nothing         ignore it -> unaccepted
   silence looks like busy      unaccepted -> critical by default
   deniability intact           critical -> rolls up with your name on it
   attrition wins               deniability gone
```

**Non-participation stops being a way of avoiding the system and becomes a way of using it badly, in public.** That is the structural answer to the memo's problem, and it works precisely because it does not depend on anyone wanting to cooperate.

Two honest limits. The mechanism requires that somebody upward is actually looking, which is the same cultural dependency identified this morning. And it converts attrition into escalation, which means the top of the organisation absorbs the load created by everyone below who declined to decide.

## The Inversion: Distrust As The Safety Mechanism

Here the memo turns its own premise around, and this is the most original idea in it. The project lead: **"they don't trust each other, and sometimes they don't even like each other, and they don't want to be held accountable and take responsibility for something that is outside of their domain."** Then the inversion. The project lead: **"this situation, if you take into account what it means, means that the system has a very built-in self-protection mechanism, because it's unlikely that if bad decisions are being made these multiple parties will all agree to it."** With the mechanism. The project lead: **"it's almost in their interest to challenge the other one, it's in their interest to pass the responsibility to somebody else."**

This is adversarial verification, and it is the same principle that makes several other institutions work: opposing counsel, external audit, short sellers, peer review. None of those rely on the participants being cooperative or public-spirited. They rely on the participants wanting different things, in opposing directions.

Applied here, the properties are useful. **Nobody accepts a risk they believe belongs to somebody else**, so exposures get pushed toward their correct owner by self-interest rather than by policy. **Nobody lets an overstatement stand if it lands on them**, so claims get challenged without anyone having to organise a review. And **collective agreement to a badly calibrated decision becomes unlikely**, because it would require every party to accept a share none of them wants.

So the misalignment that looks like the obstacle to adoption is the thing that makes the output trustworthy. That is worth saying out loud in positioning, because the objection this system will most often face is that the parties do not get along.

## Why This Repairs This Morning's Failure Mode

There is a specific connection to the brief written earlier today that makes the inversion more than an elegant observation.

That brief established, from the accountability research, that anticipated review improves judgement only under particular conditions, and that the effect **collapses when the person can guess what their reviewer wants**, at which point they abandon the effort to reach a defensible position and simply shift toward the presumed preference. That was recorded as the principal design risk, because within a few months most people can predict their own manager quite well.

Multi-party accountability dissolves that risk structurally. The memo describes exactly the situation. The project lead: **"people then have to start thinking, when I accept this risk, how am I going to defend this to my boss, my partner, my regulation, my fiduciary duties as a professional."**

You can guess one audience. You cannot simultaneously satisfy a manager who wants delivery, a supplier who does not want the liability, a customer who wants assurance, a regulator who wants evidence, and a professional duty that has its own standard. **The audience becomes unguessable by construction**, which is the condition under which anticipated accountability actually produces careful thinking rather than conformity.

So the memo's inversion is not only a defence of the system against distrust. It is the repair for the most serious weakness identified in the mechanism this morning.

## When Suppliers And Customers Get Access

The memo identifies where this becomes strongest. The project lead: **"especially when you introduce partner supplier relationships, where the suppliers and the consumers who have quite a lot of power start to get access to some of the risk data, then the system ought to correct itself, because you have a lot more accountability."**

This connects directly to the buyer-driven adoption argument made earlier today about due diligence. A customer with commercial leverage who can see a supplier's risk position is the most effective auditor available, because they have both the standing to ask and a reason to care, and they cost nothing to deploy.

It also has a limit worth naming immediately: the mechanism requires the data to cross the boundary. Adversarial verification only works if the adversary can see something, and sharing risk positions with customers is precisely what organisations resist. So the strongest form of the self-correction depends on the transparency that is hardest to obtain, which is a real constraint rather than a detail.

## The Tension Inside The Memo

Worth stating plainly, because the two halves are in some tension and the resolution matters.

The first half says the system must work when parties will not participate. The second says the system is protected because parties will challenge each other. **But challenging requires engagement.** A disengaged party does not scrutinise what they are asked to accept; they simply do not respond. Adversarial verification needs adversaries who turn up.

The resolution is the roll-up mechanism, and it is why that property is load-bearing rather than incidental. Non-participation is not free: it produces a visible escalating item attributed to the non-participant. That cost is what forces enough engagement for the adversarial dynamic to begin, and once a party is engaged, their self-interest does the rest without anyone needing to motivate them.

So the sequence is: **make non-participation visible, which forces minimal engagement, which activates the mutual scrutiny, which produces the calibration.** Each stage depends on the one before it, and the first is the only one the system controls directly.

## Definition Of Success

The memo closes on something it does not answer. The project lead: **"over time you have to have a clear set of definitions of success, and that's how you then measure if it's working or not."**

That is right and it is unresolved. It is also harder here than usual, because the obvious measures are misleading. Participation rate rewards compliance rather than judgement. Number of accepted risks rewards volume. Absence of incidents measures luck over any short period.

Candidates worth considering, offered rather than settled: the proportion of exposures with a named acceptor at an appropriate altitude, the time between a risk becoming visible and somebody accepting it, the rate at which acceptances are challenged or returned by a reviewer, and the proportion of risks whose stated owner has changed after challenge. The last two are the interesting ones, because they measure whether the adversarial mechanism is running at all. A system in which nothing is ever challenged is not a system in which everyone agrees; it is one in which nobody is looking.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not an adoption strategy.** It is a design premise: assume non-cooperation and build for it.
- **Not a claim that resistance is irrational.** Accountability arriving where it was not wanted is a reasonable thing to resist.
- **Not dependent on parties getting along.** The safeguard works because they do not.
- **Not automatic.** The self-correction needs the data to cross boundaries and somebody upward to be looking.
- **Not a settled measurement framework.** The definition of success is named as necessary and left open.

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What is the definition of success? | Named as necessary in the memo and unresolved, with challenge rate the most informative candidate |
| What stops escalation overwhelming the top? | The volume created when everyone below declines to decide, and whether an intermediate mechanism is needed |
| How much data must cross a boundary? | The minimum a supplier or customer needs to see for the adversarial mechanism to engage |
| How is circulating responsibility distinguished from correct routing? | Both look like passing it on, and only one is productive |
| Does the roll-up actually force engagement? | The load-bearing assumption of the whole design, and it has not been tested |
| What does a healthy challenge rate look like? | Too low means nobody is looking, too high means the system is not calibrated, and neither bound is known |
