# The Project Manager Is Where The Register Becomes Work: A Register Per Project, The Net Score Nobody Measures, And The Reference Class Nobody Has

**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Strategy, Commercial, Engineering

*Fifteenth of 31 July, from a conversation with a practising project manager. Research on project estimation and benefits is grounded and cited. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

## What This Is

A position on who the natural ally for this work is, arrived at from a real conversation and pointing at a group the corpus has not yet built for: **a project manager running several teams was asked whether it would be useful if every stakeholder were aware of and accountable for the project's risks, aligned on them, with executives understanding the risks, the actions, the funding and the deadlines, and answered that it would be amazing, which is the answer of somebody describing something they do not have and rarely see; the reason they do not have it is not negligence, since a project manager is measured on getting things done and the risk view is the one that competes with delivery for attention, so decisions get taken without full sight of their implications and progress gets reported on a single axis, which means a project can be entirely on schedule while quietly introducing exposure to the business, depending on things nobody accounted for, or letting costs run in a way the status report does not show; the structural answer is a register per project alongside the register per stakeholder, and the interesting property of a project register is that a project should carry a net positive risk score, reducing more exposure than it creates, which is a claim almost every business case implies and almost no organisation ever measures; worse, the score decays, because delays, scope changes, cost increases and things learned during execution erode the advantages that justified the project in the first place, and nothing in a normal governance cycle re-tests the business case mid-flight, which the research on large projects names precisely as lock-in through sunk cost, escalation of commitment and the need for justification; and the deepest argument is about the future rather than the present, since the only proven method for making better project decisions is to use the recorded outcomes of comparable past projects rather than fresh estimates, and the binding constraint on that method has always been that the outcome datasets do not exist, so an organisation that records what was promised, what was accepted, what changed and what actually happened is building the reference class it will need, as a by-product of running the register; all of which makes the project manager the ally, because maintaining project data is already their job rather than an imposition, because every funded risk decision lands on them as work, and because the loop from decision to outcome to better future decision closes through them.** It is the fifteenth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.54 register-maintains-itself brief, the v0.33.49 fractal-registers brief, the v0.33.53 accepted-is-not-acceptable brief, the v0.33.48 compete-against-the-burden brief, and the v0.33.49 interval-ladder brief). New contributions: **the project manager identified as the point where the register becomes work and the ally that follows, the register per project with a net positive score requirement, erosion of the business case during execution named as a rateable phenomenon, and the recognition that the historical record being built is the reference class the only proven debiasing method requires.**

## The Conversation

Worth recording as the origin, because the answer is more informative than the question. The project lead: **"wouldn't it be amazing if you knew, on a project, all your stakeholders were fully aware and accountable for your risks, and aligned with it, and everybody had a really good understanding, including the executives, of the risks for the project and the actions and the funding and the deadlines."** And the response. The project lead: **"the answer was, yeah, that would have been amazing."**

That is somebody describing an absence. And the absence is normal rather than exceptional. The project lead: **"it's actually going to be quite rare to find project management and teams that have, at that level, a really good mapping and alignment of risks, especially with stakeholders."**

The reason offered is generous and correct. The project lead: **"the project managers have a really hard job because they're basically trying to get stuff done."** Risk work competes with delivery for the same attention, and delivery is what the role is measured on.

## Status Without Risk Is Half A Status

The consequence the memo identifies is sharper than the usual complaint about risk registers. The project lead: **"how is the project going? If you don't take the risks view, you can have a very unilateral view of the status."**

A project reporting green on scope, schedule and budget can simultaneously be introducing exposure to the business, depending on assumptions nobody has tested, or accumulating cost commitments that have not yet surfaced. The project lead: **"you can have a project that actually delivers, that has massive risks, that maybe has introduced risk to the business, that depends on other things that are not taken into account."**

So the status report and the risk position are two different questions, and only one of them is asked weekly. That is a gap a register per project closes directly, and it is a gap a project manager feels rather than one that has to be explained to them.

## A Register Per Project

The structural move. The project lead: **"not only are we going to have a risk register per stakeholder, we also need to have a risk register per project."**

This is the fractal register pattern applied along a different axis. The corpus has registers per accepting role, arranged by altitude. A project is a different kind of accepting entity: temporary, cross-cutting, with its own stakeholders and its own exposure, and it is the unit at which funding decisions are actually made. Both axes are needed, and a risk can appear in both, seen once as an exposure somebody owns and once as an exposure a project creates or removes.

```
   BY ROLE (altitude)              BY PROJECT (temporary, cross-cutting)
   engineer -> manager -> board    this project's risks
   who owns this exposure          what it creates, what it removes
   permanent                       has a start and an end
                                   and should net out positive
```

## The Net Score, And Why Nobody Has It

The idea that repays the most thought. The project lead: **"the project, even from a cost point of view, has to have a net positive risk score."** Followed immediately by the honest observation. The project lead: **"but there's a lot of projects where this never gets measured, so how do you know that the project is actually having a positive net score?"**

The claim is implicit in every business case. A project is justified because it produces something worth more than it costs, and where the project is a remediation the value is explicitly risk reduced. What is rarely counted is the exposure the project itself creates: new systems, new access, new dependencies, new third parties, new agents. A remediation that closes one exposure and opens two has a negative net score and will report as a success.

Making it explicit has three consequences. It gives a project register a single headline number that a board can read. It forces the risks a project creates to be named at approval rather than discovered later. And it makes possible the thing the next section is about, which is noticing when the number changes.

## Erosion: The Business Case Decays In Flight

This is the strongest observation in the memo and the research supports it precisely. The project lead: **"sometimes the delays, or certain decisions that you make during the project, start to erode the advantages of the project in the first place."** With the pattern. The project lead: **"there's lots of projects that get funded because of certain requirements or certain decisions, and after the project gets executed, either you learn, or delays, or more costs, and sometimes the benefits don't fully materialise."**

The literature on large projects identifies exactly this and names its mechanism. The pattern of underestimating costs, time and risks while overestimating benefits is described as the planning fallacy at scale, with three drivers: optimism bias, defined as judging future events more positively than actual experience warrants; uniqueness bias, the belief that our project is different regardless of how similar it is to previous ones; and strategic misrepresentation, the deliberate shaping of information to secure approval. Cost escalation in large projects is reported not to have improved across seventy years, which suggests the problem is structural rather than a matter of better practice.

More directly relevant is the concept of lock-in, which the same work breaks into four indicators: **sunk costs, escalation of commitment, the need for justification, and inflexibility with the closure of alternatives.** That is a precise description of why a project whose case has decayed continues anyway.

And the third of those connects to a finding recorded earlier today. The brief on the self-correcting property noted that people accountable for positions they are committed to spend their effort justifying rather than reconsidering them. Applied to a risk acceptance, that is a warning about re-acceptance. Applied to a project, it is the same psychology producing lock-in. **The two are one mechanism at different scales**, which suggests the same countermeasure: re-examination should lead with what has changed rather than with the decision previously taken.

The practical proposal follows the corpus's own vocabulary. A project's net score is not a number set at approval; it is a level in a dated ledger, re-tested at intervals, with the interval implying the response. A project whose net score has crossed below zero is a project whose continuation is now an acceptance somebody should make explicitly rather than by default.

## The Historical Record Is The Binding Constraint

The memo's closing argument is about the future, and it is stronger than it appears. The project lead: **"part of the reason why we're doing all this is so that in the future, better decisions can be made; if you don't have access to historical data like this, how are you going to make better decisions in the future?"**

That question has a well-established answer, and it turns the memo's point into something considerably more forceful.

The proven method for improving project forecasts is to take an outside view: rather than estimating a project bottom-up, place it in a class of comparable past projects and use their **actual outcomes** rather than their original estimates. The approach traces to work on the planning fallacy in the nineteen-seventies and was first applied in practice in 2004, when the promoters of a tram line submitted a base cost of two hundred and fifty-five million pounds with a twenty-five percent contingency, and a reference class of forty-six comparable rail projects showed the estimate was optimistic and required substantially larger uplifts. The method was subsequently adopted by the United Kingdom Treasury and transport department, including for a fifteen-billion-pound rail programme.

Its advantage is that it works whether the original estimate was over-optimistic or deliberately understated, because it never uses the estimate at all, only what comparable projects actually did.

And here is the point that matters. The recurring obstacle reported in that literature is that **valid datasets of comparable outcomes are rare**, and the first practical application was only possible because years of systematic research had assembled one. The method is not the constraint. The data is.

Which means an organisation running registers per project, recording what was promised, what was accepted, what changed during execution and what was actually delivered, **is assembling its own reference class as a by-product of ordinary operation.** After a dozen projects it can answer questions about its own delivery that no external benchmark can. That is a compounding asset nobody has to fund separately, and it is the same move the corpus already makes with revealed appetite, which reads the record of decisions actually taken rather than the policy describing intent.

## Why The Project Manager Is The Ally

Four reasons, and the last is the one that matters most.

**The data is already their job.** The project lead: **"project managers are one of the key pieces of the puzzle where it is already in their job spec to maintain the data."** This matters more than it sounds, because it inverts the usual adoption problem. Every other role in the corpus's chain is being asked to do something additional. A project manager is being offered a better way to do something they already do and are already accountable for, which is the burden-not-features position applied to a person rather than to a product.

**Funded risk work becomes their work.** The project lead: **"when you have a risk reduction project, the work will end up with the project manager, with somebody whose responsibility is to manage the project."** Every acceptance decision that resolves to fund it produces a project, and every project has a manager. They are the downstream recipient of the entire acceptance workflow.

**They have an unmet need nobody serves.** No existing tool tells a project manager whether the project is still worth doing. Schedule, budget and scope are all instrumented. The question of whether the justification still holds is asked at approval and then never again.

**And the loop closes through them.** This is the structural reason rather than the commercial one. A register produces decisions, decisions produce funded projects, projects produce outcomes, and outcomes are the historical record that makes the next decision better. The project manager sits at the point where decisions become work and where work becomes evidence. **If the corpus does not serve them, the chain breaks at its last step and never closes into a loop.**

The conclusion the memo reaches is therefore right and slightly understated. The project lead: **"these are one of our best allies, and we have to have a really good message and really good systems that we can give to them, but also UIs and technology that we should be developing specifically for them."**

## What To Build For Them

Sketched rather than specified, and worth a proper design pass.

A project view of the register, showing risks the project carries, risks it creates and risks it removes, with the net position visible. The net score as a dated level with a history, so that erosion is visible as a trend rather than discovered at closure. A stakeholder alignment view, since the original question was about whether stakeholders are aware and accountable, which the corpus can answer directly because acceptance is attached to named people. And a closure record capturing what was promised against what was delivered, which is the entry into the reference class and the thing that makes the next project's estimate better.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a project management tool.** It is a risk view for people who already have delivery tooling.
- **Not a replacement for the status report.** It is the axis the status report does not carry.
- **Not a judgement on project managers.** The gap exists because delivery competes with risk for the same attention.
- **Not a benchmark against other organisations.** The reference class being built is the organisation's own.
- **Not a claim that the net score is easy to compute.** It is easy to state and hard to quantify, which is the open question.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| A net risk score versus quantifying it | The concept is clear and the number is not, and a score that cannot be defended will be argued with rather than acted on |
| Re-testing the business case versus who wants it re-tested | The people best placed to notice erosion are the people most committed to the project, which is exactly the lock-in the research describes |
| The project manager as ally versus the project manager's workload | The pitch is that this is already their job, and in practice it will still feel like another system unless it replaces something |
| Building a reference class versus the time it takes | The compounding argument is real and the payoff arrives after a dozen projects, which is beyond most attention spans and most budget cycles |
| Registers on two axes versus one truth | A risk appearing in a role register and a project register must be the same risk, or the model has quietly forked |
| Recording what was actually delivered versus who wants that recorded | An honest closure record is the most valuable artefact here and the one with the most organisational resistance behind it |
