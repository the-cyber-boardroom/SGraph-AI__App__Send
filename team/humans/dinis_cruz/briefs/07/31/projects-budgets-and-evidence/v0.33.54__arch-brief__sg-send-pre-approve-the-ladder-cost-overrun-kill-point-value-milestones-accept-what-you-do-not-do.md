# Pre-Approve The Ladder: Accepting The Cost Overrun Before It Happens, Naming The Point Where The Case Dies, And Accepting What You Will Not Do Instead

**version** v0.33.54
**date** 31 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Engineering, Board, Commercial

**type** Architecture brief

*Twenty-fourth of 31 July. A workflow the graph makes possible. Research on escalation and precommitment is grounded and cited, including a study that tests this memo's proposal directly. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A workflow for accepting a class of risk the register has not previously handled, which is the risk of a future state rather than a present fact: **the case is cost overrun, chosen because cost projections on large projects are systematically optimistic and because, as the memo puts it, budgets are frequently constructed to be approvable rather than to be accurate; the proposal is that at the moment a project is approved at one figure, the revisions that will follow are approved too, so that a million-pound project carries pre-approved positions at one and a half, at two, at five, up to a stated point beyond which the business case no longer exists and the project stops, with the stopping point named while it is still cheap to name; alongside the cost ladder the value side is pre-committed, meaning the return expected by a given date even if delivery is incomplete, and alongside both the sponsor states the probability they attach to each rung, which costs nothing at the time and accumulates into a calibration record; the reason all of this must happen before any money is spent is established rather than intuitive, because the foundational experiments on escalation found that people personally responsible for an initial failing decision committed substantially more additional resources than people who merely inherited it, which means the person who approved the first million is, on the evidence, the worst available decider on the second; the standard organisational remedies are external oversight and divided responsibility, which the same literature notes are expensive and carry their own costs, whereas this memo's proposal is the cheap alternative and has been tested directly, since a recent programme of preregistered experiments involving over seven and a half thousand participants found that a public pledge to change course conditional on a concrete future state made decision makers who subsequently did change course be seen as more trustworthy rather than less, which addresses the real reason projects are not stopped; and the memo adds a genuinely novel acceptance type, that the things not done because this project was funded should themselves be accepted, with an owner, so that opportunity cost stops being invisible.** It is the twenty-fourth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.54 project-manager brief, the v0.33.54 register-maintains-itself brief, the v0.33.49 interval-ladder brief, the v0.33.54 villagers brief, and the v0.33.35 digital-twins brief). New contributions: **the pre-approved escalation ladder with a named kill point, value milestones committed alongside cost, probability statements accumulating into a calibration record, the research finding that the original approver is the worst decider on escalation, precommitment shown to protect rather than damage the reputation of whoever stops a project, and opportunity cost as a first-class acceptance.**

## The Budget Designed To Be Approved

The memo starts with an observation about what a budget actually is, and it is worth quoting because it is more honest than most planning documentation. The project lead: **"cost projections tend to be very skewed, especially on bigger projects, and they tend to be more defined in terms of let's create a believable budget that will be approved, versus let's create a budget that actually is a good representation of reality."**

That distinction is the whole problem. A number produced to clear an approval gate is optimised for approval, not for accuracy, and everybody in the room knows it. The corpus grounded the mechanism earlier today: the pattern of underestimating cost, time and risk while overestimating benefit is documented as a general phenomenon, driven by optimism bias, by the belief that this project is different, and by deliberate understatement to secure a decision.

The memo's response is not to demand better numbers. It is to change what gets approved.

## Pre-Approve The Ladder

The proposal. The project lead: **"when you approve a particular risk, for example the risk of cost overruns, you should already pre-approve the next set of budget costs."** With the shape. The project lead: **"if a project costs a million, when you approve that you should also approve what happens when the cost in six months is revised to 1.5 million, and then 2 million, and then 5 million."**

So the artefact produced at approval is not a number. It is a schedule.

```
   APPROVED TODAY
   1.0m   the project as proposed          probability stated
   1.5m   pre-approved, no new decision    probability stated
   2.0m   pre-approved, no new decision    probability stated
   3.0m   pre-approved, requires the board probability stated
   ----------------------------------------------------------
   5.0m   NOT approved. The case does not survive this.
          At this point the project stops.
```

The lower rungs remove the ritual of returning for approval when a revision was foreseeable. The top rung is the important one. The project lead: **"you should approve as many as the ones that you're comfortable with, and as many as the ones that you then can say if this goes below a certain amount, the business case is just not there."**

And the reasoning is a value judgement rather than a budget one. The project lead: **"if this project costs a million and is delivered within six months, then it makes sense; but if this project costs 5 million and is delivered over five years, then it doesn't make business sense, because we might as well invest somewhere else."**

## And The Value Side

Cost is only half of it, and the memo insists on the other half. The project lead: **"you should also approve the milestones for value provided, so you should commit to saying we expect this project to produce this value, this return on investment, even if it's not complete, but at least some return, by X."**

That is the more demanding commitment and the more useful one. A cost ladder alone can be satisfied by spending less; a value ladder requires the project to be delivering something by a stated date. Together they express the actual proposition, which is a ratio rather than either number alone, and they make erosion detectable, since a project can stay inside its cost ladder while its value milestones slip until the case is gone.

## And The Probabilities

A small addition with a large downstream consequence. The project lead: **"you should also commit on the percentage that you think that will happen."** With the reason. The project lead: **"this goes to the point of starting to create a good body of evidence and body of track record."**

Stating a probability at approval costs nothing and creates something no organisation currently has. After twenty projects, the record shows whether a sponsor who said there was a ten percent chance of reaching two million was right. That is the calibration record the corpus identified this morning in the downgrading brief and this afternoon in the project manager brief, arriving here from a third direction, which suggests it is a real primitive rather than a recurring idea.

It is also the mechanism that makes the reference-class argument work. Recorded predictions plus recorded outcomes is exactly the dataset that the only proven debiasing method for project forecasting requires and almost nobody has.

## Why It Must Happen Before

The memo asserts this and the research establishes it, more strongly than the memo claims.

The foundational work on escalation, published in 1976 under a memorable title about being knee-deep in a big muddy, placed people in managerial simulations where earlier investments had been rigged to fail. The finding was consistent: **people who had been personally responsible for the initial failing decision committed substantially more additional resources than those who merely inherited the decision from someone else.** Personal responsibility and the need for self-justification were identified as the primary drivers, distinct from organisational inertia or economics.

Later work describes the mechanism as recommitment aimed at erasing losses and vindicating the earlier decision, and observes that decision makers cognitively distort the negative consequences of a prior choice rather than change behaviour, so that the original decision continues to appear rational.

The implication for this workflow is sharp and worth stating plainly: **the person who approved the first million is, on the evidence, the worst available decider on the second.** They are not weak or foolish; they are exhibiting the most reliably reproduced effect in this literature.

The memo describes the experience exactly. The project lead: **"there is this push to make a bad decision, which is, we already spent a million, we don't want to waste that million, so let's spend another half a million, and then, we already spent a million and a half, let's spend another one, and it keeps going."** And the conclusion. The project lead: **"it's much harder to stop a project than it is to start it."**

## The Standard Remedy Is Expensive; This One Is Not

The literature's usual prescriptions are external oversight and divided responsibility, deliberately separating whoever decides the escalation from whoever made the original commitment. The same literature notes that these remedies require substantial resources and carry adverse effects of their own, which is why organisations rarely sustain them.

Pre-approval achieves something similar at almost no cost. The person deciding the ladder has not yet spent anything, has no prior decision to vindicate, and no losses to erase. They are, for that one moment, in the position of the inheriting manager rather than the responsible one, which is precisely the condition the research found produces better judgement. **The decision is moved to the only point at which the decider is unbiased about it.**

## The Finding That Answers The Real Objection

There is a recent study that tests this memo's proposal directly, and it addresses the reason projects are not stopped in practice.

Across four primary and five supplemental preregistered experiments with a combined sample of more than seven and a half thousand participants, researchers examined **precommitment, defined as a public pledge to change course conditional on a concrete future state of the world.** The finding was that where such a precommitment had been made, observers judged decision makers who subsequently de-escalated as **more trustworthy** than where it had not.

That matters because the barrier to stopping a project is rarely analytical. Everyone in the room can usually see the case has gone. What stops them is that terminating looks like admitting the original decision was wrong, and the person who must say so is the person it reflects on.

**Precommitment converts stopping from an admission into the execution of a plan.** The sponsor who halts at the pre-declared ceiling is not conceding error; they are doing exactly what they said they would do, and the evidence suggests they are seen better for it. That reframes the whole proposal: it is not only a decision device, it is a reputational instrument, and the second may matter more than the first.

It also connects to the mechanism established this morning, since separate experiments found that accountability applied before a decision reduces the sunk cost effect, with the effect attributed to decision makers expecting to be evaluated more favourably for ignoring sunk costs. Pre-approval is predecisional accountability applied to money.

## Accept What You Do Not Do

The genuinely novel contribution, and it belongs in the model as a new acceptance type. The project lead: **"you should also accept the things that you don't do while you're funding this."**

Opportunity cost is universally acknowledged and never recorded. A funding decision is documented as what was approved; what was displaced leaves no trace, so nobody owns it, nobody reviews it, and it never appears when the programme is assessed. Making it an acceptance changes that: a named person accepts that these specific things will not happen because this was funded, with a date and an interval, which means the displaced work is visible and returns for review when the interval expires.

The memo's own example is the one this corpus is best placed to appreciate. The project lead: **"instead of improving the existing solution, improving software, improving testing, improving documentation, working on all the non-functional requirements that exist on a particular project, you decide to fund a complete new project on top of the previous one."** With the outcome. The project lead: **"a year later, many years later, the previous project is still there, the new project hasn't delivered expectations, and you spent a huge amount of money and time and effort."**

That is precisely the market described in the villagers brief earlier today. The non-functional requirements are unglamorous, they are what determines whether the existing thing survives, and they lose every funding round to a new project with a better story. Recording the displacement as an acceptance is the first mechanism that would make that trade visible at the moment it is made.

The memo also names the resource nobody counts. The project lead: **"all these things cost focus; there's things you don't do."**

## The Same Discipline At Every Rung

An important refinement, because the natural failure is to pre-approve once and then abandon the practice. The project lead: **"even when you go, okay, we spent a million, let's put another million in, you should already approve the next million and the next five and the next ten, so that you've thought it through."**

So the ladder is regenerated at each escalation rather than consumed. That keeps the decision predecisional at every stage, which is exactly what the research says is required, and it prevents the workflow from degrading into a single planning exercise that is out of date within a year.

The memo's summary of the intent is the right test for the design. The project lead: **"we want to make the cost of making the decision to be very explicit, and we don't want to push those decisions to the future."**

## What The Graph Makes Possible

The memo closes by noting why this is now buildable. The project lead: **"this is a good example of, because we can simulate this and we can even do this with digital twins, our infrastructure would actually allow this type of decision making."**

The pieces already exist. A twin represents a project and its dependencies. Pre-approved rungs are acceptances with owners and intervals, which the register already models. The value milestones are facts with dates that can be checked against reality. The probabilities are properties that later become evidence. And the displaced work is another acceptance, connected to the funded one by an edge that makes the trade queryable.

What simulation adds is the ability to ask the question before committing, which the memo identifies as the cheapest moment. The project lead: **"can you do more planning to understand what will happen in these next four or five scenarios, because that's cheaper to do before you commit to the project."**

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a prediction of the overrun.** It is a decision about what to do at each level, taken while the decider is unbiased.
- **Not an argument against expensive projects.** A large return can justify a large ladder; the memo says so explicitly.
- **Not a gate.** The lower rungs remove approval ritual rather than adding it.
- **Not a substitute for stopping.** It is what makes stopping executable rather than an admission of failure.
- **Not only about cost.** Value milestones and displaced work are part of the same acceptance.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Pre-approving five million versus anchoring | Naming an upper rung may make it feel permitted, and a ladder can become a licence rather than a limit |
| Naming a kill point versus honouring it | The pledge is made by people who may not be present when it triggers, and the research on trust assumes the pledge was public |
| Optimistic sponsors versus honest ladders | Whoever constructs a believable budget will construct a believable ladder, and the same incentives apply one level up |
| Probability statements versus their use | Calibration data is valuable for learning and becomes a weapon if used to punish, which is the same tension recorded twice already today |
| Accepting what you do not do versus enumerating it | The displaced set is potentially unbounded, and deciding which non-decisions are worth recording is unresolved |
| Predecisional advantage versus regeneration | Regenerating the ladder at each escalation is correct and is done by someone who is now personally committed, which reintroduces the bias the design avoids |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| How many rungs, and how spaced? | Doubling, or tied to decision thresholds, or to the altitude at which each requires acceptance |
| Who accepts each rung? | Higher rungs plausibly need higher altitude, which makes the ladder a pre-agreed escalation path as well as a budget |
| How is the kill point protected? | Whether a pre-declared ceiling can be overridden, by whom, and whether the override is itself an acceptance |
| Which displaced work is recorded? | The bounding rule for opportunity-cost acceptances, since everything not done is too large a set |
| How are value milestones verified? | Somebody has to check whether the promised return appeared, and that check is the part organisations skip |
| Does regeneration reintroduce the bias? | The person extending the ladder is now committed, which may argue for a different accepting role at that point |
| What does the simulation actually produce? | The four or five scenarios the memo wants costed before commitment, and what makes them credible |
