## What This Is

The specification for the agent implementing the canonical AI Act, built around one architectural decision that turns the hardest problem into the most valuable feature: **the base unit is the paragraph rather than the article, which is not a new choice but the position the corpus settled in February when it argued that a document is not one blob but a collection of paragraphs, clauses, amendments and signatures, each individually versionable and verifiable, with the document itself behaving as a data room in which each paragraph is a file; every paragraph therefore carries its own graph of links, meaning, analysis, facts and interconnections, and because provisions reference each other it must be possible to start anywhere and navigate outward, which makes each paragraph a universe in itself and the whole instrument a graph of graphs of graphs; the decision that matters most concerns how the amendment is applied, because the instinct in the memo is exactly right and its consequences are larger than it suggests, since the operations needed to turn the original Act into the current one, substitute this wording, delete those paragraphs, insert this article, are the same operations needed to turn the full instrument into the version that applies to a particular organisation, which means consolidation and customisation are one mechanism rather than two, and the flagship feature and the maintenance burden share an engine; from which follows the recommendation that the merged text should not be stored at all, and that the base text plus the amendment instructions should be held as data with the consolidated version computed as a projection, which makes the result reproducible, auditable, point-in-time queryable, and honest in a way a hand-merged file cannot be, since even the official consolidated texts carry a warning that they are documentary rather than authentic; a deleted paragraph must remain in the graph marked as repealed from a date rather than removed, because losing it destroys both the audit trail and the ability to answer what the provision used to say; and the payoff is the vision the memo describes, that a reader can stand on any provision and see the entire instrument from that vantage point.** It is the second document of 31 July (cross-ref: the companion research brief of today, the v0.6.14 fractal document signing brief, the v0.33.53 every-paragraph-is-a-graph brief, the v0.33.53 customised-standard brief, and the v0.31.9 vault-per-standard brief). New contributions: **amendment established as a first-class graph operation rather than a migration, the identification that consolidation and organisational customisation are the same mechanism, the recommendation to compute rather than store the consolidated text, the rule that repealed provisions are marked rather than deleted, and the resolution of the file-per-article question against the corpus's own prior position.**

## The Prior Work Answers The Scalability Question

The memo ends on an open question about granularity. The project lead: **"let's think about the scalability, maybe you have a file per article, or a graph per article, let's have a bit of research on the best way to do this."** And it points at where the answer might be. The project lead: **"look at the librarian on the project, because I'm sure that we've written before about the idea that for documents like this, we sometimes need to go all the way to the paragraph."**

It has. In February the corpus set out an architecture for treating a single document as a portable data room, on the reasoning that a contract is not one blob but a collection of paragraphs, clauses, amendments and signatures, each of which can be individually signed, versioned and verified, with the conclusion stated directly: the document is a data room in which **each paragraph is a file**. The same brief named the structure as graphs of graphs of graphs, and its motivating problem was that conventional revision tracking cannot prove who changed what.

So the answer to file-per-article or graph-per-article is neither. **Paragraph per file**, which the corpus chose eighteen months of work ago for exactly this class of document, and which has a specific payoff here that the February brief could not have anticipated: when the unit of storage matches the unit of amendment, the amendment shows up as a diff. Nobody else can display the change to Article 10 as a legible difference, because nobody else stores the Act at the granularity at which it was amended.

## Every Paragraph Is A Universe

The modelling ambition is stated clearly and should be preserved. The project lead: **"almost every paragraph that we have in here should fundamentally have a graph of its own; the paragraph should fundamentally have links, should have meaning, should have analysis, should have facts, should have interconnections, what does he mean, what's the connection, what's the point, how everything fits together."**

And the navigation requirement follows from the nature of legal text. The project lead: **"we should be able to look at each of those paragraphs as a graph, and even navigate out of that paragraph, especially because there's paragraphs who reference other things."** Cross-reference is not incidental in an instrument like this; Article 9 refers to paragraph 2 point (d), Article 10 refers to provisions of three other regulations, and the annexes are load-bearing. Those references are edges, and they are what make the corpus of provisions a graph rather than a list.

The project lead's summary of the ambition is the right test for the build. The project lead: **"the paragraph is almost this universe in itself."**

## Amendment Is A Native Operation, Not A Migration

This is the most consequential sentence in the memo and its implications reach further than the AI Act. The project lead: **"the adjustment that we have here is actually something that should be native to these graphs, because when we bring this into an organisation, we probably need to do similar things; we need to remove a bunch of paragraphs, we need to amend other paragraphs, so we need to make it in a way that is compatible and aligned."**

Consider what those two jobs actually require.

**Consolidation** takes the original Act and applies the amending regulation: substitute this wording, delete these paragraphs, insert this article, with each change effective from a stated date.

**Customisation**, the flagship feature specified on 28 July, takes the full instrument and produces the version relevant to a particular organisation: these provisions do not apply, these are in scope, this one applies only from a later date.

**These are the same operation.** Both take a base instrument and a set of instructions and produce a projection. If the graph models amendment natively, then the mechanism that keeps the Act current is the mechanism that customises it per customer, and the corpus does not need two engines. That is a genuine simplification of the roadmap, and it means the maintenance burden and the differentiating feature are the same investment.

```
   BASE INSTRUMENT (paragraph per file, stable identifiers)
        |
        +-- amendment operations from Regulation 2026/1744  --> the current law
        |
        +-- organisational scoping operations               --> the customised standard
        |
        +-- both together                                   --> what applies to you, today
                                        one engine, three products
```

## Therefore: Do Not Store The Merged Text

The recommendation that follows is specific and worth arguing for, because the obvious approach is to produce a merged document and keep it.

**Hold the base text and the amendment instructions as data, and compute the consolidated version as a projection.**

The memo's phrasing leans the other way. The project lead: **"if you take the original, the canonical version, because we really need to merge it."** The merge is right as an output and wrong as a stored artefact, for five reasons.

**Reproducibility.** A computed projection can be re-derived and checked. A merged file is somebody's assertion about what the merge produced, and after two amendments nobody can verify it without redoing the work.

**Point-in-time queries come free.** Because provisions take effect on at least four different dates, the question is not what the Act says but what it says on a given date. A projection parameterised by date answers that; a merged file answers it for one date only.

**Audit and provenance.** Every clause in the output traces to either the original or a specific instruction in the amending instrument. That is the force-of-proof property the corpus argues for everywhere, applied to the text itself.

**Honesty.** Official consolidated texts carry a standing warning that they are of documentary value only and are not authentic. A computed projection that shows its inputs is more defensible than a merged blob that looks authoritative and is not, and it lets the artefact say exactly what it is.

**It is the same engine as the product.** Per the section above.

## Repealed Is Not Deleted

A rule the schema must enforce from the start, because it is expensive to retrofit. A paragraph removed by an amendment stays in the graph, marked as repealed with effect from a date. It is not deleted from the store.

Two things break if it is. The audit trail disappears, so nothing can show that a provision existed and was removed, which is precisely the information a reader with an older copy needs. And point-in-time queries stop working, since answering what Article 10 required in June requires the June text to still exist.

The same applies to substituted wording: the superseded text is retained and superseded, not overwritten.

## The Schema, Sketched

Building on the types proposed on 28 July, with amendment added. All of this is proposed rather than settled, per the corpus rule that new node and edge types are declared in prose.

**Provision** remains the base node: an article, paragraph or point, with a stable identifier that survives amendment, its source text, and its position in the hierarchy.

**Amendment** is the new node type and the important one: an instruction with an operation of substitute, insert or delete, a source instrument, a target provision, replacement content where applicable, and an effective date.

Alongside them the types already proposed: Definition, Requirement, Obligation, Prohibition, Control, Actor.

New edges: `amends`, `substitutes`, `inserts`, `deletes`, `repealed_by`, `effective_from`, and `references` for the provision-to-provision links that make navigation possible. Established edges should be preferred wherever they fit, and the set should be exhausted before any of these is adopted.

The identifier question deserves attention early, because everything depends on it. Provisions need identifiers that survive amendment, so that a deletion has something to point at and a substitution has something to attach to. The identifier and legal-XML work surveyed on 28 July is the place to start rather than inventing a scheme.

## The View From Any Provision

The payoff is the reading experience the memo describes. The project lead: **"I should be able to start in any paragraph and map the entire graph that is related to that paragraph, and then view just the standard from that point of view."** With a worked case. The project lead: **"an article, I think it's nine, that has the risk framework; I should be able to then just see the entire standard just from the point of view of that article."**

This is an established primitive rather than a new capability. The corpus already specifies a wide first pass that captures the universe around a subject, with queries layered on top that simplify it so that a reader starting at one point sees only what is relevant to them. Applied here, the graph holds the whole instrument and the view is computed from wherever the reader is standing.

Article 9 is a good first target for exactly the reason the memo gives, since it is where the accepted-and-acceptable distinction was found, so the view from Article 9 is immediately useful rather than a demonstration.

## Connecting Outward

The memo notes that others are producing mappings. The project lead: **"other projects are doing mappings against this, the OWASP projects, and so on, so we should basically be connecting to this."** Those crosswalks are edges from external frameworks into provisions of the instrument, and they are cheap to add once provisions have stable identifiers. They are also the most likely source of inbound interest in the artefact, since a project that has mapped its controls to the Act has a reason to link to a graph that renders those provisions properly.

## Start Small

The memo says so and the constraint should be honoured. The project lead: **"let's start small, but let's figure out the best way from a technology point of view, from a schema point of view."**

A defensible first slice: one article modelled completely at paragraph granularity, with its cross-references, its amendment operations applied as a projection, and the view-from-here query working. Article 9 or Article 10 are both good candidates, the first because it is already in use by the corpus and the second because it is the one the amendment visibly changed, which makes it the demonstration of the whole thesis.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not an article-level model.** The paragraph is the unit, per the corpus position from February.
- **Not a merged document.** The consolidation is computed and shown with its inputs, not stored as a blob.
- **Not authoritative.** The projection is derived, and must say so wherever it appears.
- **Not a one-off migration.** Amendment is a standing operation the graph performs, not a task that completes.
- **Not a full import.** Start with one article modelled properly rather than the whole instrument modelled thinly.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Paragraph as file versus file count | The granularity that makes amendment legible produces a large number of very small files, and the tooling has to make that navigable rather than merely correct |
| Computed projection versus what people want to read | Readers want a document, and the answer has to render like one while remaining derived, which is more engineering than storing text |
| Consolidation and customisation as one engine versus different audiences | The mechanism is shared and the correctness bar is not, since getting the law wrong is a different order of problem from scoping a customer's view wrongly |
| Stable identifiers versus the instrument's own numbering | Legal numbering is not stable under amendment, and an internal identifier scheme risks diverging from how everyone else refers to provisions |
| Retaining repealed text versus clarity | Keeping everything is right for audit and makes the default view harder to get right, since a reader must never accidentally read a repealed provision as current |
| Start small versus the 2 August date | A single article modelled properly is the right first step and arrives after the date on which the transparency obligations begin to apply |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What is the identifier scheme? | Must survive amendment, and should build on the existing legal identifier work rather than being invented |
| Is the amending instrument available as structured data? | If the instructions are machine-readable, the projection is computable rather than transcribed |
| How is the projection rendered as a document? | Readers want continuous text, and the derivation has to remain visible without ruining the reading |
| How are the four application dates modelled? | The projection is parameterised by date, and provisions differ in when they bite |
| Which article is the first slice? | Article 10 demonstrates the amendment thesis; Article 9 is already in corpus use |
| How do external crosswalks attach? | The edge type and the provenance for a mapping produced by another project |
| Where does organisational customisation diverge from amendment? | They share an engine, and the differences that remain need naming before they are discovered |
