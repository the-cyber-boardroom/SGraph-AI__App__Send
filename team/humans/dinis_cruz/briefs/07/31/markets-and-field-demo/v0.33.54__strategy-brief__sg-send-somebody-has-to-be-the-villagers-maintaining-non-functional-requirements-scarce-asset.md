# Somebody Has To Be The Villagers: Maintaining What The Explorers Built, And Why The Displaced Firms Hold The Scarce Asset

**version** v0.33.54
**date** 31 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Commercial, Partners

**type** Strategy brief

*Seventeenth of 31 July, the first of two markets the memo asks to be split apart. Market data is grounded and cited, with source quality noted. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A business for the software services firms whose traditional work is disappearing, built on the gap between building an application and being able to keep one running: **the customer is a team, department or professional who has built something with generative tooling, whose application now matters to other people, and who has neither the skill nor the inclination to maintain it, because their expertise is the domain rather than the engineering; what they are missing is precisely what the discipline calls non-functional requirements, version control, reliability, resilience, security, backups, consistency, explainability and documentation, none of which the building process produces and all of which become necessary at the moment the thing stops being a personal tool; the commercial trigger is specific and the memo names it exactly, that willingness to pay appears when a system becomes mission critical and more people need to use it, and industry analysis puts a number on when that reckoning tends to arrive, at roughly ninety days after first shipping; the market data is unusually emphatic, with reported technical debt increases of thirty to forty-one percent following adoption of these tools, refactoring activity falling by more than half over three years, roughly half of generated code reported to contain security vulnerabilities, credential exposure roughly doubling, and a fifth of strategic acquirers reported to have walked away from transactions over anticipated risk in the target, which turns this from operational hygiene into asset-value protection; the buyer cannot assess the work, which is both the opportunity and the hazard, since limited technical knowledge is why they need the service and also why they cannot tell a good provider from a bad one; and the reason displaced software firms specifically are well placed is that the scarce asset in this market is the ability to read and repair code somebody else wrote, a skill built by years of maintenance work, which is exactly what these firms have and exactly what the industry is currently failing to produce in anyone new.** It is the seventeenth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.49 consulting-model brief, the companion mass-customisation brief of today, the v0.33.53 scaling-collaboration brief, the v0.33.49 commercialising-the-primitives brief, and the v0.33.50 freelance-next-steps brief). New contributions: **the non-functional requirements named as the sellable unit, the mission-critical transition identified as the commercial trigger with a reported timeframe, the observation that the buyer's inability to assess the work is both the market and its main hazard, the identification of code-reading as the scarce asset the displaced firms already hold, and the acquisition-value framing.**

## Who This Is For

The memo is specific about the supplier rather than only the customer, and that framing is what makes it a business plan rather than an observation. The project lead: **"this is specifically by the companies that used to provide software development services, that used to provide integration services, programming services, to companies who are seeing their businesses dry up."**

That is a real and growing population. Firms built on supplying development capacity are watching the demand for that capacity change shape, and the obvious response, to do the same work faster with better tools, competes directly with the customer's ability to do it themselves. The proposal here is different: stop selling the building and start selling everything that has to be true after the building.

## The Non-Functional Requirements Are The Product

The framing is exact and it is the most useful thing in the memo. The project lead: **"the sweet spot is to find the economic value where it's cheaper for these companies to pay a third party to maintain what I call the non-functional requirements, which fundamentally is the whole version control, reliability, resilience, security, backups, consistency, explainability, and documentation."**

Non-functional requirement is precisely the right term, because it names what the generative process does and does not produce. The functional requirement is met: the application does the thing. Everything that determines whether it will still do the thing next month, under load, after a change, in somebody else's hands, is absent, and its absence is invisible to the person who built it because the application works.

```
   WHAT THE BUILDER GOT          WHAT NOBODY GAVE THEM
   it works                       version control
   it does the domain thing       reliability and resilience
   it was fast                    security
   it is theirs                   backups
                                  consistency
                                  explainability
                                  documentation
                                  -> none of it visible while it still works
```

And the reason they will not do it themselves is not laziness. The project lead: **"none of the people who are doing it know how, and they don't want to, because they are focusing on the domain."** That is the correct division of labour rather than a failing. A clinician who built a scheduling tool should be doing clinical work.

## The Trigger Is Mission Critical, And It Has A Timeframe

The commercial insight is knowing when the willingness to pay appears, and the memo locates it precisely. The project lead: **"once a system becomes mission critical and more people need to use it, you now need to have a way to maintain it."**

Before that moment, nobody buys. A personal tool that breaks is an inconvenience its author fixes or abandons. The transition happens when other people depend on it, when it holds data that matters, or when its failure would stop something. That transition is usually unnoticed at the time and obvious in retrospect.

Industry analysis puts a rough clock on it. Reporting in 2026 describes a pattern arriving around ninety days after a team first ships something built this way, at which point somebody opens a function to fix a small bug and finds three hundred lines handling validation, database writes, notifications and business logic referencing a constant defined five files away, with no explanation and a commit message that says nothing. The same analysis reports teams spending twenty to thirty percent of sprint capacity on defects traceable to generated code by that point.

Ninety days is therefore a usable sales signal. A firm targeting this market should be talking to people about three months after they shipped, not before.

## The Data

The evidence is strong on direction and should be read with care about provenance, since much of what circulates is vendor commentary recycling a smaller set of underlying studies. Attributing to the underlying work where possible:

- Analysis of 8.1 million pull requests is reported to show technical debt increasing by thirty to forty-one percent following adoption of these tools.
- Code-analysis work reports duplication rising several-fold, and refactoring falling from around a quarter of changed lines in 2021 to under a tenth by 2024.
- A study of roughly eight hundred developers reported a forty-one percent increase in bug rates after adoption, with initial speed gains offset by defects.
- Roughly half of generated code is reported across several studies to contain security vulnerabilities, concentrated in injection flaws, hardcoded credentials, missing authorisation and information exposure.
- Security research reports assisted commits leaking secrets at roughly twice the baseline rate.
- Forecasts suggest three-quarters of technology decision-makers will face moderate to severe technical debt by the end of 2026, with a majority of chief information officers reporting debt actively growing.
- Adoption is near-universal while trust is not, with reported usage above eighty percent and trust in accuracy falling from forty-three to twenty-nine percent over eighteen months.

The single most commercially useful figure is different in kind. A 2026 mergers and acquisitions report is cited as finding that **one in five strategic dealmakers walked away from a transaction** because of anticipated risk in the target, with codebases built this way at risk of being classed as unauditable and devaluing the asset.

That reframes the offer. Maintaining the non-functional requirements is not tidying up; it is protecting the value of the thing when somebody comes to buy it, and that is a conversation with a chief executive rather than with a developer.

## The Buyer Cannot Assess The Work

The memo identifies this plainly. The project lead: **"although they vibe code, their knowledge of technology is limited, their knowledge of what's actually going on is limited."**

This is the market and it is also its principal hazard. A buyer who cannot evaluate the work cannot distinguish a firm that genuinely hardened their system from one that produced a report saying so. Three consequences follow for anyone entering it.

The service must be sold and demonstrated in outcomes the buyer can verify without technical knowledge: it recovered from a simulated failure, the change was made without breaking anything, somebody new understood it. Trust and referral will dominate the sales process, because there is no product specification the buyer can read. And the market is vulnerable to poor providers, which over time is a reason for the corpus's assessment and evidence work to attach here.

## The Scarce Asset Is Reading Code You Did Not Write

This is the argument that makes the displaced firms specifically well placed, rather than merely available.

The skill this work requires is unglamorous and specific: the ability to open unfamiliar code, work out what it does, find where it is wrong, and change it without breaking three other things. It is built almost entirely by doing it, usually over years, usually early in a career, on systems somebody else wrote.

Reporting on the maintenance problem makes the observation that the engineers who will be needed to maintain these systems are precisely the people who would otherwise have spent this period as juniors reading and modifying code they did not author, and that this pipeline is being disrupted at exactly the moment the maintenance need is growing. The corpus reached adjacent evidence on 28 July, where studies showed review burden shifting from juniors onto senior maintainers.

So the shape of this market is unusual and favourable. **Demand for a specific skill is rising sharply while the mechanism that produces that skill is being dismantled**, and firms with established engineers hold an asset that is appreciating rather than depreciating. That is close to the opposite of the story those firms are currently telling themselves.

## Beyond Maintenance, The Things That Need Engineering

The memo does not stop at keeping things alive. The project lead: **"they should also be providing the next level of features that require good engineering, from billing to new capabilities to visualisations."**

That is the natural expansion and probably the better margin. Some capabilities remain genuinely hard to produce by prompting: taking money reliably, handling identity and permissions, integrating with systems that have their own rules, and doing anything where being wrong has consequences. A firm that has already earned trust by keeping the thing running is the obvious supplier for the next thing that needs building properly.

It also changes the relationship from a cost the customer resents to a capability they return to.

## This Is The Handover, Made Into A Business

The memo names the frame. The project lead: **"fundamentally this is the whole Wardley Maps situation of the explorers, and I'm saying that these are the villagers and the town planners; that's their job."**

The corpus used the same vocabulary on 17 July, describing the consulting model as serving the handover from explorer to villager. This memo makes the sharper move: for a displaced software services firm, **being the villagers is the business**, not a service offered around it. The explorers are now everybody, which is what makes the market large. The project lead: **"this market is going to grow exponentially because every single team, every single department, every single function, even professionals, are going to create tons and tons of apps."**

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not selling development.** The offer begins where the building stops.
- **Not a criticism of the builders.** Focusing on the domain rather than the engineering is correct division of labour.
- **Not an early-stage service.** The trigger is the transition to mission critical, and before it nobody pays.
- **Not assessable by the buyer.** The work has to be demonstrated in outcomes a non-technical person can verify.
- **Not a temporary market.** The skill it depends on is becoming scarcer rather than more common.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| A buyer who cannot assess the work | It is why the market exists and why it will attract providers who cannot do it either, and the customer has no way to tell them apart |
| Maintaining what should not have been built | Some of these applications should be retired rather than hardened, and a firm paid to maintain has no incentive to say so |
| Vendor data versus primary research | The figures circulating are largely recycled from a smaller set of studies through commercial blogs, so the direction is well supported and the precision is not |
| The scarce-asset argument versus how long it lasts | Tooling for reading and repairing unfamiliar code is improving quickly, and the advantage may be a window rather than a moat |
| Selling to the domain expert versus who signs | The person who built it is rarely the person with a budget, and the mission-critical trigger is exactly when somebody else starts caring |
| Ninety days as a signal versus a generalisation | It is a useful heuristic from industry commentary rather than a measured constant, and it will vary enormously |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What is the smallest sellable engagement? | The first thing a firm does that a non-technical buyer can see was worth paying for |
| How is quality demonstrated to a non-technical buyer? | Recovery from a simulated failure is a candidate, and the demonstration matters more than the report |
| What is the pricing shape? | Retainer, per-application, or per-incident, given the customer cannot size the work either |
| Where does the acquisition-value argument land? | It is the strongest commercial hook and it reaches a different buyer than the operational one |
