## What This Is

The second business for the same displaced software firms, aimed at a customer the industry has never served properly: **the buyer is an organisation that has never really had technology, whose only previous contact with it was the kind of large expensive system they could not afford and could not have absorbed, and who has therefore run the business on spreadsheets and on the knowledge in somebody's head; what has changed is that the cost of building something specific has collapsed, so a firm with real engineers can assemble a customised tool on open foundations, on existing components, on small utilities they already have, and sell it at a price that would previously have been absurd; the product range the memo lists is deliberately mundane and that is the point, covering customer records, version control, websites, stock management, distribution, visualisation and similar, and the honest description of what is being replaced is not a competitor's product but the spreadsheet the organisation already runs on, which is the incumbent in almost every one of these accounts; the two requirements the memo attaches are the whole specification, that it be reliable and that it be efficient, because a customer with no technical capability cannot recover from a system that misbehaves and has no appetite for one that makes their day longer; the same firms are well placed for the same reason as the first market, since they employ people who can build something that stays built, and the difference is only which end of the market they point at; and one item on the memo's own list needs separating from the rest, because face recognition is a regulated category under the instrument this corpus has spent the week studying, so selling it to an unsophisticated buyer would put both parties inside a regime neither understands, which is a distinction the seller has to make rather than the buyer.** It is the eighteenth document of 31 July (cross-ref: the companion villagers brief of today, the v0.33.49 open-source-as-strategy brief, the v0.33.54 in-scope-versus-in-breach brief, the v0.33.49 consulting-model brief, and the v0.33.50 tools-to-productise brief). New contributions: **the un-technologised customer named as a distinct market for the same displaced firms, the spreadsheet identified as the actual incumbent, reliability and efficiency stated as the entire specification for this buyer, and the observation that some items on the proposed product list are regulated categories requiring the seller to carry the compliance knowledge.**

## The Customer Who Never Had Software

The memo describes a buyer the industry has mostly written off. The project lead: **"customers who never really had technology, whose only interface with technology were these massive super expensive things."**

That is an accurate description of a very large population: small manufacturers, distributors, clinics, professional practices, agricultural businesses, local operators of every kind. They were never served because the economics did not work. A bespoke system cost more than the business could justify, and the packaged alternative was built for organisations ten times their size, requiring configuration they could not perform and processes they did not have.

So they run on spreadsheets, on paper, on email, and on the fact that one person knows how everything works.

## What Changed

The cost of building something specific fell far enough that the arithmetic inverts. The project lead: **"now you can sell them super cheaply, on top of open source technologies, on top of customisations, even on mini tools."**

Three things compound. Open foundations mean the substrate is free rather than licensed. Generative tooling means the bespoke part is hours rather than months. And small existing utilities mean much of what is needed has already been built once. The result is that a genuinely customised tool becomes affordable to a business that could never have commissioned one, which is a new market rather than a cheaper way to serve an old one.

The memo also notes that the firms in question already have what is needed. The project lead: **"those companies which have software developers, have engineers, they did themselves."**

## The Incumbent Is The Spreadsheet

This is the sharpest framing available and the memo lands on it. The project lead: **"basically replacing the spreadsheets that the companies already have."**

Naming the incumbent correctly changes how the thing is sold. The competitor is not another vendor; it is a workbook that has grown over eleven years, that only one person fully understands, that breaks when they are on holiday, and that everybody quietly knows is a single point of failure. The customer is not comparing features. They are weighing a known and tolerated risk against an unknown change.

That has three consequences worth carrying into any sales approach. The spreadsheet works, which means the pitch cannot be that they are doing it wrong. Its weaknesses are already understood by the person who maintains it, which means the entry point is that person's own anxiety rather than a discovery exercise. And a replacement has to be visibly better on the first day, because the spreadsheet's great advantage is that everybody already knows how to use it.

```
   THE INCUMBENT                   WHAT HAS TO BE TRUE TO REPLACE IT
   a spreadsheet, eleven years     reliable enough to trust with the business
   one person understands it       usable without training anyone
   free, familiar, already there   visibly better on day one
   a known single point of failure and cheap enough not to need a business case
```

## Reliable And Efficient Is The Entire Specification

The memo attaches two requirements and they carry more weight than their brevity suggests. The project lead: **"it needs to be reliable and super efficient."**

**Reliable**, because this customer has no recovery capacity. A business with an internal technology function experiences a fault as an incident. A business without one experiences it as a stop, with no one to call and no way to work around it. Reliability is therefore not a quality attribute here; it is the entire value proposition, and it is why the non-functional requirements from the companion brief are as central to this market as to the other one.

**Efficient**, because the only measure this buyer applies is whether the day got easier. A tool that is technically superior and takes longer to use will be abandoned within a fortnight and the spreadsheet will come back. The corpus's own cognitive load argument applies directly: work is judged by the effort it removes, and anything that adds steps loses to the familiar thing regardless of its merits.

## One Item On The List Needs Separating

The memo lists candidate products, and most are unremarkable: customer records, version control, websites, stock management, distribution, visualisation. One is not. The project lead: **"from CRMs to version control, to websites, to stock management, to distribution, to visualisation, to face recognition, to all sorts of tool-specific things."**

Face recognition is a regulated category. Under the instrument the corpus has spent this week mapping, biometric identification sits among the highest-risk uses, with some applications prohibited outright and others carrying substantial obligations, and the penalty tier for prohibited practices is the most severe in the regime. The transparency obligations that begin applying two days from now touch the wider class of systems that interact with people.

The commercial consequence matters more than the legal detail. **A buyer with no technical capability certainly has no regulatory capability**, so they will not know they have entered a regulated activity, and they will assume the supplier would have told them. Selling a biometric tool into that account without raising it is a genuine exposure for both parties and a reputational one for the seller.

Two things follow. The product list should be triaged into the plainly unregulated majority and the small number of items that require the seller to hold compliance knowledge, and the latter should not be sold casually. And this is a real, if modest, connection between the two halves of the corpus: a firm selling small systems into unsophisticated accounts is exactly the kind of organisation that needs the assessment work, and exactly the kind that would never commission it as a separate exercise.

## Why The Same Firms

The argument for the supplier is the same as in the companion brief and worth restating briefly. These firms employ people who can build something that keeps working, which is the scarce capability in both markets. The difference is only which end they point at: one market maintains what somebody else built badly, the other builds properly for somebody who never had anything.

The second has an advantage the first does not. There is no incumbent codebase to inherit, no accumulated debt, and no argument about who broke what. The firm builds it, therefore the firm can maintain it, and the maintenance relationship follows naturally from the build rather than having to be sold separately.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not enterprise software made cheaper.** It is bespoke work made affordable, which is a different product.
- **Not a feature competition.** The incumbent is a spreadsheet, and the comparison is with a familiar working thing.
- **Not a sophisticated buyer.** Reliability and efficiency are the whole specification because nothing else will be assessed.
- **Not a list to sell uniformly.** Some items are regulated categories requiring the seller to carry the knowledge.
- **Not separate from the first market.** The same firms, the same scarce skill, pointed at the other end.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Cheap to build versus cheap to support | Build cost has collapsed and support cost has not, and a low price with an open-ended support obligation is how service firms lose money |
| Bespoke for everyone versus a product | Every account customised is a portfolio of one-offs, which is the model that made this market unservable in the first place |
| Replacing the spreadsheet versus who wants it replaced | The person who maintains it may be its author, and their standing in the organisation often rests on being the one who understands it |
| Reliability promised versus reliability delivered | This customer has no recovery capacity, so the consequences of getting it wrong land harder here than in any enterprise account |
| Regulated items on the list | The buyer will not know and will assume the seller checked, which puts the obligation on the least commercially convenient party |
| Open foundations versus who maintains them | Building on free components transfers a dependency the customer cannot evaluate and the firm must therefore track |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| How is customisation kept from becoming one-offs? | A common core with thin per-customer configuration, or the economics revert to the old model |
| What is the support model and its price? | The part most likely to be underestimated, since this customer cannot self-serve at all |
| Which items are safe to sell without regulatory knowledge? | The triage of the product list, done once, before anyone sells the wrong thing |
| What does the first day look like? | The replacement has to be visibly better immediately, and that is a design problem rather than an engineering one |
| Who is the buyer inside the account? | Often the owner, sometimes the person whose spreadsheet is being replaced, and those are different conversations |
| Does the corpus's assessment work attach here? | A firm selling into unsophisticated accounts carries obligations it may not know it has |
