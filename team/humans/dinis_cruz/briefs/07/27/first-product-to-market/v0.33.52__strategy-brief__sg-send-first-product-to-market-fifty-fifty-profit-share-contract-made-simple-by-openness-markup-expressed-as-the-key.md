# First Product To Market: A Fifty-Fifty Profit Share, A Contract Made Simple By Openness, And A Markup Expressed As The Key

**version** v0.33.52
**date** 27 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Commercial, Legal, Strategy, Partners

**type** Strategy brief

*Second of 27 July. The commercial model for the first SGraph product going to market; the architecture is a companion brief. Legal and financial points are factual and are not legal or financial advice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

The commercial arrangement behind the first SGraph product taken to market, a voice note transcription tool built with a freelance partner: **the deal is a fifty-fifty split of profit rather than revenue, between the project lead's legal entity and the partner's entity, running as a ninety-day initial term from the first of August 2026, with the project lead carrying the costs on the reasoning that they are minimal because the whole thing is statically hosted, and with the small real costs, a payment processor fee and one or two others, passed through before the split; the contract can be very simple, and the reason it can be simple is the most transferable idea here, because everything produced is open source and released under Creative Commons and published openly as it goes, so there is almost nothing to protect and the agreement only has to cover the genuinely sensitive residue, the keys, the passwords, and the financial data, which means openness has produced a legal dividend rather than only a technical one; pricing passes the model cost to the user and adds a margin on top, with a transcription intended to land at or under twenty pence and users either paying as they go or buying credits in amounts large enough to clear the processor's fixed fee; and the margin is implemented rather than invoiced, because instead of building any billing system the operator issues the user an OpenRouter key carrying seventy-five percent of what remains after the payment processor's fee, so the platform meters and deducts the usage while the retained quarter is the margin, which makes the commercial model a property of the key rather than a piece of software anyone has to write.** It is the second brief of 27 July (cross-ref: the companion architecture brief of today, the v0.33.49 commercialising-the-primitives brief, the v0.33.50 freelance-next-steps brief, the v0.33.50 tools-to-productise brief, and the v0.33.49 open-source-as-strategy brief). New contributions: **the fifty-fifty profit share with costs carried and passed through, openness as the thing that collapses the contract surface to keys and passwords and financial data, the markup implemented as the issued key's credit limit rather than as a billing system, and the first live instance of the shared commercialisation kit the portfolio has been waiting on.**

## The Deal

The arrangement is between two companies rather than between a company and an individual. The project lead: **"this is a relationship between the Cyber Boardroom, which is my legal entity, and the other freelancer entity."** The split is even and is explicitly on profit rather than on gross. The project lead: **"what we do is we split fifty-fifty any of the income generated."** Then the correction, which matters because it is the operative term. The project lead: **"of course, I'm saying it's a fifty-fifty of the profit, because there will be a Stripe cost and a couple of other costs, but they are minimal, that we put through it."**

Costs sit with the project lead and are expected to be near-invisible. The project lead: **"I'm actually happy to support any of the costs because the costs are minimal, because this is all hosted using static site generators, so there's very little cost on top of this."** That is the commercial dividend of the no-backend architecture in the companion brief: a product with no servers to run has almost no floor, which is what makes an even split of a small early revenue worth either party's time.

The term is short and starts at a defined date. The memo stated it two ways, thirty days and three months, and that ambiguity has since been resolved. The project lead: **"we start with an initial 30-day contract, let's call it from the 1st of August, so three months."** The settled term is **ninety days beginning 1 August 2026**, running to the end of October. Ninety days is a sensible first commitment: long enough to ship, put the product in front of real users, and see whether anyone pays, and short enough that neither party is trapped if the answer is no. The contract should still state the counting convention and what happens on the ninetieth day by default, whether it lapses or rolls.

## Why The Contract Can Be Simple

This is the part worth extracting and reusing, because it generalises well beyond this product. The contract is short not because the parties are casual but because the operating model has removed most of what contracts normally exist to protect. The project lead: **"it's actually quite simple, and it's a good example of the architecture makes all of this actually very simple, because everything we're doing is open source, and everything that we do is released in the Creative Commons, everything that is done is published on LinkedIn, and there's no private information apart from some keys and passwords, and some financial data, so we map all that out, so it gets very simple there."**

```
   WHAT A CONTRACT NORMALLY PROTECTS      HERE
   ownership of the code            ->    open source, so nothing to allocate
   confidentiality of the work      ->    published as it goes, by design
   IP assignment and licensing      ->    Creative Commons, settled up front
   ----------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT ACTUALLY NEEDS COVERING           keys
                                          passwords
                                          financial data
                                          the split, the term, the exit
```

The 17 July thesis argued that open source is self-interested strategy rather than charity, and located the benefits in simplification, deployment reach, and where lock-in sits. This adds a benefit that brief did not name: openness shrinks the legal surface. When there is no secret to keep and no ownership to divide, the agreement collapses to the split, the term, and the handling of a short list of genuinely sensitive items. That is faster to draft, cheaper to review, and much easier for a partner to accept, which matters when the whole engagement model depends on partners saying yes quickly.

## Pricing: Pass The Cost, Add A Margin

The pricing intent is that the user pays close to what the work actually costs. The project lead: **"it's also about we pass the cost to the user, so the user basically just pays for the cost of it, and again it should be like less than 20p per transcription or something like that, or even less."** Users can pay per use or buy a small balance. The project lead: **"people pay either on demand or on credits, so we can basically say that maybe each one costs 20p, 30p, or you buy credits via a pound or two, we'll just need to figure out the best workflow."** And the margin is deliberately thin. The project lead: **"we just do a markup on top of it, and the markup is probably 20%."**

## The Markup Is The Key

The implementation is the elegant part, and it is why this product can ship without anyone building a billing system. The project lead: **"instead of developing a billing for this, what we'll do is we'll use OpenRouter for the billing, and when somebody buys credits, we give them an OpenRouter key that has an amount of credits that is 80% of what they purchased."**

The rate has since been settled, and it is not eighty percent of the gross. The house takes **twenty-five percent of what remains after the payment processor's fee**, and the user's key is provisioned with the other seventy-five percent.

Taking the quarter after the fee rather than before it is the right way round. It means the processor's cut is borne by both parties in proportion instead of being absorbed entirely by the house, and it keeps the partner's fifty percent a share of money actually banked rather than of a headline that was never received.

```
   user pays                                     P
   less payment processor fee                  - S
   -------------------------------------------------
   net receipts                                  N  =  P - S
        |
        +-- issued OpenRouter key limit     0.75 x N
        |     user consumes inference; OpenRouter meters and deducts
        |
        +-- retained by the business        0.25 x N   <- the margin,
                                                          then split 50/50

   no invoice, no metering code, no reconciliation
```

Two points of precision are worth putting on the record before this becomes a contract term.

**The rate is now fixed, which removes the earlier ambiguity.** The eighty percent key implied two different readings, a twenty percent gross margin on revenue or a twenty-five percent markup on cost, and only one could go into an agreement. That is settled: twenty-five percent of net receipts after the processor fee. Expressed the other way, since the remaining seventy-five percent is the cost of goods, it is a one-third markup on cost. The first form is the one to write down, and the partner's fifty percent is a share of it.

**Small top-ups still do not clear their own payment costs, and now the numbers can be stated.** Stripe's published UK schedule is one and a half percent plus twenty pence per domestic card transaction, with European cards at two and a half percent and non-European at three and a quarter percent, all carrying the same twenty pence fixed component. On small purchases that fixed component dominates, and because the quarter is taken after it, the value the user actually receives moves sharply with the size of the top-up.

| User pays | Processor fee | Net | Retained (25%) | Key credit (75%) | User receives |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GBP 1 | 0.215 | 0.785 | 0.196 | 0.589 | 59 percent of what they paid |
| GBP 2 | 0.230 | 1.770 | 0.443 | 1.328 | 66 percent |
| GBP 5 | 0.275 | 4.725 | 1.181 | 3.544 | 71 percent |
| GBP 10 | 0.350 | 9.650 | 2.413 | 7.238 | 72 percent |
| GBP 20 | 0.500 | 19.500 | 4.875 | 14.625 | 73 percent |

At a one pound top-up the user hands over a pound and receives fifty-nine pence of inference. That reads as a forty-one percent charge and is hard to defend on a pricing page, even though the business is only taking twenty-five percent of what it banked. By five pounds the ratio settles near seventy-one percent, and by ten pounds it is close to the seventy-three percent ceiling the schedule allows.

The recommendation that follows is concrete: **set the minimum credit purchase at five pounds, and prefer ten**, so that the rate the user experiences reflects the rate actually being charged rather than the processor's fixed fee. The memo's suggestion of a pound or two sits below that floor and should be revised.

Two smaller notes. Stripe's fees carry UK VAT, recoverable if the entity is VAT registered and a real cost if it is not, and which of those applies should be settled before the split is computed. And the figures above are the published domestic schedule, which should be confirmed against the account's actual pricing and card mix before launch, since European and international cards cost more.

## What This Product Is Really Testing

The revenue here will be small and that is not the point. The 23 July inventory found nine mature tools all missing the same three things, the token purchase, a page, and users, and concluded that the highest-leverage work available was building that commercialisation kit once and reusing it across the portfolio. This is the first attempt at that kit, and the OpenRouter decision resolves the hardest of the three by not building it: the token purchase becomes an issued key with a cap, which is configuration rather than software.

If that holds, what this engagement produces is a template, not a tool. The next product needs a page, a payment link, and a key-provisioning call, and inherits the rest. The 17 July brief made the same argument in the abstract, that the real product of the go-to-market push is the shipping capability itself, on-demand charging and billing that every other venture will need. This is where that claim gets tested for the first time, and it should be judged on whether the second product ships faster than the first rather than on what the first one earns.

It is also the revenue-share rung of the 17 July partnership ladder, instantiated. That brief set out three rungs, direct payment, time-boxed revenue share, and venture creation with equity. This is the middle one with a short clock, which is the sensible place to start with a new partner and a product neither party has proven yet.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a revenue share.** The split is on profit, after pass-through costs.
- **Not a long commitment.** A short initial term from the first of August, with the exact length still to be settled.
- **Not a billing build.** The markup is the issued key's credit limit; no metering, invoicing, or reconciliation code is written.
- **Not a complex agreement.** Openness removes most of what a contract would otherwise protect.
- **Not a revenue bet.** The point is the reusable commercialisation kit, and the test is how fast the second product ships.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Profit split versus defining profit | Fifty percent of profit requires an agreed list of deductible costs; payment fees, developer programme fees, domains, and the funded credit pool are all small and all real, and an undefined divisor is the most common way an even split turns sour |
| Fixed processor fees versus small top-ups | Taking the quarter after the fee is correct, but the fixed twenty pence still dominates a one pound purchase, where the user receives fifty-nine pence of inference; the minimum top-up is the only real control, and it has to be set high enough that the experienced rate matches the charged rate |
| App store distribution versus selling credits | Selling digital credits inside an iOS or Android app usually engages the store's in-app purchase rules and commission, which would exceed the entire margin; selling credits on the web and letting the apps consume them is the likely answer and needs confirming before launch |
| Costs carried versus costs shared | The project lead carrying costs is generous at this size and quietly makes the split uneven in his favour once costs grow, or in the partner's if they do not; either way the arrangement should be revisited at renewal rather than inherited |
| Pre-funded credit pool versus pass-through | Every issued key draws on one funded balance, so the operator holds working capital between purchase and consumption; small now, and a genuine obligation as volume grows |
| Ninety days versus what ninety days can prove | The term is long enough to ship and short enough to exit, and probably too short to show whether the commercialisation kit is reusable, which is the actual thesis; that verdict may not arrive before the first renewal decision |
| A simple contract versus the awkward cases | Openness removes ownership and confidentiality, but not what happens on early termination, who holds the store accounts and signing keys, or what the partner keeps if the product continues without them |
| Template value versus first-product revenue | The strategic case rests on reuse, which nobody can verify until a second product ships on the same kit |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What are the notice and renewal terms inside the ninety days? | Whether either party can exit early, what notice applies, and whether the term lapses or rolls on the ninetieth day |
| What is deductible before the split? | The agreed cost list that turns revenue into the profit being halved |
| How is the take applied to the browser-local mode? | That tier consumes no OpenRouter credit, so a quarter of net has no cost of goods to sit against and needs either a different basis or a different price |
| What is the minimum credit purchase? | Set by the payment processor's fixed fee rather than by intuition, and checked against the intended price points |
| How are credits sold on mobile? | Whether the apps link out to a web purchase to stay outside store commission, and whether that is permitted by each store's rules |
| Who holds the accounts and keys? | Store developer accounts, signing keys, the payment account, and the OpenRouter management key, and what happens to each on exit |
| What happens at the end of the term? | Renewal, wind-down, and what each party keeps if they stop working together |
| Does the second product ship faster? | The measure that decides whether the kit is real, and the thing worth tracking from day one |
