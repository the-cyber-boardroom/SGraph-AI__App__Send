## What This Is

An honest read of the market conditions that created the opening, and of who might take it: **the opportunity exists because most GRC tooling is air-gapped from reality, a glorified set of spreadsheets or a platform with a few feeds, holding a register that describes a world it is not actually connected to, and the reason is structural rather than a tooling failure, because GRC functions were never set up as engineering functions, they had no engineers, they built almost nothing, they relied on tools, and above all their place in the organisation never gave them access to the good data the business already holds, which produced a function that is process-rich and data-poor, excellent at capturing risk as a workflow and weak at capturing it from reality; that asymmetry is the gap we are built for, since we strategically position as a connector that integrates with GRC tools and takes sensor data from others rather than collecting it ourselves, but the same asymmetry names our real competitors, because the threat does not come from the process side coming down, it comes from the data side coming up, from the observability, logging, telemetry, posture, and identity companies who already hold the context, already employ the engineers, already own the pipelines, and could do genuinely good risk analysis if they chose to, and the uncomfortable truth is that data is the harder half to acquire and governance is the cheaper half to add, which makes them the more efficient player on paper; what defends the position is not data, which we deliberately do not collect, but the things neither side has, a deep semantic model that turns telemetry into a business risk with a named owner, the acceptance and underwriting chain that makes a residual a decision rather than a chart, the board and general-counsel language that a data platform does not speak and would have to acquire against a different buyer and a different motion, the agentic wedge where both sides are currently weak, and sovereignty and open portability that neither a data giant nor a GRC incumbent will match because both depend on lock-in; so the strategy is to be the connector on purpose, ride the data side's improvement rather than fight it, partner early enough that we are the governance layer they recommend rather than the one they build, and go deep enough on the ontology and the acceptance model that the middle we occupy is not thin.** It is the ninth brief of 12 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.48 governance-layer-not-diagnostic brief, the v0.33.48 Microsoft comparison, the v0.33.48 vendor-sprawl brief, and the v0.33.44 ontology brief). New contributions: **the diagnosis of GRC as air-gapped from reality for structural rather than tooling reasons, the process-rich and data-poor asymmetry as the origin of the opening, the competitor map read from the data side rather than the process side, and the honest claim that data is the harder half so the data side is the more dangerous competitor.**

## GRC Is Air-Gapped From Reality

The first condition is a disconnection. The project lead: **"most GRC tools are air-gapped from reality, they are not connected, they basically live almost as spreadsheets or a glorified set of spreadsheets."** Even the platform versions do not close it. The project lead: **"even when there is a platform, and some have feeds already, it is still not as connected to the real world as possible."** The consequence is that a register becomes a document about a world it does not touch: entries age, the estate changes underneath them, and nobody finds out until an incident proves the map was wrong. This is exactly why the corpus insists on the twin and the connected-to edge, because a risk that is not attached to something real is an assertion, not a finding.

## The Reason Is Structural, Not A Tooling Failure

The important part of the diagnosis is why, because it tells you whether the gap will close on its own. The project lead: **"most GRC functions were never set up as engineering functions, they never really have engineers, they didn't develop a lot of stuff, they basically were relying on tools."** And the deeper cause is organisational position rather than skill. The project lead: **"more importantly, they didn't have access to all the good data that exists in a business because of where they fit in the function."** That is not something a better GRC product fixes, because the constraint is where the function sits and what it can reach, so the gap has persisted through several generations of tooling and will persist through the next one.

## The Asymmetry: Process-Rich, Data-Poor

That history produced a lopsided function, and the lopsidedness is the opening. The project lead: **"the ability to capture risks was very good from a process point of view, but was not very good from a data capture point of view."** GRC is process-rich and data-poor: strong at workflow, roles, cadence, and reporting, weak at knowing what is actually true right now. The mirror image exists elsewhere in the business, in the teams that are data-rich and process-poor, holding enormous quantities of ground truth with no governance model over it. The market opportunity is the space between those two, and the strategic question is which side crosses it first.

## Our Position: The Connector

We have positioned deliberately in that space. The project lead: **"we are strategically positioning ourselves as a connector, we integrate with GRC tools, we integrate with other tools, and we get sensors from other people."** That is the same consume-not-probe position stated commercially: we do not collect the telemetry and we do not replace the workflow, we connect the two and add the layer that neither has, the grounded graph, the rated residual, and the owned, dated decision. It also means our fortunes rise with the sensors, since every improvement in the data layer improves our view without any work on our part.

## The Competitor Map

Read honestly, the threats arrive from several directions, and the dangerous ones are not the obvious ones.

| Category | What they already own | What they lack | How dangerous |
|----------|----------------------|----------------|---------------|
| Incumbent GRC platforms | The buyer, the process, the register, the board relationship | Engineering culture, and access to the business's real data | Lower than it looks: the gap is structural, and they have failed to close it for years |
| Observability, logging, and telemetry | The data, the pipelines, the engineers, the ingestion | The risk ontology, the acceptance model, the board buyer | The most dangerous: they could do genuinely good risk analysis if they chose |
| Posture and findings tools | Configuration and findings data, and the language of risk | Business risk rather than technical risk, and the underwriting chain | Real: closest to rolling findings into a register |
| Identity and non-human identity | The grants, which is where reach is computed from | The governance layer above the grants | Real: they hold the input our blast radius is derived from |
| Agent security and testing | Agent discovery and behaviour findings | Governance, ownership, and board framing | Partners first, per consume-not-probe, but they could add a thin register |
| Hyperscalers and large platforms | Effectively all of the data | Focus, and they are conflicted as agent vendors themselves | Slow, but decisive if they moved |
| Consultancies | The board relationship and the process | A product that scales without people | Compete for the budget, not the position |
| The customer themselves | Their own data and, if mature, the scaffolding | A reason to build a product that is not their business | Real for the largest programs, per the mature-program brief |

## The Uncomfortable Answer: The Data Side Is The Threat

The memo asks who could play this game more efficiently, and the honest answer is the people who already have the data. The project lead: **"that industry is going to have quite a lot of interesting threat and competitors, which will come from the other way, which will come from the context, which will come from data collection, like a lot of the logging and observability companies would actually be able to do a lot of really good risk analysis."** This deserves to be stated without softening. Data collection is the capital-intensive, slow, unglamorous half: pipelines, agents, connectors, retention, scale, and a customer who already sends you everything. Governance is comparatively cheap to add on top: a schema, a workflow, a few roles, a dashboard. So on paper, a company that already holds the telemetry is better placed to build a risk layer than a risk company is to acquire the telemetry, because the hard half is already done. Any strategy that assumes the data side will not notice is a strategy built on hope.

## Why They Have Not, And What That Buys Us

The reasons they have not are real but not permanent. Their buyer is the engineering or SRE organisation, not the board, the general counsel, or the risk officer, and selling governance means a different motion, a different language, and a different champion, which large companies are famously bad at crossing. Risk governance is a small adjacent market next to their core, so it competes badly for internal attention. And most importantly, their data is about infrastructure, while a risk register is about the business: a semantic model that says this configuration reaches this data which exposes this customer obligation which lands on this executive is not telemetry, it is an ontology plus an underwriting chain, and no amount of log volume produces it. That is a genuine moat, but it is a moat of modelling and language, not of data, and it holds only while we stay ahead on the modelling.

## What Actually Defends The Position

Given all of that, the defensible parts are the ones that are not data. The semantic model and the grounding ladder, which turn a finding into a business risk with a blast radius and an owner, and which are accumulated work rather than a schema anyone writes in a sprint. The acceptance and underwriting model, which makes a residual an owned, dated decision that rolls to a board, and which is the thing every category above lacks. The board and general-counsel language, which is a distribution and credibility asset as much as a product one. The agentic wedge, where both sides are currently weak and the risk class is growing fastest. And sovereignty with open portability, which neither a data platform nor a GRC incumbent will match, because both are built on keeping the customer's state inside their system, whereas ours is built on the customer keeping it even if we disappear.

## The Strategy: Ride The Sensors, Deepen The Middle

The conclusion is to lean into the connector position rather than drift out of it. Do not build collection, because it is the expensive half and it turns our best partners into competitors. Consume from the data side deliberately, so that their investment compounds into our view and their improvement is our improvement. Partner early and visibly, so that when a telemetry or identity company reaches for governance, the cheaper path is to recommend us rather than to build a thin version themselves. Deepen the middle relentlessly, because a thin translation layer is exactly what gets squeezed, and the ontology, the acceptance chain, and the evidence graph are what make it thick. And move on the agentic wedge now, while the process side cannot see the agents and the data side has not yet decided that governing them is its problem.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim the incumbents are the threat.** The process side has failed to close this gap for years; the data side has not tried yet.
- **Not a dismissal of the data side.** They hold the harder half, and assuming they will not notice is not a strategy.
- **Not a data-collection plan.** We deliberately do not collect; collection turns partners into competitors.
- **Not a permanent moat.** Modelling and language defend the middle only while we stay ahead on them.
