# A Full Register, Not A Short One: Everything That Does Something Has Risks, And Surprise Is The Signal

**version** v0.33.48
**date** 12 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Architect, Product, Strategy

**type** Arch brief

---

## What This Is

The property that a real risk register is dense, not sparse, and what its size and its surprises actually tell you: **the common failure of a risk register is that it holds only the few very big risks and treats the goal as having no risks, when the truth is the opposite, that everything which does something has risks, because a risk is just an unintended side effect of a capability, so a system that can edit a customer database or sits on the internet carries a great many of them, and the honest relationship between what a product does and its risks is one to many, one feature spawning many risks, especially for new features; the sharper version of this is that capabilities exceed features, because capabilities are everything the application can actually do, including the undocumented behaviour nobody sells or even remembers, while features are only what people think or claim it does, so a register built from the feature list will always undercount, and the size of the register is itself a measurement, a thin register does not mean a safe product, it means the creators do not yet understand the risks their product has; this reframes calibration, because as events unfold the thing you should rarely worry about is a risk you already listed materialising, since that was expected and owned, and the real alarm is a risk you never thought of materialising, which raises two questions at once, why was it missed and what else has been missed, so surprise is the signal of an incomplete register rather than of bad luck; the conclusion is that a complex product should have dozens, hundreds, or thousands of interconnected risks precisely because it does many things, and the work is not to make that number small but to map them, own them, and decide the acceptance of each, the level, the liability, and whether every stakeholder is prepared to bear them, because the risks will exist whether or not they are written down, and that is the key property.** It is the fourth brief of 12 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.46 every-action-has-risks brief, the v0.33.47 kitchen-sink brief, the v0.33.46 risk-to-an-exec brief, and the v0.33.35 no-deny risk-register brief). New contributions: **the register-density thesis that a complex product has many risks by nature, the capabilities-exceed-features gap as the reason feature-derived registers undercount, the register as a measure of the creators' own understanding, and calibration-by-surprise where an unlisted risk materialising is the real alarm and a signal of incompleteness.**

## The Flaw: Registers That Only Hold Big Risks

The starting observation is about how registers usually look. The project lead: **"a lot of risk registers only capture really big risks, and it is all about not having risks."** The mindset behind a short register is that risk is an exception to be minimised toward zero, so only the handful of catastrophic items get written down and the goal becomes the absence of entries. That inverts what a register is for. A register with five items on a product that does a thousand things is not a safe product; it is an unexamined one.

## Everything That Does Something Has Risks

The premise underneath is total, and it is the same one the every-action brief rests on. The project lead: **"everything has risks, if a product or a service does something you will have risks, because risks are the unintended side effects."** A risk is not an add-on to a capability; it is the shadow the capability casts. The bigger the thing the product can do, the larger that shadow. The project lead: **"if you have a system that can edit a customer database, there is a crazy amount of risks, and a system on the internet, a lot more."** And the mapping from what a product does to what it risks is not one to one. The project lead: **"there should be a one-to-one, actually in most cases a one-to-many relationship, between the features of an application, especially the new features, and the risks."** One capability spawns many risks, so a register should fan out from each thing the product can do, not collapse to a shortlist.

## Capabilities Exceed Features

The reason a feature-derived register undercounts is a gap the corpus has hit before from the other side. The project lead: **"there is a big difference between capabilities and features, capabilities is almost everything the application can do, features is what people think or sell that the application does."** Features are the advertised surface; capabilities are the real surface, and the real surface is larger. The project lead: **"you would be amazed at the undocumented features."** Those undocumented capabilities, the things the system can do that nobody wrote down or sold, are exactly where the unowned risks live, which is the same point the kitchen-sink brief made as rate what is possible, not what is permitted. A register built from the feature list inherits the feature list's blind spots, so the enumeration has to start from what the product can do, not from what it claims to do.

## The Register Measures The Creators' Understanding

This gives the register a second, diagnostic use. The project lead: **"you can even use the risk register to understand how much even the creators of the product understand the risks it has."** The size and grounding of a register is a readout of comprehension: a thin register on a complex product is not evidence of safety, it is evidence that the people who built it have not yet enumerated what it does and therefore what it risks. When you look at a product, a website, or a service, the honest expectation is a full list. The project lead: **"when you look at a product, a website, or a service, you should get a full list of risks connected to it, because that is where the product works, if the product does something it will have risks."** So the register is not only a management tool; it is a mirror held up to how well the makers know their own creation.

## Calibration: The Real Alarm Is The Risk You Did Not List

The most useful reframing here is of calibration. The project lead: **"you have this concept of calibration, as events unfold you should rarely be worried that a risk you already had has materialised, the worry is that a risk you had not thought about has just materialised, and that raises two questions, why did you not pick it up in the first place, and what else is there that you have not thought through."** A listed risk firing is not a failure of the register; it was foreseen, owned, and accepted, so its materialising is the system working. The alarm is an unlisted risk arriving, because it proves the map was incomplete, and it does so in a way that generalises: if this one was missed, the process that missed it will have missed others. Surprise, in this model, is not bad luck; it is a measurement of incompleteness, and it should trigger a search for the siblings of the risk that surfaced rather than a patch of the single instance.

## Density Is The Point: Dozens To Thousands

The conclusion runs against the instinct to keep the number small. The project lead: **"you should have dozens, hundreds, thousands of risks, all interconnected and mapped together, in complex products, because it means they do stuff."** A large, connected register is the expected shape of a product that does many things, not a sign of a product in trouble. The number is not the problem to solve; the acceptance is. The project lead: **"then it becomes a question of acceptance, is it a low or medium risk, is the customer prepared, is the company prepared, is there liability, are all the stakeholders happy to bear those risks, but they will exist, and that is a key property."** The work moves from pretending the risks are few to mapping them all, rating each, and deciding, per risk and per stakeholder, whether to accept it, because the risks are there whether or not anyone wrote them down.

## Why This Matters For RiskMandate

Everything in this property is something the graph is built to hold and a conventional register is not. A graph of graphs can carry thousands of interconnected risks and keep them navigable, where a spreadsheet forces the shortlist that causes the problem. The capabilities-exceed-features point is the union-of-possible we already rate, so enumeration starts from reach rather than from the brochure. The register-as-understanding-measure is a product in itself, a readout of how well a team knows its own system that a buyer or a board would value. And calibration-by-surprise feeds re-underwriting directly: a materialised unlisted risk is a trigger to re-open the register and hunt for what else was missed, which is exactly the kind of event our model turns into an owned action rather than a one-off fix.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a call for a bigger number for its own sake.** Density is the natural shape of a complex product, not a target to pad toward.
- **Not fear.** The point is acceptance of many owned risks, not alarm at their count.
- **Not a claim that all surprises are failures.** Some risks are genuinely unforeseeable; the signal is to search, not to blame.
- **Not a spreadsheet property.** A dense, interconnected register needs the graph to stay navigable.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Density versus usability | Thousands of risks is complete but can overwhelm unless the graph makes the density navigable |

| Count as understanding versus padding | A register can be padded to fake comprehension, so grounding quality matters more than raw count |
| Surprise as signal versus hindsight | Calling a materialised risk missed can be unfair hindsight; some risks were genuinely unforeseeable |
