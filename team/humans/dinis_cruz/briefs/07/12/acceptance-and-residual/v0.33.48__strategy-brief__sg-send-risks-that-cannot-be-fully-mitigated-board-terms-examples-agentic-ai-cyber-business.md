## The Board Question, Answered Plainly

Mitigation is not elimination. Every control lowers the likelihood that a risk occurs, or the impact when it does, but a control that drives either all the way to zero almost never exists, because to reach zero you would usually have to switch off the capability that creates the value in the first place. So the honest answer to why a risk cannot be fully mitigated is that a material residual remains after every reasonable control, and the board's real decision is not how to reach zero, which is unavailable, but which residuals the enterprise will consciously bear, at what level, for how long, and with which stakeholders informed and prepared. A board that demands zero does not get zero; it gets a team that stops reporting the residual, which is covert acceptance, and covert acceptance is the one outcome worse than an owned one.

## Why A Residual Always Remains

The reasons a residual cannot be removed fall into a small number of structural categories, each stated as a board would hear it.

| Reason | In board terms |
|--------|----------------|
| Inherent to the capability | We cannot remove this without giving up the business we are in |
| Third party we do not control | Our exposure is a function of a supplier's security we cannot govern |
| Threat evolves faster than the control | The adversary changes faster than our defences can keep up |
| The control has its own risk | The fix introduces its own exposure, so mitigation is a trade, not an erasure |
| Probabilistic, not binary | We can lower the odds, but not to zero |
| The human factor | People remain fallible and can be manipulated, and we need people |
| Emergent and unenumerable | The system can act in ways we did not design and cannot fully list |
| Irreversibility | Once data leaves or trust breaks, no control undoes it after the fact |
| Compliance is not immunity | Meeting the standard reduces, but does not remove, liability |
| Speed outpaces response | By the time we detect it, the damage is already done |

## Agentic AI: The Sharpest Current Example

Agentic AI is the case boards are asking about now, and its residual is high and, today, genuinely irreducible below a material level. Prompt injection is an unsolved problem: an agent cannot reliably separate the instructions it should follow from the untrusted content it is asked to process, so a poisoned input can redirect it, and there is no patch for this, only partial reduction. Agents exhibit emergent behaviour, doing things nobody specified, because a broad instruction admits interpretations no one wrote down. Their non-determinism means the same input can produce different outputs, so the system cannot be fully tested or certified the way deterministic software can. Their reach is the union of everything their credentials can touch, executed at machine speed, so the blast radius is large and the response window is short. And the underlying model is a supply-chain dependency the enterprise did not build and cannot fully inspect. In board terms: we cannot guarantee the agent only does what we intend, we cannot enumerate everything it might do, we cannot reproduce its behaviour to certify it, and it can act faster than we can stop it, so the residual is real, and the decision is to accept it consciously and bound it, or to decline the capability, not to pretend it has been mitigated.

## Cyber Risks That Cannot Be Mitigated To Zero

| Risk | Why it cannot be fully mitigated | Board-term residual |
|------|--------------------------------|---------------------|
| Being on the internet at all | To be reachable by customers is to be reachable by attackers | We accept exposure as the price of operating online |
| Email and phishing | We need email, and people can always be deceived | A share of phishing will land; we reduce and recover, not prevent |
| Zero-day vulnerabilities | Unknown by definition, so no patch or signature exists yet | There is always a window we cannot close in advance |
| Software supply chain | We inherit our suppliers' and open-source flaws and cannot audit all of it | Our software depends on code we did not write and cannot fully vet |
| Insider threat | Legitimate access is required to do the job, and trust can be abused | We cannot remove trust without removing function |
| Credential and token theft | Users hold credentials, and stolen session tokens can bypass multifactor | Authentication can be defeated by theft, not only by guessing |
| Ransomware | We can harden and recover, but not guarantee we are never hit | We lower likelihood and impact, not the possibility |
| Social engineering | People can be manipulated, including the service desk | The human path around controls cannot be closed entirely |
| Patch lag | Patching is never instant across a real estate | A window between disclosure and patch always exists |
| Data exfiltration | Once data leaves, no control brings it back | A breach is irreversible; we manage before and after, not undo |
| Denial of service | A large enough attack can still degrade service | We absorb and reduce, but cannot guarantee availability |
| Cloud and configuration | Complex systems drift, and one setting can expose a lot | Some misconfiguration risk is inherent to operating at scale |

## Business Risks That Cannot Be Mitigated To Zero

| Risk | Why it cannot be fully mitigated | Board-term residual |
|------|--------------------------------|---------------------|
| Key-person dependency | Knowledge and relationships leave with the person | Succession reduces the loss but cannot insure it away |
| Market and competition | We do not control the market or competitors' moves | Competitive and demand risk is inherent to operating |
| Reputation | Perception can turn on events we do not control | We prepare and respond, but cannot prevent all reputational harm |
| Regulatory change | We do not control the legislature or regulators | New rules can impose cost and liability we cannot pre-empt |
| Litigation | Anyone can sue, and outcomes are uncertain | We reduce exposure, not the possibility of a claim |
| Concentration | A key customer, supplier, or geography carries weight | Diversification reduces, but some concentration remains |
| Macroeconomic and currency | Rates, currencies, and cycles move against us | Hedging has cost and residual; the exposure is never zero |
| Vendor acquisition and sunset | Suppliers are bought, change direction, or disappear | We are exposed to changes in companies we do not own |
| Fraud | A determined insider or outsider can still act | Controls reduce fraud; they do not eliminate it |
| Force majeure and black swan | Pandemics, disasters, and shocks are unpredictable | We build resilience; we do not prevent the event |
| Talent market | We do not control the supply of skills | Hiring and retention risk is inherent to a labour market |
| Product liability | A product that does something can cause harm | Doing anything useful carries a liability we cannot zero out |

## The Acceptance Answer

If a risk cannot be mitigated to zero, the board still has a precise job, and it is the one our whole model is built around. Rate the residual honestly, using the worst case of the reach and the controls actually in place. Set the level the enterprise is prepared to bear. Attach a named owner who accepts it, at the altitude that can carry it, rolling up to the CEO and the board. Put a clock on it, so the acceptance is revisited as the world and the controls change. And confirm that the stakeholders who bear the consequences, customers, shareholders, regulators, are informed and prepared. That is the answer to the board's question: the risk cannot be mitigated because a material residual is inherent to doing the thing that creates the value, and therefore the residual must be owned and accepted explicitly, not driven to a zero that does not exist. The board's role is not to demand the impossible; it is to decide, on the record, which residuals the enterprise will live with.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not an argument against mitigation.** Controls are essential; the point is that they reduce the residual, they do not remove it.
- **Not a counsel of despair.** An unmitigable residual is manageable once it is owned, rated, and bounded.
- **Not a reason to decline every risky capability.** Some residuals are worth bearing for the value; that is the decision.
- **Not legal advice.** The liability and governance points are factual, not determinations.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Reduce versus eliminate | Strong controls can make a residual small, but boards must not mistake small for zero |
| Accept versus decline | Some residuals should be accepted for the value; others mean the capability should not be run |
| Transparency versus comfort | Naming an unmitigable residual is uncomfortable, and hiding it is worse |
| Residual today versus tomorrow | Some residuals shrink as controls mature, so acceptance must be time-boxed, not permanent |
