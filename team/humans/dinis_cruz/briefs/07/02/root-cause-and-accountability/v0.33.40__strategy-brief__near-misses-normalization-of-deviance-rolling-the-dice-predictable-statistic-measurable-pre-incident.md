# Counting the Rolls of the Dice: Near-Misses, Normalization of Deviance, and Turning a One-Off Into a Statistic

**version** v0.33.40
**date** 2 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Architect
**type** Strategy brief

---

## What This Is

A briefing on an under-attended root cause and how to measure it: **the question that matters is not only why a single event went wrong, but how many times the same roll of the dice was made and got away with it, which reframes an incident from a one-off into a predictable statistical event and, crucially, is measurable before an incident ever occurs; this operationalises what safety science calls the normalization of deviance and drift, and instruments it through near-miss frequency, turning the free lessons an organisation currently ignores into a leading indicator; it connects directly to the risk mandate, because the count exposes when one party is rolling dice that others depend on, it echoes the error-budget idea from site reliability engineering, and it applies to decisions like letting key personnel go without understanding the change to the risk profile.** The project lead asked for a better name; the established terms and a proposed metric are given below (cross-ref: the v0.33.35 five-whys, the v0.33.38 risk-appetite, confidence, and data-accuracy briefs). New contributions: **the roll-the-dice count, one-off-to-statistic, and measurable-before-incident.**

## The Under-Attended Root Cause

When something goes wrong, the usual analysis asks why that decision was made, and reaches for confirmation bias, gut calls, and the second story behind the error. The project lead points at a different question. The project lead: **"one of the root causes we do not pay enough attention to is how many times the same kind of event, the same roll of the dice, happened where they got away with it."** The failure on the day is only the visible instance. The real object of study is the pattern behind it. The project lead: **"the important question is not just what happened, but how many times similar actions and similar decisions were made where they got away with it."** Those earlier rolls, the ones that happened to land well, are where the culture actually lives.

## The Established Names

This is not a new phenomenon, and safety science has named it. Diane Vaughan, studying the Challenger disaster, called it the normalization of deviance: the gradual process by which unacceptable practices become acceptable because repeated deviations do not immediately cause catastrophe, so outcomes owed to luck start to feel like skill. Sidney Dekker describes the same slow slide as drift. The measurement side comes from high-reliability organisations and their treatment of the near-miss: where an ordinary organisation treats a near-miss as good news, a high-reliability one treats it as a crisis and a free lesson, because it reveals that the defences failed and only luck prevented harm. The related idea of risk normalization captures how a raised risk threshold erodes the ability to perceive the vulnerability at all. What the project lead is adding is the metric these mostly lack: a count. A workable name is the getting-away-with-it rate, or the near-miss frequency, or simply the roll count.

## When Things Go Right, No One Looks

The reason the count is missing is that success suppresses inquiry. The project lead: **"when things go right there is never any desire to look at what could have gone wrong, because fundamentally we got away with it."** Cybersecurity shows the pattern plainly. The project lead: **"an attacker comes in and exploits, and we look at the damage they did, not the damage they could have done, and we go, that is all right, we were okay."** The damage that was possible, the blast radius that luck kept closed, goes unexamined. The high-reliability discipline is the opposite instinct: investigate a success with the same rigour as a failure, and ask what controls were bypassed and what margins were eroded to reach it.

## From One-Off to Predictable Statistic

The count changes what kind of thing an incident is. The project lead: **"the important thing is to move from what happened was a one-off to what happened was actually a very predictable statistical event, because if you do ten or twenty dangerous events, eventually one or two will not work out."** Framed as a one-off, an incident invites the story of bad luck on the day. Framed as a base-rate event, it invites the true root cause: a pattern of risky rolls whose failure was only a matter of time. The dice were always going to come up badly eventually; the only open question was when.

## Measurable Before an Incident

This is the property that makes it valuable rather than merely wise. The project lead: **"the reason this is important is that we can measure this already, before an incident has occurred."** The rolls can be counted while they are still landing well, so the metric is a leading indicator rather than a post-mortem. An organisation can know it is accumulating dangerous rolls long before one fails, which is precisely the knowledge a risk register is meant to hold.

## Back to the Risk Mandate: Whose Dice, Whose Consequences

The count feeds straight into the risk mandate, and into the appetite-misalignment question. The project lead: **"one party may be willing to take that risk, but do the people who depend on them want them to be that cavalier and risk-insensitive."** A department may rationally accept a high roll rate for itself while exporting the consequences to others who never agreed to it, which is why the dependents should be pushing back. There is a mature version of this already in engineering. The project lead: **"Google SRE introduced error budgets, that systems should be able to go down a certain amount without side effects to their consumers, already taking into account that stuff will happen and planning for it."** An error budget is a roll rate made explicit and planned for, rather than left to luck and denial.

## Worked Examples

Beyond the cyber case, the clearest example is people. The project lead: **"we should measure the number of times companies roll the dice, for example firing someone without fully understanding the implications, and key personnel is a good example, when someone leaves the risk profile should change for better or worse."** Letting a person go is a roll of the dice whose consequences are rarely counted, and when key personnel leave, the register should move, for better or worse, and the organisation should be able to go back and learn from it. That learning-from-departures point is a topic in its own right.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not hindsight blame.** The point is the base rate, not condemnation of the person on the day.
- **Not anti-gut.** Gut and speed are often right; the question is how often the same roll is made.
- **Not anti-risk-taking.** Taking risk is fine; taking it without knowing the roll rate is not.
- **Not the key-personnel-departure memo.** Learning from who leaves is a separate topic.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Counting rolls versus success bias | The organisation least likely to count is the one getting away with it |
| Base rate versus small numbers | Estimating a failure rate from rare events is statistically hard |
| Roll rate versus legitimate appetite | A high rate can be a valid choice; the issue is whether dependents consented |
| Before-incident measurement | Counting near-misses requires seeing them, which success actively hides |
