# Open Source As Strategy, Not Charity: No Tech Moat, Lock-In In Quality And Certification, Shared Across Companies

**version** v0.33.49
**date** 17 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Commercial, Board

**type** Strategy brief

*Fifth of 17 July, a standalone memo on why open source is a commercial advantage rather than a gift. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A strategic case for open source in the Gen AI startup world, made from self-interest rather than altruism: **the value of open source is not that it is nice for the community or a way to give back, it is that it simplifies the tech stack, removes technology as the moat, and relocates the lock-in somewhere more durable and more honest; the moat argument is that software increasingly is not the moat, because technology now gets recreated and customised quickly, so defending the code is a losing position, and a healthier one is to make the code open and compete on what cannot be copied as cheaply; openness does not mean the customer receives everything, and in fact the model wants customers to hold only a subset of what lives in the main repo, for reasons of security, maintainability, and complexity, which is a deliberate design choice rather than a contradiction; open code is far easier to deploy everywhere, which is itself a requirement, and deploy-everywhere unlocks business models and a partner-distribution posture that closed software cannot reach, because partners gain real control; the lock-in then sits not on the technology but on the quality, the services, the new versions, the maintainability, and the certification of versions, which is a more positive ecosystem because the customer stays for value received rather than because they are trapped; the portfolio consequence is large for a founder who runs several focused companies in parallel, since open technology can be shared freely across them, and work developed but not commercialised in one place stays available to the team rather than being lost, so a new venture continues where the last left off rather than starting cold; and the closing reframe is that open source is less a licence decision than a workflow, one that produces healthier companies, which is why it is worth adopting for reasons that have nothing to do with generosity.** It is the fifth brief of 17 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.48 vendor-sprawl brief, the v0.33.48 fractal-semantic-graphs brief, the v0.33.42 commercial-model brief, and the v0.33.49 consulting-model brief). New contributions: **open source reframed as self-interested strategy rather than community goodwill, the removal of technology as the moat, the customer-holds-a-subset design choice, deploy-everywhere as a business-model unlock, the relocation of lock-in onto quality, services, versions, maintainability, and certification, the cross-company technology sharing for a serial founder, and open source as a workflow for healthier companies.**

## The Reframe: Not Charity, Strategy

The memo opens by refusing the usual justification for open source. The project lead: **"the power of open source is not for the community, it is not because it is nice for others, and it is not to give back."** That clearing of the ground matters, because the community framing is what lets executives treat open source as a cost or a concession rather than a strategy. Stated as self-interest, it becomes something a founder adopts because it wins, and the rest of the brief is the argument for why it wins. The setting is the serial Gen AI founder: someone who will start several companies, spin up services fast, add value, and then sell or grow them, for whom the reusable substrate across ventures is the asset, not any single codebase.

## It Simplifies The Stack

The first mechanism is the plainest. The project lead: **"first of all it dramatically simplifies the tech stack, everything becomes simpler."** Open components remove the bespoke glue, the licence gates, and the closed dependencies that accrete around proprietary choices, and simplicity is not a nicety here but the precondition for the rest, because a simple stack is what can be deployed everywhere, shared across companies, and handed to a partner. Simplicity is treated as the goal that the other benefits fall out of.

## Technology Is Not The Moat

The load-bearing claim is that defending the code is defending the wrong thing. The project lead: **"it allows for the removal of the idea that technology is the moat, more and more technology and software is not the moat, technology gets recreated, gets customised."** In a world where a capable team plus a model can reconstruct most software quickly, a moat built on secrecy is shallow and shrinking. The strategic response is not to guard the code harder but to stop pretending the code is the defensible asset, open it, and move the contest to ground that does not erode the moment someone else writes the same functions. This is the same instinct the corpus already applies to buyers, where the argument is made on arithmetic a buyer can check rather than on a black box.

## The Customer Gets A Subset, By Design

A nuance that keeps this from being naive follows immediately: open does not mean the customer receives the whole repository. The project lead: **"more and more we want situations where the customers only have a subset of the code that exists in the main repo, because of security, because of maintainability, because of complexity."** So the main repository holds the full technology, and what a given customer runs is a curated cut of it, chosen so that they carry less attack surface, less to maintain, and less complexity than the whole. This is a deliberate distribution decision rather than a contradiction of openness, though the tension between a fuller main repo and an open-source claim is real and is named below.

## Deploy Everywhere, And The Business Models That Unlocks

Openness buys reach, and reach buys models. The project lead: **"when you have open source it becomes much easier to deploy everywhere, which is a key requirement, and when you can deploy everywhere you have new business models."** Closed software is constrained to the environments its vendor will support; open software can run in the customer's own environment, on a partner's infrastructure, in an air-gapped estate, wherever the value is, and each of those becomes a place a business model can attach. The partner posture improves for the same reason. The project lead: **"the partner support is much better because the partners have much more control."** A partner who can see, run, and adapt the technology is a stronger channel than one reselling a box they cannot open.

## Where The Lock-In Actually Lives

This is the centre of the thesis. If the technology is open, the customer is not held by it, so the question is what holds them, and the answer is a healthier list. The project lead: **"the lock-in is not on the technology, the lock-in is in the quality and the services and the new versions and the maintainability and the certification of versions."** Each of those is earned rather than imposed, which is why the project lead calls the result better. The project lead: **"it is a much more positive ecosystem that allows a much more interesting creation."** A customer who stays because the versions are certified, the maintenance is real, and the service is good is a customer who could leave and chooses not to, which is the only kind of retention that survives a portable, open substrate. Certification of versions is worth flagging as a candidate product in its own right, since it is the same force-of-proof move the evidence-economy work makes, applied to releases rather than facts.

```
   CLOSED SOFTWARE                    OPEN SOURCE
   lock-in = the technology           technology is open, recreatable
   customer stays because trapped     customer stays for VALUE:
                                        quality
                                        services
                                        new versions
                                        maintainability
                                        certification of versions
```

## The Portfolio Play: Share Across Companies

The benefit compounds for a founder who is not running one company but several. The project lead: **"when you have multiple companies, and people working on multiple companies in parallel, especially focused companies, you can easily share technology between them."** Open technology moves across a portfolio without the friction that proprietary licences would impose between separately held businesses, so the same substrate powers many focused ventures. Continuity is the other half. The project lead: **"when you want to start something new, you continue where you left off, or you explore."** And nothing built is wasted. The project lead: **"a lot of times you develop a lot of technology but you only end up commercialising a part of it, and this way all of that technology still continues to be available to the team."** The uncommercialised remainder, which in a closed model dies with the venture that paid for it, stays in the shared core and seeds the next thing.

```
   SHARED OPEN CORE (the team's technology)
        |             |             |
        v             v             v
   COMPANY A      COMPANY B      COMPANY C      focused, parallel
   subset +       subset +       subset +
   its services   its services   its services

   uncommercialised work stays in the core, available to the next venture
```

## Open Source As A Workflow, Not A Gift

The reframe the memo closes on is the one to keep. The project lead: **"open source is so powerful, almost like a workflow that allows for much healthier companies."** Treating it as a licence checkbox misses the point; treated as a workflow, it changes how a portfolio simplifies, deploys, shares, retains, and continues. The health is structural: no energy spent defending a moat that does not hold, no technology lost between ventures, no customer retained by a trap, and a partner ecosystem that can actually carry the product. The generosity is incidental. The advantage is the reason.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not an argument from community or goodwill.** The case is self-interest; the benefit to others is a side effect, not the reason.
- **Not a claim that the customer gets everything.** By design the customer runs a curated subset of the main repo.
- **Not "technology is the moat."** The moat moves to quality, services, versions, maintainability, and certification.
- **Not a licence decision only.** Open source is framed as an operating workflow across a portfolio of companies.
- **Not a settled answer on revenue.** Exactly which of quality, services, or certification is the billable unit stays open.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Relocated moat versus durable moat | Quality and services are harder to copy than code, but a competitor on the same open code can also offer them; is the new moat actually defensible |
| Open source versus the customer subset | A fuller main repo than the customer receives sits uneasily with a pure open-source claim; this is open-core, and worth saying so plainly |
| Sharing across companies versus clean IP | Shared technology across separately owned or sold ventures raises licence hygiene and due-diligence questions at exit |
| Deploy everywhere versus support surface | Running in every environment multiplies what must be supported and secured; partner control is also a loss of central control |
| Simplicity asserted versus earned | Everything becomes simpler is a design goal, not automatic; open dependencies can add their own integration and maintenance load |
| Certification as lock-in versus openness | If certified versions are the retention mechanism, certification must add real assurance rather than gate access to open code |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| Where does the revenue actually sit? | The standing commercial-boundary question: which of quality, services, versions, or certification is metered |
| What is the licence, and how is the subset reconciled with it? | The open-core boundary: what is in the open main repo, what is withheld, and under which terms |
| What makes the quality and services moat durable? | The defence against a competitor building on the same open code |
| How is certification of versions operationalised? | Whether a signed, provenance-backed release becomes a product, and how it relates to the evidence economy |
| How is shared portfolio technology governed at exit? | Contribution-back, licence contamination, and what happens to the shared core when one company is sold |
