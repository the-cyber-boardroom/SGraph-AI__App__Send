# The Gen AI Consulting Model: On-Demand Hours, Priced By Speed, Billed From Zero, Empowering The Client

**version** v0.33.49
**date** 17 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Commercial, Board

**type** Strategy brief

*Fourth of 17 July, from a separate memo to the three-brief risk-mandate cluster. A commercial and delivery model for Gen AI era consulting. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A model for how Gen AI era consulting should be delivered and priced, offered as the next evolution of the contracting business: **the work, done by a freelancer or a company, is sold in blocks of minutes and hours drawn weekly from a pool rather than as a fixed-scope contract, which inverts the liability, because in the traditional model the client signs for the whole engagement up front and carries its liability from the first day, and that front-loading creates perverse incentives for the provider, whereas here the contract is only a ceiling of hours that burns up from zero, so if little useful work is done, or the work does not fit the client's workflow, the engagement can end after an hour or two with nothing more to invoice; price scales with speed rather than with volume, since a task that carries a couple of days of response time is charged less than one that has to be done in the next hour, which makes urgency, not size, the thing the client pays for; the arrangement de-risks both sides at once, the client because they commit to nothing beyond the hours actually used and useful, and the provider because every hour spent is a paid hour, so there is no unpaid speculative work; the thing that makes this newly possible is Gen AI, which supplies the project management, quality control, objectives and key results, briefs, tasks, and custom tooling that let senior delivery be metered at the granularity of hours, all of it Git-native and version-controlled so the workflow between the two parties is auditable and portable; the engagement is not a detached deliverable handed over a wall but an extra member of the team, because the provider works with the client's own agents, setup, and materials, so the relationship is with the customer's system and not only with the customer, and the whole point is to empower the client rather than to keep them dependent; in Wardley's terms it is the handover from explorer to villager and on toward town planner, helping the new generation of entrepreneurs and companies who have vibe-coded genesis and custom-built solutions turn them into maintainable, well-engineered, productised systems, which is the gap they hit the moment the building stops and the maintaining starts; the provider must run the same playbook they bring, their own agentic workflows and their own setup, and the economics come from concentrating high-value talent in one lower-cost, high-talent location, here the Algarve, from which one person can serve many clients at once, which the memo frames as the next generation of consulting.** It is the fourth brief of 17 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.42 commercial-model brief, the v0.33.49 risk-mandate brief, the v0.33.49 interval-ladder brief, and the v0.33.44 evidence-packs brief). New contributions: **the liability inversion from a fixed-scope contract to a zero-based ceiling of pooled hours, response time as the pricing axis, the symmetric de-risking of client and provider, Gen AI as the project-management and tooling layer that makes hour-granular senior delivery viable, the Git-native version-controlled workflow between the parties, the consultant as an extra team member working inside the client's own agentic setup, and the explorer-to-villager handover as the market the model serves.**

## The Inversion: Who Carries The Contract

The whole model turns on one reversal. The project lead describes the thing being fixed: **"the client says, here is what I want to do, the consultant says, okay, this is going to take X days, create the brief, sign the contract, and at that moment in time the client has the liability for the whole contract, which creates all sorts of perverse incentives for the providers."** The perverse incentive is the padded estimate and the defended scope, because once the client is liable for the whole job the provider is paid to fill it rather than to finish it. The proposal runs the other way. The project lead: **"the logic is the other way round."** What the two parties agree is a rate, not a scope. The project lead: **"they agree an hourly rate for the type of services, or maybe different hourly rates depending on the type of service, and also depending on the timeframe."** The contract then becomes a ceiling rather than a commitment. The project lead: **"the contract, when it is done, is up to an amount of hours, so up to five or ten or twenty or a hundred hours, but it starts from zero, and if there is no work done there is nothing to invoice."** The consequence is that a bad fit is cheap to discover. The project lead: **"the contract could end with just one hour being done or two hours being done, if there is not enough work, or if the work being delivered is not working with the client's workflow."**

Legal points are factual and not legal advice.

```
   TRADITIONAL FIXED-SCOPE                ON-DEMAND POOLED HOURS
   contract signed for the whole job      contract is a ceiling only
   client liable from day zero    ---->   hours burn UP from zero
   provider paid to fill the scope        client pays for used + useful
   incentive: pad and defend the job      end at 1 to 2 hrs if it does not fit
```

## Priced By Speed, Not By Size

The second move is that the price axis is response time. Faster work costs more, slower work costs less, so what the client is really buying when they pay a premium is urgency. The project lead: **"a task that can be done in a week, or has a couple of days response time, can be charged a lower price than something that needs to be done in the next hour or the next day."** This is worth putting next to the interval ladder in the companion risk brief, because it is the same shape seen from the commercial side: there, the acceptance interval a person chooses implies the response they have committed to, and here the response time a client chooses sets the price they pay. In both, the clock is the substance of the decision rather than metadata about it.

```
   RESPONSE TIME               PRICE PER HOUR
   next hour / next day           highest      urgency is the thing you pay for
   within a few days              middle
   within a week or more          lowest       patience is the discount
```

## Both Sides De-Risked At Once

The reason the inversion holds is that it removes a real risk from each party rather than shifting one party's risk onto the other. For the client, the removed risk is commitment to work that may not land. The project lead: **"this dramatically de-risks the cost and the commitment from the client."** For the provider, the removed risk is unpaid speculative effort, because in the pooled-hours model there is no such thing. The project lead notes that every hour the provider puts in is a billed hour, so the provider is never working ahead of payment on the promise of a signed scope. That symmetry is the point: the client stops carrying a contract they might regret, and the provider stops carrying the estimate risk that used to push them toward padding it.

The honest edge, addressed in the tensions below, is that this does not make the provider's risk vanish, it changes its shape. The provider no longer risks doing unpaid work; they risk an empty pipeline, since nothing guarantees the pool fills. The model trades estimate risk for utilisation risk, which is a better risk to hold but not a free one.

## What Gen AI Makes Possible

Metering senior delivery at the granularity of hours would have been unmanageable before, because the overhead of running a tight engagement used to swamp small blocks of work. The claim is that Gen AI collapses that overhead. The project lead: **"the power of Gen AI is that we can now apply a level of project management, quality control, objectives and key results, briefs, tasks, and even custom tooling for all of this that before was just impossible."** The delivery substrate is version control end to end. The project lead: **"everything should be based on Git, ideally GitHub, everything should be version controlled, and you have a set of workflows that are highly efficient between the client and the provider."** Git-native is not incidental here; it is what makes the pooled-hours model auditable and portable, because the record of what was done, when, and against which brief lives in a history the client keeps, which is the same sovereignty property the vault work argues for elsewhere in the corpus.

## Not A Deliverable, An Extra Team Member

The engagement model is the sharpest departure from classic consulting. The consultant does not take a specification away and return a finished artifact; they work inside the client's own environment. The project lead: **"you are not just talking to a customer, you are talking to the customer's agents, you are talking to the customer's setup."** The role is a seat on the team, not a vendor at arm's length. The project lead: **"the consultant acts like fundamentally an extra member of the team, and is working with the materials that the client is creating."** And the stated goal is to leave the client stronger, not more dependent. The project lead: **"it is all about empowering the client, all about enabling the client to have better engineering, better quality."** The added value is engineering judgement applied to work that non-engineers produced. The project lead on the blind spot: **"because they are not engineers, because they do not have an engineering, development, and DevOps background, they sometimes are not asking the right questions, the models over-engineer stuff, they do not have good engineering practices, or they go on tangents that you actually need to control."**

## The Explorer-To-Villager Handover

The market this serves is a specific moment in a product's life. In Wardley's evolution model the client is the explorer, doing the genesis and custom-built work where uncertainty is high and structure is low, and the pain arrives at the transition, when a thing that was fun to create now has to be maintained. The project lead: **"it is all nice and good to create, but this is where the handover between the explorers to the villagers happens; the client is the explorer, doing genesis and custom-built solutions, and now they need to maintain."** The service walks the client along that evolution. The project lead: **"the evolution from explorer to villager, and even villager to town planner, depending on which role the consultant takes on."** This is the gap a wave of vibe-coded MVPs is now hitting: the building was cheap and fast, the maintaining was not designed for, and there is no engineering discipline underneath to carry it forward. The model sells exactly the discipline that the genesis phase skipped.

## The Provider Runs The Same Playbook

The symmetry that keeps the model honest is that the provider cannot sell an agentic way of working they do not practise. The project lead: **"the service provider also has to work this way, has to have its own setup of agents, its agentic workflows, that it is going to operationalise and even help the customer with."** So the provider's own tooling is both how they achieve the efficiency that makes hour-granular delivery profitable and part of what they transfer to the client. A provider without their own agentic setup would be selling a standard rather than meeting it, which is the same failure the corpus flags when a governance vendor concentrates risk it does not itself carry.

## Talent Arbitrage From One Location

The economics rest on concentrating high-value talent in a lower-cost, high-talent place, here the Algarve, and serving clients anywhere. The project lead: **"the providers are in the Algarve, leveraging living in a remote place which is a bit cheaper, but also with access to a lot of great talent that does not usually have access to the business."** The model scales because the constraint is no longer one consultant to one engagement. The project lead: **"one person can provide services to multiple clients, and it is highly empowering."** The whole thing is paid on demand, which is what lets a single provider spread across several clients without any of them holding a fixed claim on their time. The framing the memo lands on is that this is the next generation of consulting: remote, agent-operated, hour-metered, and located where talent is strong and cost is lower.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not fixed-scope contracting.** There is no whole-job liability signed up front; the contract is a ceiling of hours that burns from zero.
- **Not a retainer.** Nothing is invoiced for hours not worked, and an engagement can end after one or two hours.
- **Not a detached deliverable.** The provider works inside the client's own setup, agents, and materials, as an extra team member.
- **Not location-independent.** The economics depend on concentrating talent in one lower-cost, high-talent place.
- **Not a claim that Gen AI replaces the engineer.** It is the layer that makes granular senior delivery viable, applied to work non-engineers produced.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Client de-risked versus provider utilisation | The model removes the provider's unpaid-work risk and replaces it with pipeline risk; a better risk to hold, but not zero |
| Response-time pricing versus gaming | Clients can under-declare urgency for the cheaper rate, then escalate, unless the tier is defined and enforced |
| Empowerment versus dependency | Working inside the client's agentic setup can deepen reliance on the provider, the opposite of the stated goal |
| Deep access versus trust and security | An extra team member inside the client's agents and repos is a real access surface the model does not yet price; that access is itself a risk mandate |
| Hour-granular control versus overhead | Applying briefs, OKRs, and tooling to tiny blocks can cost more in ceremony than the block is worth |
| Talent arbitrage versus its own erosion | The lower-cost-location advantage compresses as remote work normalises; the durable moat is the tooling and the talent, not the postcode |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What is the minimum billable unit, and how is time evidenced? | The pool needs a trusted, auditable log of hours, ideally Git-native, so the client can verify what a block bought |
| How are the response-time tiers defined and enforced? | The service-level definition that makes speed-based pricing legible rather than a source of dispute |
| How is the provider's deep access governed? | The access to the client's agents, setup, and repos framed and rated as a mandate, with its own scope and blast radius |
| What is the utilisation model? | How many concurrent clients a single provider can serve before quality drops, and how the pool schedules across them |
| Where does this sit relative to RiskMandate and the platform? | Whether this is a services arm, a delivery channel for the vault and register products, or a separate business |
| How is IP and liability handled in a zero-based ceiling? | Ownership of work product and residual liability when an engagement ends after one or two hours |
