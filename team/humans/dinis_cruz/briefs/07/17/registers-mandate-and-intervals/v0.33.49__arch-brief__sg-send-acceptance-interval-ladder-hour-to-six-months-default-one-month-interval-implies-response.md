## Why The Interval Is The Decision

The corpus has always said the only variable is how long. The consequence, which this memo makes explicit, is that the duration is not administrative metadata attached to a decision; it is the decision. Choosing an interval simultaneously declares the severity and commits the resources, because each rung implies a different response, and a person who picks one hour has said pull the plug, whether or not they realised it. That collapses two steps that are normally separate and normally disconnected: rating a risk, and deciding what to do about it. Here they are the same act, which is why the ladder is worth writing down once and using everywhere.

## The Ladder

| Interval | What choosing it means | The response it commits you to |
|----------|------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Under 1 hour | I will not carry this at all, not even today | Pull the plug now |
| 1 to 24 hours | A P1 incident | Pull the plug, stop the thing, in most cases |
| 1 day to 1 week | An incident, lower grade | Assemble the team, decide, make the change, report back |
| 1 week to 1 month | A project for a team that already exists | An existing team takes it on, now |
| 1 to 3 months | Work that has to be stood up | Assemble the team and fund it |
| Over 3 months | We are not doing anything about this | Wait and see, and revisit |

The project lead on the top of the ladder: **"if you have less than a day risk acceptance, then that is fundamentally a P1, because if you say I do not want to accept this risk for more than an hour once I know about it, then that means you need to pull the plug."** And on the twenty-four-hour rung. The project lead: **"between one hour and twenty four hours, that is an incident, and that is fundamentally, usually in most cases, pull the plug, stop it."** On the week rung. The project lead: **"if it is between a day and a week, that is an incident, lower level incident, but it is still an incident, because you still have to assemble the team, make the decisions, make the change, and report back."** On the month rung. The project lead: **"between a week and a month, that needs to hit a project of an existing team."** On the quarter. The project lead: **"you assemble the team, and you need to then fund it."** And on the honest bottom. The project lead: **"more than three months, you do not do anything, you just wait to see what happens."**

## Anything Under A Week Is An Incident

The rule that makes the ladder legible in one line. The project lead: **"anything less than a week is an incident, the level is the type of incident that you have."** So the sub-week rungs are not shorter acceptances, they are incidents wearing an acceptance's clothes, and the interval simply names the grade. This is useful because it connects the register directly to the incident process an organisation already runs: choosing a sub-week interval is raising an incident, and the register should say so rather than leaving someone to notice later.

## The Default Is A Month

The proposed default sits deliberately just above the incident line. The project lead: **"we can default to kind of a month."** A month is short enough to force a genuine first decision rather than a shrug, and long enough that it is not an incident, so it makes the honest middle the path of least resistance. It also carries a promise rather than only a deadline. The project lead: **"another month is also meant to be reviewing in a month, you could also do you do not do anything, but it means that in a month from now, nothing has changed on that level."** So a month is both an acceptance and an appointment: someone looks again, and if nothing changed, that is a fact on the record rather than a fact nobody noticed.

## The Gap: Risks With No Plug To Pull

The ladder exposes something the agentic briefs have circled without naming. The project lead: **"it is interesting when you start to have risks that do not have that capability, what are the risks where you do not have the ability to pull the plug."** If the honest interval for a risk is under twenty four hours, the ladder says pull the plug, and if there is no plug, the acceptance cannot be honoured. That is not a scheduling problem, it is a finding: a risk whose required response is unavailable is a risk the organisation cannot actually control, which is exactly the shape of an agent with standing credentials, a third-party platform with no off switch, or a dependency nobody can disconnect. The ladder therefore doubles as a test: for every short-interval risk, does the plug exist, and who can pull it.

## Unaccepted Means Critical, And It Is Yours Right Now

The strongest rule in the memo is about the risks with no acceptance at all. The project lead: **"any risk that exists that has not been underwritten, it is actually a big risk, almost like a critical risk or super high risk for an executive at any level, until you hit the board, or until you hit the point where there is already risk accepted above."** And the consequence is personal and immediate. The project lead: **"any risk that has not been accepted immediately goes into that one's risk dashboard, because that is a massive risk, that means that that person right now is accountable for the business, which is very bad from a business point of view, but is also very bad for the individual."**

This is worth stating plainly because it inverts the usual incentive. In most organisations, a risk that has not been escalated feels safest for the person holding it, since nobody is watching. In this model it is the worst possible state, because an unaccepted risk has not vanished, it has simply come to rest on whoever is nearest, and that person is personally carrying an enterprise exposure with no signature above them. So the default rating for an un-underwritten risk is critical, it appears on the holder's own dashboard the moment it is known, and it stays critical until it is accepted at a level that can carry it. Escalation stops being an admission of failure and becomes the thing that protects you, which is the same protection argument the mandate brief makes and the practical reason a person would want their mandate at all.

## How This Fits The Model

The ladder slots into the machinery already built. It is the how-long that the no-deny principle leaves as the only variable. It is the clock on the level ledger from the accept-first brief, so a one-month acceptance is what schedules the next adjustment. It gives the funded-project trigger its natural home, since the one-to-three-month rung is literally assemble and fund. And it gives the mandate a face, because what a role receives is a list of what they carry, at what level, until when, and who signed above, and the until-when is this ladder.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a form field.** The interval is the severity decision and the resourcing commitment in one act.
- **Not a way to avoid incidents.** Sub-week intervals are incidents; the ladder just names the grade.
- **Not a promise that the plug exists.** A short interval with no plug to pull is a finding, not a schedule.
- **Not a fixed default.** A month is proposed as the default because it forces a real first decision.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Interval implies response versus gaming | If a short interval triggers an incident, people will pick longer ones to avoid the work |
| A one-month default versus review fatigue | Monthly re-underwriting is honest and will feel relentless at scale without good tooling |
| Over three months is honest versus dispiriting | Saying we are waiting to see is truthful and reads as neglect unless the reasoning is on the record |
| Unaccepted equals critical versus noise | Making every un-underwritten risk critical is correct and will flood a dashboard on day one |
