# Scaling Collaboration: The Coordination Tax Was Always The Ceiling, And Agents Are The First Thing That Pays It

**version** v0.33.53
**date** 28 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Engineering, Operations, Board

**type** Strategy brief (position)

*Sixth of 28 July. A position on why team size has been capped and what changes it. External research is grounded and cited, including evidence that complicates the position. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A position on the oldest constraint in software management and why it may now be movable: **team size has been capped for fifty years not by any limit on how much work people can do but by the cost of keeping them coordinated, which grows quadratically while the work itself does not, and the industry converged independently on the same answer from several directions, the two-pizza team, the observation that adding people to a late project makes it later, and a body of research placing the useful ceiling somewhere between five and ten; underneath that ceiling sits a structural absurdity worth naming plainly, which is that the people who could produce an accurate status were the ones with the least time to do it, the least interest in doing it and the least enjoyment of it, while the people who were good at maintaining communication and status did not understand the work deeply enough to describe it, and were therefore pure overhead on the system, so small teams worked not because they were well organised but because they could skip the apparatus entirely, and ticketing systems scaled the apparatus only to a point and never came close to their promise because the maintenance cost swallowed the benefit; the claim here is that agents can now carry that apparatus, meaning the briefings, the status, the task graphs, the dependency maps, the reports and the meeting preparation, which are the non-functional requirements of working together rather than the work itself, and that this is where the real power of agents lies rather than in generating the deliverables; the position is explicitly not that the machine does the work, because the documents still have to be approved, the risks still have to be accepted, and the tasks still have to be delivered by people, and the system governs itself precisely because those people notice when what is written about their work is wrong; and if the coordination tax falls then the ceiling it produced should move, which means larger teams become viable, juniors become viable in numbers that have not made sense for years, and coordination between teams rather than merely inside them becomes tractable, with dependency mapping, the single largest overhead in multi-team work, becoming a graph that can be maintained rather than a document that goes stale.** It is the sixth document of 28 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.50 freelance-next-steps brief, the v0.33.49 consulting-model brief, the v0.33.53 scenarios-are-the-MVP brief, the v0.33.49 fractal-registers brief, and the v0.33.48 fractal-semantic-graphs brief). New contributions: **the diagnosis that the reporting burden was assigned to exactly the wrong people in both directions, the reframing of coordination artefacts as non-functional requirements that agents can carry, the argument that the artefacts were never wrong and only ever too expensive, the self-governing property that errors surface because people notice claims about their own work, and the claim that the team-size ceiling is a function of coordination cost rather than a human constant.**

## The Diagnosis: The Burden Was On The Wrong People, In Both Directions

The sharpest part of the argument is not about agents at all. It is about why status reporting has always been bad, and it is a structural point rather than a complaint about individuals. The project lead: **"in the past there was an almost highly inefficient model, which is that the people who could actually provide the updates and provide the status, and who were supposed to maintain the status, were the people who had the least amount of time, with the least amount of interest in doing that, and in fact enjoyed doing that the least."**

And the other half, which is the part usually left unsaid. The project lead: **"the people who could be good at promoting and managing communication and managing status did not have enough technical expertise, did not understand in a deep way what's going on, and fundamentally were a massive overhead to the system."**

So the task was split between a group who could do it and would not, and a group who would do it and could not. Every project management methodology of the last three decades is an attempt to work around that split, and none of them dissolved it.

The consequence was that small teams worked by avoiding the apparatus rather than by mastering it. The project lead: **"whenever you have a small team, the communication was straightforward, the decision-making process was straightforward, it was efficient because it was inefficient."** That paradox is exactly right: a small team is efficient because it can afford to be informal, not because it has better process.

And the tooling never closed the gap. The project lead: **"ticketing systems like Jira and ServiceNow and others, even GitHub, scale up to a point but require a huge amount of overhead, a lot of maintenance; it did work to some degree, especially in some of the more technical elements, but it never really lived to fulfil its potential."**

## What The Ceiling Actually Rests On

The external position is well established and worth stating precisely, because the argument here depends on what the ceiling is made of.

Fred Brooks published the observation in 1975 that adding people to a late software project makes it later, a formulation he himself called an outrageous oversimplification. The mechanisms behind it are three. New people take time to become productive and consume the time of those who train them. Much software work does not partition, which Brooks put as the observation that a child takes nine months regardless of how many people are assigned. And communication channels grow as the square of headcount, at n times n minus one over two, so a team of five carries ten channels, a team of ten carries forty five, and fifty people carry over twelve hundred.

The convergence the memo notes is real. Amazon institutionalised the countermeasure as the two-pizza team, on the reasoning that if two pizzas cannot feed the team it is too large, giving roughly six to ten. Independent research lands in the same region from different directions, with one classic study placing the ideal near five, engagement research finding manager effectiveness peaking around eight or nine direct reports, and the Ringelmann effect, confirmed across a large meta-analysis, showing individual effort declining as group size grows. Beyond roughly ten, process loss reliably exceeds the value of additional perspectives.

The important thing about that list is what it is made of. Only one of the three Brooks mechanisms is about people. **The quadratic term is about coordination, and coordination is a cost that a tool can pay.**

## Open Source Already Broke It Once

There is a precedent for exactly this argument and it strengthens the position considerably. Linus Torvalds has challenged Brooks's Law on the grounds that open source is an observable counterexample, with the Linux kernel sustaining on the order of a thousand contributors and consistent growth.

What is instructive is how that was achieved. It was not by making individual contributors dramatically more productive. It was by changing the coordination mechanism: distributed version control removed the need for synchronous integration, asynchronous review removed the need for meetings, modular architecture reduced the number of channels that had to carry anything, and a maintainer hierarchy meant no contributor needed a channel to every other contributor. The quadratic term was attacked directly, and the ceiling moved by two orders of magnitude.

That is the shape of the claim being made here. The argument is not that agents make people faster. It is that agents pay the coordination tax, which is the same move open source made with different technology.

## Agents Carry The Apparatus, Not The Work

The reframing that follows is the useful one. The project lead: **"this is where I feel is the real power of the agents, to allow all these non-functional requirements, all these overheads."** Treating briefings, status, task graphs and dependency maps as non-functional requirements is exactly the right category: they are not the product, nobody wants them for their own sake, and the system fails without them.

On meetings specifically, the memo makes a distinction usually collapsed. The project lead: **"everybody talks that meetings are pointless or meetings are a waste of time, but nobody prepares for it; meetings are super amazing if you have the right people with the right information, with the right briefing, with the right focus, with the right agenda, and you can get a lot of stuff done."** With a precise account of when a meeting is genuinely waste. The project lead: **"the meeting always feels like a waste of space when the context is not relevant to you, or you're not getting value from it, or you're not contributing."** And an observation that ties it back to team size. The project lead: **"that's what happens when you have a smaller team, you just have informal meetings, it's still meetings, but it just doesn't feel like a meeting."** A small team is not meeting-free. It is meeting-prepared by default, because everyone already has the context.

The precedent named is a good one. The project lead: **"this is what Amazon did very well with the whole press release and working backwards documents, I think those are brilliant documents to make sure everybody's aligned; now we can maintain them, and now they can be edited, maintained."** The insight there is not that the document is good, which was already known, but that maintaining it was the cost that stopped most organisations adopting it.

## The Artefacts Were Never Wrong, Only Expensive

This is the line that generalises furthest. The project lead: **"there's a reason why these things were invented; the problem was the overhead of them."**

Applied across the list the memo gives, profit and loss statements, progress reports, infographics before and after, task management, issues, documentation, architecture diagrams, flows, code, strategic risks and objectives and key results, the observation is that none of these were bad ideas. Each was invented because it solved something. Each was abandoned or done badly because maintaining it cost more than the organisation could bear. If the maintenance cost collapses, the sensible response is to reinstate them rather than to invent new ones.

```
   THE OLD TRADE                        THE PROPOSED TRADE
   artefact is valuable                 artefact is still valuable
   maintaining it is expensive          agent maintains it
   so it is skipped or goes stale       so it stays current
   so the team stays small              so the ceiling can move
```

## What People Still Do

The memo is careful here and the care should be preserved, because the position is easy to overstate. The project lead: **"this is not to say that AI does everything, it's the contrary; what the AI does is makes it a lot more efficient."** And the division is explicit. The project lead: **"the documents still have to be approved and maintained, and the risks have to all be accepted, and the tasks have to be delivered."**

That maps directly onto the corpus's own position. Acceptance is a human act performed by a named person with the standing to perform it, and no amount of generated documentation changes who is accountable. The agent produces the artefact; the person carries the consequence.

There is also a self-correcting property worth naming. The project lead: **"this is a good example of the system will govern itself once you put tasks and activities for people, because if it's wrong, people will complain or notice that it's not correct."** That is a real mechanism and a cheap one: people are reliable detectors of inaccurate claims about their own work. It has a limit, which is that it only catches errors in the parts somebody reads and cares about, and that limit is worth stating alongside the mechanism.

## Bigger Teams, And More Juniors

The conclusion follows from the premise. The project lead: **"I can now go to a team and not worry about the overhead of management I give to that team."** And the scaling claim. The project lead: **"I think you can now have much bigger teams, you can add a lot more people; in fact, you can add a lot more juniors to teams in ways that makes them a lot more effective and productive."**

The between-teams case is where the memo sees the most upside, and it is the harder problem. The project lead: **"the challenge to make decisions is always to make sure you have the correct alignment, you have the right people at the right time, all the dependency mappings."** With the specific claim that the largest cost is now addressable. The project lead: **"even creating all the dependencies is a massive overhead, but now we can do that, and we can manage that, and we can graph it, and we can use semantic graphs for it."** Dependency mapping is the right thing to name, because it is the artefact that decays fastest and whose decay is least visible until something blocks.

## What The Evidence Says, And What It Does Not Measure

The junior claim is the most testable thing in this position and the evidence is genuinely mixed, so it should be set out rather than selected from.

**Supporting it.** A 2026 analysis reports pull request productivity gains of roughly forty percent for junior developers against roughly seven percent for seniors, with effects strongest for newly hired and previously lower-productivity developers. Telemetry across more than ten thousand developers and over a thousand teams found high-adoption teams interacting with nine percent more tasks and forty seven percent more pull requests per day.

**Complicating it, seriously.** A separate dataset reports the opposite ordering, with senior engineers capturing nearly five times the gains of juniors, on the reasoning that seniors know when to override a suggestion and juniors do not. In the same dataset, pull requests authored with AI took over four times longer to clear review, because somebody senior still checks the work. A 2025 paper found that less experienced developers increased output while experienced maintainers absorbed the additional review burden, concluding that apparent gains shifted work from juniors to seniors rather than removing it. Independent review of several hundred real pull requests found AI-generated code carried roughly seventy percent more issues than human-written code.

**And the headline number moved.** A randomised controlled trial published in July 2025 found experienced open-source developers were nineteen percent slower with AI assistance, against their own prediction of a twenty four percent speedup and a post-hoc belief that they had been twenty percent faster. The same evaluation is reported to have reversed to roughly an eighteen percent speedup a year later. A learning curve of thirty to a hundred hours before clearly positive effects is reported, which would explain part of the reversal.

**The labour market is currently moving the other way.** Junior developer job postings are reported down around sixty percent from their 2022 peak, with employment for developers aged twenty two to twenty five down nearly twenty percent between late 2022 and mid 2025, on the stated reasoning that a senior with an assistant outperforms a senior plus a junior.

The honest reading is that the memo's junior claim is contrarian against both the market and part of the evidence. But there is a real distinction that rescues it from the strongest objection, and it should be stated clearly: **almost all of this evidence measures individual output, and the position here is about the coordination term.** The studies ask whether a developer with an assistant writes code faster. The claim here asks whether a team of fifteen with maintained briefings, live task graphs and current dependency maps coordinates as cheaply as a team of seven without them. Nobody appears to have measured that. So the position is not contradicted by the evidence so much as untested by it, and the review-burden findings are the warning worth taking seriously, because burden shifted from juniors to senior reviewers is a coordination cost relocated rather than removed, which is precisely the Brooks mechanism wearing new clothes.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim that agents do the work.** They carry the apparatus around the work; people approve, accept and deliver.
- **Not a productivity claim about individuals.** The argument is about the coordination term, not about typing speed.
- **Not a silver bullet.** Brooks argued explicitly that no single development yields an order of magnitude in a decade, and that objection stands unrefuted.
- **Not evidence-backed on its central claim.** The team-size effect is untested; what exists measures something adjacent.
- **Not an argument against small teams.** It is an argument that the ceiling is a cost, and costs can change.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Coordination cost falls versus review cost rises | The strongest counter-evidence is that AI-assisted work shifts burden onto senior reviewers, which is a coordination cost relocated rather than removed, and is the Brooks mechanism in a new form |
| The junior claim versus the labour market | Hiring is moving sharply against adding juniors, on a stated mechanism that directly contradicts this position; being contrarian is defensible and should be argued rather than assumed |
| No Silver Bullet | Brooks specifically claimed no single development delivers an order of magnitude within a decade, and this position claims something close to that |
| Self-governing versus what nobody reads | People catch errors about their own work, which means errors in the parts nobody owns or reads survive indefinitely, and those are the ones that matter in coordination |
| Maintained artefacts versus trusted artefacts | An always-current document is only useful if people believe it, and cheaply generated material can lose credibility faster than stale hand-written material |
| More artefacts versus more noise | Removing the cost of producing reports removes the discipline that scarcity imposed, and an organisation can drown in current, accurate, unread documents |
| Open source as precedent versus disanalogy | Linux scaled through modularity and a maintainer hierarchy, meaning it reduced the channels rather than servicing them; whether servicing channels scales the same way is unproven |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |

| What is the actual new ceiling? | The claim implies the number moves, and nobody has measured where to |
| How would this be tested? | A team-level rather than individual-level measurement, comparing coordination cost at matched sizes with and without agent-maintained apparatus |
| Does review burden eat the gain? | The central empirical risk, and the one the existing evidence points at most directly |
