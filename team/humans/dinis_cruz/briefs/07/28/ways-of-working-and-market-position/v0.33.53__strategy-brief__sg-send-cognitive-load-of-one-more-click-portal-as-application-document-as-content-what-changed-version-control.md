## What This Is

on cognitive load, the point can be made precisely rather than as a complaint, because working memory is a fixed and small resource whose concurrent processing capacity is measured in a handful of chunks over a span of seconds, and the established distinction is between the load intrinsic to the material, which for seven people's interlocking roles is real and irreducible, the germane load that actually builds understanding, and the extraneous load imposed purely by how something is presented, and since all three draw on the same fixed pool, the account resolution and the folder navigation and the wondering whether this is current are not minor irritations but a direct subtraction from the attention available to absorb the content, which is why the infographic that was asked for is a particularly sharp case, since an infographic exists to reduce load and was delivered in a way that added it; and on version control, the decisive gap is that a drive can tell you that something changed and who touched it, while version control tells you what changed, which matters because after the first reading the only question anyone has about a roles document is what moved, so a portal makes every reading a first reading, cannot produce a citable version, cannot let the document and the infographic change as one reviewable unit even though they must always agree, and cannot answer when a responsibility became yours or who decided, which for a corpus built on named accountability is not a usability complaint but a governance gap.** It is the seventh document of 28 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.53 scaling-collaboration brief, the v0.33.53 regulation-graph brief, the v0.33.50 freelance-next-steps brief, the v0.33.49 open-source-as-strategy brief, and the v0.33.51 distributed vault architecture brief). New contributions: **the portal-as-application versus document-as-content distinction, cognitive load stated in terms of extraneous load displacing germane load from a fixed pool, the observation that an infographic is a load-reduction device undone by its delivery, the identification of what-changed as the only question that matters on a second reading, atomic change across a document and its diagram as a requirement rather than a nicety, and a roles document without history treated as an accountability gap.**

## The Exchange

The request was ordinary. With the group now at seven people, could there be a document and an infographic setting out everybody's roles and responsibilities. The answer was a cloud drive folder link, pasted into the chat and pinned. The reply was a joke. The project lead: **"GDrive doesn't work on my laptop, it only likes GitHub or WhatsApp."**

The drive is not broken. What the joke encodes is that the message did not deliver anything. Seven people learned that a document exists somewhere. The pin is worth noticing too: a link needed pinning because it would otherwise be lost in the scroll, which is an admission that the place it was published is not a place where things stay findable.

## The Experience: An Application Versus A Document

The crispest way to state the difference is that **a portal arrives as an application and a document arrives as content.**

Following the drive link starts an interface. There is a load, then a chrome, then a file listing. Nothing you have seen yet is the material you were sent for. You are in a file manager, performing information architecture on somebody else's behalf, and the actual content is at least one further action away, quite possibly in a different application again. On a machine with more than one account signed in, which is most working machines, there is a prior step of establishing which identity you are currently wearing and whether it is the one with access.

A rendered file in a repository is the material. The page that loads is the document, formatted, with its diagrams inline next to the sentences that reference them. You are reading within about a second. There is no listing to interpret, no handoff, and for anything public no identity to resolve.

That difference is not a preference between tools. It is the difference between being handed a thing and being handed directions to a thing.

## The Cognitive Load, Named Properly

This can be stated with more precision than irritation, and the precision makes it a stronger argument.

Working memory is a small, fixed resource. Research in this tradition puts the number of chunks it can hold at around seven, the number it can actively process concurrently at nearer two to four, and its unaided duration at roughly twenty seconds. Cognitive load theory, developed by John Sweller from the late 1980s, divides the demand on that resource into three kinds that are additive and that compete for the same capacity.

**Intrinsic load** is the inherent difficulty of the material. For a roles-and-responsibilities document across seven people this is genuinely high, because roles are element-interactive: each one only means anything in relation to the others, so they have to be held simultaneously rather than read in sequence. This load cannot be reduced without changing the content.

**Germane load** is the effort that actually builds understanding, the work of forming a durable mental model of who does what.

**Extraneous load** is demand imposed purely by how the material is presented, unrelated to the content, and it is the one that good design removes.

The consequence is the whole argument. Because the three draw on one fixed pool, **extraneous load does not merely annoy the reader, it displaces the germane load they were about to spend on the content.** Resolving which account, interpreting a folder listing, opening a second application, and wondering whether this file is the current one are all extraneous. Each is small. Together they can consume most of a working memory whose concurrent capacity is a few chunks and whose span is seconds, and they are spent before the reader has encountered a single role. The material with the high intrinsic load then arrives at a reader who has nothing left to give it.

```
   ONE FIXED POOL OF WORKING MEMORY
   +------------------------------------------------------+
   | intrinsic: seven interlocking roles, held together   |  irreducible
   | germane:   actually building the mental model        |  what you wanted
   | extraneous: which account / which folder / which app |  pure waste
   +------------------------------------------------------+
        extraneous crowds out germane. it does not sit beside it.
```

## The Infographic Is The Sharpest Case

There is a particular irony in what was asked for. An infographic is a cognitive load reduction device. The research prescription that images combined with a small amount of text reduce extraneous load and free capacity for understanding is one of the better-established findings in this literature, and it is precisely why a request for roles and responsibilities asks for a diagram alongside the prose.

Delivering that infographic behind a portal spends the load it was built to save. The diagram exists to let seven people hold seven interlocking roles at once. Reaching it costs several chunks of the capacity required to do so. The artefact and its delivery are working against each other.

## What Changed? The Question A Portal Cannot Answer

This is the gap the other two follow from, and it is not fixed by naming the folder better.

A cloud drive has something called version history. It can tell you **that** a file changed and **who** touched it and roughly **when**. Version control tells you **what** changed. Those are not the same capability and the difference decides whether the document is usable more than once.

After the first reading, the only question anyone has about a roles document is what moved. Nobody re-reads seven people's responsibilities from the top to discover that one line changed. So a store that cannot express a diff makes **every reading a first reading**, which means in practice that the second reading does not happen, which means the document is authoritative and unread simultaneously. That is the worst state a shared document can be in, and it is the normal state of documents in portals.

Four specific things follow from the absence:

**No citable version.** You cannot refer to the roles as they stood on a particular date in a way anyone else can retrieve. Every reference is to a moving target.

**No review before it becomes authoritative.** The pinned link was canonical the moment it was pinned. Nobody approved anything, because there was no mechanism through which approval could have happened.

**No line-level attribution.** You cannot ask when a responsibility became yours, or who decided that. The history records that a file was edited, not that a duty moved from one person to another.

**No atomic change.** Which is significant enough to take separately.

## The Document And The Diagram Have To Change Together

The request was for a document **and** an infographic. Those two artefacts must always agree, because they describe the same seven roles. If a responsibility moves, both are wrong until both are updated.

Version control treats that as one change: a single commit touching both files, reviewable as a unit, either applied or not. A portal treats it as two independent edits to two unrelated objects, with an unbounded window in which the prose and the picture disagree and nothing anywhere records that they are supposed to match. Anyone reading the diagram during that window is reading something false, and there is no mechanism by which they could know.

The generalisation is that a diagram which is a flat export is a picture of a fact as it stood on the day somebody exported it, whereas a diagram generated from a file that lives beside the prose is a projection of something maintained, and the maintenance is visible.

## Roles Without History Is An Accountability Gap

For this corpus specifically, the version control point is not a matter of engineering taste. The corpus has spent weeks arguing that accountability requires a named person, a recorded decision, a date, and a chain back to evidence. A roles-and-responsibilities document is the most basic instance of that: it is the artefact that says who is accountable for what.

Held in a store that cannot say what changed, when, or on whose authority, it cannot support the thing it exists to do. Someone can discover a responsibility is theirs without ever having been told, without a record of when it moved, and with no way to establish who decided. That is the same defect the corpus names elsewhere as a risk accepted by nobody, appearing here in the most mundane possible document.

## A Note On Clicks

The obvious framing of this complaint is about click counts, and it is worth setting aside because the evidence does not support it. The widely repeated rule that anything should be reachable in three clicks was never grounded in research, and a study analysing more than eight thousand clicks found no relationship between the number of clicks and either task success or satisfaction, with some people persisting through twenty five and others abandoning after two. What predicts persistence is whether a person can tell where a link goes before they follow it.

That refinement supports the argument made above rather than replacing it. Clicks are cheap. What is expensive is the deciding, the waiting, the identity resolution, and the uncertainty, which is to say the extraneous load. An opaque identifier and an account index belonging to the sender's own session are expensive for exactly that reason, and a path that names the repository, the branch, the folder, the file and its format is nearly free.

## The Two Modes That Work

Read carefully, the joke named the only two shapes that survive a busy group.

**Push the content into the conversation.** No navigation, no identity, no application. The content arrives where the person already is, which is why a diagram pasted into a chat is looked at and the same diagram behind a link is not. The cost is real: the chat is not a source of truth and the material decays into scroll, which is what the pin was trying to compensate for.

**One durable link to a canonical, versioned artefact.** One step, to content rather than to an interface, that renders without an account and stays valid.

Everything between those two is a portal, and a portal manages to combine the navigation cost of a link with the impermanence of a chat.

One further reason this matters more than it did a few years ago, connecting to the position taken earlier today: if agents are to carry the coordination overhead, the artefacts have to live somewhere an agent can read. A repository is trivially readable and citable. A drive behind an interactive sign-in is opaque to an agent in the same way it is opaque to a person in a hurry, so a portal breaks the automated loop as well as the human one.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a complaint about a colleague.** The behaviour is universal, and the artefact was produced quickly and helpfully.
- **Not a click-count argument.** The evidence does not support that framing; the load is in the deciding and the waiting.
- **Not a claim that cloud drives are useless.** They are good at storage and at simultaneous editing, and poor as the destination of a link.
- **Not a demand that everything be markdown.** It is a demand that the canonical copy be readable as content, diffable, and attributable.
- **Not an argument against chat.** Chat is one of the two working modes, with the known cost that it is not a source of truth.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Version control versus who can use it | The argument is easy for engineers and real for everyone else, and a handbook that only engineers can edit is worse than a drive that everyone can |
| Account-free rendering versus private material | Most of the benefit assumes a repository readable without signing in, and a private repository reintroduces the identity resolution this brief objects to |
| Diffable diagrams versus real design work | Diagram-as-code is diffable and limited, while serious visual work happens in tools that emit binaries, and pretending otherwise loses the designers |
| One canonical location versus where people already are | A well-organised repository still loses to the window already open, which is why the push mode has to remain part of the answer |
| Reducing extraneous load versus who pays to reduce it | The load falls on readers and the effort to remove it falls on the sender, which is the same misallocated burden named in this morning's brief |
| Atomic change versus how people actually work | Requiring the prose and the diagram to move in one reviewed change is correct and slower than editing a file, and the discipline will be resented before it is valued |
