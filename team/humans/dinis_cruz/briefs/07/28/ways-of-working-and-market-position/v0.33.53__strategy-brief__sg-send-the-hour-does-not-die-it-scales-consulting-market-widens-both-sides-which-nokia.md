## What This Is

A position against the prevailing account of what artificial intelligence does to professional services, arguing that the market expands rather than collapses: **the widely circulated version says the billable hour is dying, illustrated with images of ruined towers bearing the logos of the large firms, and it rests on an arithmetic claim that inverts what was actually said, because the firm concerned did not replace twenty five thousand of its sixty thousand people with agents, it kept roughly forty thousand humans and added roughly twenty five thousand agents alongside them, which is how the sixty thousand figure was arrived at in the first place, so an addition has been reported as a subtraction, and the same executive's stated ambition is for every human to be enabled by one or more agents, which is a description of leverage per person rising rather than of people being removed; on that reading the hour does not die, it scales, because what falls is the cost of assembling and maintaining context, which is exactly what made small engagements uneconomic and wide talent pools unusable, so both sides of the market widen at once, downward into organisations that could never previously afford a competent engagement, and outward into talent that was previously unreachable because it was remote, part-time, narrowly specialised, junior, or in the wrong country; what does die is a specific and identifiable set of rents rather than a unit of account, namely charging for large volumes of hours, staffing with talent assigned by availability rather than fit, selling on the strength of a logo, and doing so without any measurable return, all four of which are attributes of how incumbents have sold rather than of the service itself; and the question that matters is therefore not whether the large firms survive but which of two historical moments they are living through, because the usual invocation of Nokia flattens a company that has two instructive episodes, one in which a distressed conglomerate divested its entire industrial base to bet on an unproven standard and became the world leader within six years, and one in which the dominant incumbent watched the basis of competition move from devices to ecosystems, treated it as a product problem because product problems were what it had always won, and lost four fifths of its market inside five years.** It is the eighth document of 28 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.49 consulting-model brief, the v0.33.53 scaling-collaboration brief, the v0.33.49 commercialising-the-primitives brief, the v0.33.50 freelance-next-steps brief, and the v0.33.48 vendor-sprawl brief). New contributions: **the correction that agents were added rather than substituted and that the reported arithmetic runs backwards, the position that the hour scales rather than dies, the identification of four dying rents as distinct from the surviving service, the two-sided market expansion argument, and the reframing of the Nokia question from a single cautionary tale into a choice between two documented episodes.**

## The Claim Being Answered, And Its Arithmetic

The post that prompted this said that a major firm is replacing twenty five thousand of its sixty thousand employees with agents, that the billable hour is dying everywhere artificial intelligence touches work, and that the professional pyramid is collapsing into an obelisk with fewer junior bodies and humans retained only where judgement is irreplaceable. It was accompanied by generated images of the large firms' towers in ruins.

The arithmetic does not survive contact with the source. McKinsey's global managing partner, Bob Sternfels, said publicly that the firm's workforce now stands at around sixty thousand, and described that as roughly forty thousand humans **plus** twenty thousand agents, with the agent figure given as closer to twenty five thousand at a separate appearance and subsequently confirmed by the firm as the more accurate number. Eighteen months earlier the count was around three thousand agents. His stated ambition is for every employee to be enabled by at least one or more agents, and he expects agents to reach parity with humans within the year.

So the sixty thousand is humans and agents combined. Saying that twenty five thousand of the sixty thousand were replaced counts the additions and then reports them as removals. The human headcount in these statements does not fall. Separately and much more modestly, the firm did cut around two hundred technology-sector roles in late 2025, citing automation of support tasks, which is real and is two orders of magnitude away from the figure being circulated.

It is also worth noting that the metric itself is contested by the firm's peers. EY's global engineering chief has said the number of agents does not translate to value and that the best returns come from a handful doing the heavy lifting; PwC's chief artificial intelligence officer has said counting agents is probably the wrong measure. A critic of the industry has made the sharper methodological point that there is no standard way to count agents at all, which makes the headline number closer to a marketing artefact than a measurement.

None of that makes the underlying trend imaginary. It does mean the most-shared evidence for the collapse thesis is, read properly, evidence of expansion.

## What Actually Dies Is A Set Of Rents

The position taken in reply separates the unit of account from the business model built on it. The project lead: **"I don't think AI will kill the hour billing, in fact I think it will make it scale."** And then the precise list of what does not survive. The project lead: **"what I think it will do is to break the business model of charging for lots of hours/days, for non-aligned talent, based on brand and without clear ROI."**

Those four are worth separating because each is independently attackable and none of them is the hour:

```
   DIES                                  SURVIVES
   volume of hours as the value          the hour as the unit of account
   talent assigned by availability       talent assigned by fit
   the logo as the guarantee             demonstrated capability
   no measurable return                  a stated, checkable outcome
```

Read as a list, these are rents rather than services. Each is a premium extracted from an information asymmetry: the client could not tell whether the days were necessary, whether the person assigned was the right one, whether the brand was doing any work, or whether the engagement paid for itself. Every one of those asymmetries is reduced by the same technology, which is why the rents are what is under threat. The service underneath is not.

## The Hour Scales

The reason the hour survives is that it was never the problem. What made hours expensive to sell in small quantities was the cost of everything around them: understanding the client's situation, assembling a team with the right context, keeping that team aligned, and producing the artefacts that made the work legible. That overhead had a floor, and the floor set a minimum viable engagement size.

If the overhead falls, the floor falls with it, and the hour becomes sellable in quantities that were previously uneconomic. That is the same argument made earlier today about coordination cost inside teams, applied to the boundary between a client and a provider. The corpus has already designed the model this implies: pooled hours drawn from zero against a ceiling, priced by response time rather than by volume, with a partner working inside the client's own environment, and one provider able to serve several clients at once. That model does not abolish the hour. It makes the hour the only thing being sold, with the padding removed.

## Both Sides Of The Market Widen

This is the part the collapse thesis misses entirely, and it is the substance of the reply. The project lead: **"it will make it scale, widen the talent pool and make it cost effective to hire a much more dynamically assembled, fractal, wider, distributed, diverse, focused, customised, multi-national, multi-cultural, and more important in context with the business requirement and customer needs external consulting team."**

Two distinct expansions are packed into that sentence.

**The demand side expands downward.** If a competent engagement no longer requires a large team on site for weeks, the minimum size of a viable engagement collapses, and organisations that could never previously afford professional advice become addressable. The large firms have always served the top of the market because their cost structure could not reach below it. That constraint is what is actually being removed.

**The supply side expands outward.** If context can be assembled and maintained cheaply, then talent that was previously unusable becomes usable: remote, part-time, narrowly specialised, junior, not operating in the client's first language, or located somewhere with a lower cost base. The word doing the most work in that sentence is **in context**, because the historical reason distributed talent underperformed was never capability, it was that nobody could afford to keep it briefed.

Both movements increase the number of engagements and the number of possible providers. The compression of hours per project is real and is only one term in the equation; the collapse thesis reads that term and stops.

## Which Nokia? There Are Two, And They Teach Opposite Lessons

The closing question in the reply is the one worth developing. The project lead: **"the question is can these consulting companies adapt and which version of Nokia will they be?"**

Nokia is normally invoked as a single cautionary tale, which wastes it, because the company has two documented episodes and they point in opposite directions.

**Nokia when mobile arrived, 1992 to 1998.** In the late 1980s Nokia was a financially distressed conglomerate making paper, rubber boots, tyres, cables and televisions, with its electronics division consuming capital. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 pushed Finland into deep recession and Nokia toward insolvency. Jorma Ollila, appointed chief executive in 1992, chose aggressive divestment: he sold the power, rubber and cable businesses, the divisions that had been the company's financial bedrock, and bet the entire company on the then-unproven GSM standard. It was widely regarded as an absurd gamble, since Nokia was a minor player behind Motorola and Ericsson. By 1998 Nokia was the largest mobile phone manufacturer in the world with over forty percent of the global market, and by 2007 was approaching half of it. Ollila's own account of the period is subtitled as a journey from near catastrophe to global success.

**Nokia when the iPhone arrived, 2007 to 2013.** Nokia was the dominant incumbent, with the largest share, the best supply chain and the deepest distribution. It had touchscreen and internet-device projects internally, and reporting indicates middle management repeatedly killed them on the grounds that they threatened the existing hardware business. The chief executive dismissed the iPhone as a niche product. Executives, insulated by optimistic internal reporting and by still-enormous sales, expected forthcoming devices to see Apple off. Stephen Elop, arriving in 2010, correctly diagnosed that Nokia was losing a war of ecosystems rather than of devices, though his chosen response remains one of the most contested strategic decisions on record. Within five years the share had fallen from around forty percent to under five. The share price went from about forty dollars in late 2007 to under two by mid 2012. The devices business was sold to Microsoft, agreed in 2013 and completed in 2014, for something over five billion euros; the buyer wrote off more than the purchase price the following year.

So the question put to the large firms is precise: **are you at your 1992 moment or your 2007 moment?** The first is an incumbent in difficulty that dismantles its own foundations to bet on a new basis of competition and wins outright. The second is an incumbent at maximum strength that can see the shift, has every asset needed to meet it, and loses anyway.

## The Sharpest Part: The Earlier Win Caused The Later Loss

There is a further layer that makes the comparison genuinely useful rather than merely cautionary, and it comes from the academic treatment of the period, which frames it as a curse of agility.

Nokia had beaten competitive threats before by restructuring. In 2003, facing Motorola's slim handsets and cheaper Asian component supply, its share fell from thirty five percent to thirty one; management reorganised, optimised production and logistics, cut costs and time to market, widened the range, and recovered the leading position quickly. That worked. It worked so well that it became the institutional reflex.

When the iPhone arrived, Nokia applied the same reflex, because a product-competition problem was the kind of problem it knew how to win. But the shift was not about devices. It was about software ecosystems, developers and platform economics, and no amount of production optimisation addressed it. **The pattern that saved the company in 2003 is what prevented it from seeing 2007 correctly.**

That is the actual risk to the large professional firms, and it is more specific than complacency. Their institutional reflex is to restructure, reprice, retrain and reorganise, and they are very good at it. If the shift is a change in the basis of competition rather than a change in cost structure, that competence is precisely what will produce the wrong answer confidently.

## And There Is A Third Nokia

Worth recording, because it changes what the question is asking. Nokia lost handsets and did not die. It reorganised around network infrastructure and patent licensing, acquired its way into scale in that market, and continues as a substantial business with revenue in the tens of billions and a workforce in the tens of thousands, having gone through further large reductions as its own market cycled.

So the question is not survival. It is what you will be on the other side, and whether you chose it or had it chosen for you. A large firm that emerges as something smaller and different has not been destroyed; it has been repositioned, possibly against its will. The towers in the illustration are the least likely outcome.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a denial that the model is changing.** The four rents named here are genuinely under threat.
- **Not a defence of the incumbents.** The position is that the market grows, not that the current holders capture the growth.
- **Not a claim that the reported figures are fabricated.** The statements are real; the arithmetic applied to them ran backwards.
- **Not a prediction.** The Nokia question is a diagnostic offered to the firms, not a forecast about them.
- **Not a comment on any named individual's argument.** The correction is to the arithmetic, which was widely repeated rather than invented.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Agents added versus what happens next | The correction holds for the statements made, and adding agents while holding headcount flat is exactly what precedes a reduction, so the collapse thesis may be early rather than wrong |
| The hour scales versus what clients want to buy | Buyers who have started asking for outcomes may not accept a metered hour however honest it is, and the corpus has already noted that outcome pricing and consumption pricing pull differently |
| Market expansion versus who captures it | Both sides widening is a claim about the market, not about the incumbents, and the same argument that predicts expansion predicts new entrants taking most of it |
| Wider talent pool versus the junior evidence | The supply-side argument depends on previously unusable talent becoming usable, and the evidence reviewed earlier today on juniors is genuinely mixed, with review burden shifting to seniors |
| Two Nokias versus hindsight | Both episodes are legible now and were not at the time, which is the whole difficulty; asking a firm which moment it is in gives it no way to answer honestly |
| Criticising a viral post versus its underlying instinct | The arithmetic was wrong and the direction of travel it points at is not, and a correction that reads as a defence of the status quo would be the wrong lesson |
