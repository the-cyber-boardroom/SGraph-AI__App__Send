# The Scenarios Are The MVP: A Shareable Vault That Walks A User From Do You Have An Agent To A Risk Register At Every Altitude

**version** v0.33.53
**date** 28 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Engineering, Design, Commercial, Board

**type** Architecture brief

*Third of 28 July, and the synthesis of the plug work, the regulation graph, and the fractal registers into a single deliverable. Regulatory positions are grounded, cited, factual, and not legal advice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

## What This Is

The definition of the first end-to-end deliverable, which is not a feature but a small set of worked scenarios: **the pull the plug work needs three or four scenarios running from simple to complicated, wired end to end, and those scenarios are the MVP, because the pieces have been circling each other for weeks and the arrival of the compliance layer is what lets them connect, so that a user can be asked whether they run an agent in production and be walked from that question to an owned risk register; the sequence of primitives now exists and is short, do you have this kind of agent, here is the regulation that applies to it, who can pull the plug and can you actually stop it, here are the five domains that matter because stopping is only one of them, and here are the regulations that bite if you do have this, with a visualisation that reflects a risk register back as the questions are answered; the register that comes back is not flat, and this is the part that matters most, because risks are confirmed at some altitudes and accepted at others, a technical stakeholder can confirm that an agent holds a capability but cannot accept the business exposure it creates, a board can accept an exposure but cannot confirm the technical fact underneath it, and a compliance function validates whether the cited obligation genuinely applies, which makes confirmation, validation and acceptance three separate predicates each held at its own altitude rather than one act performed once; the artefact carrying all of this is a vault, published as a static site, holding its state in the browser's local storage so that no backend is required and the recipient can use it without provisioning anything, which makes the same object the demo, the marketing piece and the thing a user actually keeps, sent to somebody with nothing more than an instruction to follow it, on the principle that the best explanation of a graph is the experience of using one; and the compliance layer has to be plural from the first build rather than European only, because the same system can sit inside several regimes at once with different triggers, different enforcement models and different remedies, which is precisely the condition a graph exists to represent and a checklist cannot.** It is the third document of 28 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.53 regulation-graph brief, the v0.33.51 plug-profile series plan, the v0.33.49 fractal-registers brief, the v0.33.49 demo and MVP workflow brief, and the v0.33.40 RAMM brief). New contributions: **scenarios named as the MVP rather than as demo content, the primitive question sequence from agent to register, the separation of confirmation, validation and acceptance as three altitude-scoped predicates, the compliance function introduced as a validating stakeholder, the vault-as-static-site as simultaneously demo, marketing artefact and usable tool, and the requirement that the regulation layer be multi-jurisdictional from the first build.**

## The Scenarios Are The Deliverable

The framing that changes the shape of the work is that the scenarios are not illustrations of a product; they are the product's first version. The project lead: **"we need to come up with a set of scenarios that fundamentally is our MVP, they become our first deliverable of an end-to-end solution."** And the reason it is possible now rather than a month ago is the compliance layer landing underneath. The project lead: **"we've been close, but I think we're now starting to zoom in on a piece of the puzzle, especially once we have the compliance element to it, where we can start to connect the dots."** The shape is a small graded set. The project lead: **"maybe come up with three or four scenarios that are wired in, and from simple to more complicated."**

A ladder that fits the material the corpus already holds would run roughly:

| # | Scenario | What it adds |
|---|----------|--------------|
| 1 | An agent runs in production and can change something that matters | The base case: who stops it, how fast |
| 2 | The same agent, and nobody ever accepted it | No mandate: the exposure has no owner and rolls up |
| 3 | The plug exists but taking it means taking the service down | Blast radius and side effects: the plug nobody will pull |
| 4 | The worst outcome cannot be undone, and an obligation applies to it | Recoverability at zero, plus the regulation that makes it a duty |

Each adds one dimension rather than a new concept, so a reader who understood the first understands the fourth.

## The Primitive Sequence

The question flow is short enough to state in a line, which is what makes it a demo rather than an implementation. The project lead: **"we have the scenario, which is: do you have this kind of agents? Do you, here's the regulation that now you know, so now we're going to talk about who can pull the plug. Can you stop it? And here are the five domains that matter. And then here are the regulations that matter if you have this."** With the output arriving as the user answers. The project lead: **"it's literally about a visualisation that as you answer those questions, you then reflect the risk register."**

```
   do you run an agent that can change something?
        |
        v  here is the regulation that touches it
   who can pull the plug? can you actually stop it?
        |
        v  the five domains, because stopping is only one
   who / blast radius / speed / side effects / recoverability
        |
        v  and here is what bites if you do have this
   A RISK REGISTER, reflected back, at every altitude
```

The insistence that stopping is only part of it is worth preserving as written. The project lead: **"remember that it is not just pulling the plug, it's about handling all the other stuff."** That is the five-dimension profile doing its job: the plug question opens the conversation and the profile is what the conversation is actually about.

## Confirmed, Validated, Accepted: Three Predicates At Three Altitudes

This is the most consequential idea in the memo and it sharpens something the corpus has held loosely. The project lead: **"this is very important, the multiple altitudes of the risk register, because there might be risks that are only accepted at certain altitudes, or might be risks that are only confirmed at certain altitudes."**

Confirmation and acceptance are different acts, they answer different questions, and they are held by different people:

**Confirmation** is factual. Does this agent actually hold that capability, reach that system, reify that path? Only someone close to the system can answer, and their answer is true or false rather than agreeable or not. A database administrator can confirm that an agent holds write access. They cannot accept what it means for the business.

**Acceptance** is a judgement about appetite. Are we willing to carry this, and for how long? Only someone with the standing to commit the organisation can perform it. A board can accept an exposure and has no way to confirm the technical fact underneath it.

The memo then adds a third, which has not previously had a name in the corpus. The project lead: **"we probably also want the GRC person to validate the risks, especially to do with the compliance element."** **Validation** asks whether the cited obligation genuinely applies to this system in this jurisdiction. It is neither a technical fact nor an appetite judgement; it is an interpretive act, and it belongs to a compliance function.

```
   CONFIRMED   "this is true"          technical stakeholder    fact
   VALIDATED   "this rule applies"     compliance / GRC         interpretation
   ACCEPTED    "we will carry it"      business owner, upward   judgement

   a risk can be confirmed and not validated, validated and not accepted,
   or accepted at one altitude and unconfirmed at the one below it
```

The register therefore has to track three states per risk per altitude rather than one, and the interesting cases are the mismatches. A risk accepted by an executive but never confirmed by anyone technical is an acceptance of something that may not be true. A risk confirmed and validated but accepted nowhere is the unaccepted-is-critical case the corpus already holds. Making all three visible is what turns the register from a list into a picture of where an organisation actually is.

## The Path From User To Evidence

The memo states the purpose of the whole flow in one clause. The project lead: **"the whole logic here is to show the path between the user, so the fact, and the evidence, in this case the compliance."** That is the sentence the demo should be built to satisfy. A user answers a question about their own estate, which produces a fact; the fact attaches to a risk; the risk cites an obligation in the regulation graph; and the obligation resolves to a published source with provenance. Every step is inspectable, which is the force-of-proof property the corpus has argued for throughout, appearing here as a user journey rather than an architecture.

And the journey does not stop at the person answering. The project lead: **"we have the flows of the multiple stakeholders all the way to the board."** With a conclusion available at the top that is genuinely useful. The project lead: **"from a board point, exec point of view, you can be compliant or not, and that will depend on the facts, for example, if your system is defined high risk or not."**

## The Vault Is The Marketing

The distribution mechanism collapses three artefacts into one, and it works because of an architectural property the platform already has. The project lead: **"we can host vaults as static web pages, so we can have a vault, and this vault can also be used on a website, which we can use for demos."** The state lives client-side. The project lead: **"as long as we store the data in local storage, you don't need a vault in order to materialise it."**

That means the same object is the demo, the thing sent to a prospect, and the thing they keep using. The project lead: **"think about how can we create this vault as almost our marketing exercise, so that I can send that to somebody and say, hey, here it is, now you follow this."** And the experience carries the argument rather than a deck. The project lead: **"that experience should guide the user to the concept, this is going back to the whole idea that the best presentation and the best way to explain is for somebody to use and to experience the graphs."**

This resolves a tension the 17 July demo brief left open, which was whether the airgapped demo was a sales asset or a deliverable. The answer here is that it is neither and both: it is a shareable artefact with no backend, no signup and no provisioning, which a recipient can forward, and which happens to also be the product's first version.

## The Regulation Layer Has To Be Plural From The Start

The memo closes on a correction to yesterday's scope and it is the right one. The project lead: **"that's just the EU Act, because there's also US regulation, so it's important to start taking these into account under multiple regulations."**

The current position makes this more than a completeness point. There is no comprehensive federal AI statute in the United States, so binding private-sector obligations sit mostly in state law, and that layer is both large and unstable: reporting counts one hundred and forty five state AI laws enacted across thirty eight states during 2025, against more than two thousand bills introduced. Frameworks in California, Texas, Illinois and Utah are reported in force, with California's frontier transparency act becoming operative in August 2026. A federal preemption push has been live since an executive order of 11 December 2025, with a Justice Department litigation task force stood up in January 2026, and as of mid-2026 no federal statute or court is reported to have preempted or paused any state law.

Three features of that landscape are exactly what a graph is for and a checklist cannot hold.

**The same system sits in several regimes at once.** A hiring tool used for remote roles is reported to trigger Illinois, New York City and Colorado obligations simultaneously. There is no single answer to whether it is compliant, only an answer per jurisdiction.

**Enforcement models differ, so identical technology carries different risk.** Colorado and Texas are described as attorney-general enforcement with cure periods, while Illinois carries a private right of action, which adds class action exposure. Same system, same facts, materially different consequence, which is a property of the jurisdiction rather than of the system, and therefore an edge rather than an attribute.

**Dates move, and one regime was repealed before it applied.** Colorado's 2024 AI Act, which sat on compliance roadmaps for a June 2026 date, was repealed and replaced in May 2026 by a narrower automated-decision statute whose substantive obligations begin in January 2027. Any static mapping built against the original is now wrong. This is the strongest possible argument for yesterday's conclusion that dates belong in the graph as versioned properties rather than as text.

One further property deserves a node type of its own: a voluntary framework can acquire legal effect in one jurisdiction. Texas is reported to grant enforcement protection to organisations substantially complying with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which turns a non-binding framework into something with a defensible legal position attached in that state and nowhere else. A crosswalk that treats frameworks and statutes as the same kind of thing will miss that entirely.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a feature.** Three or four wired scenarios are the deliverable, not a demonstration of one.
- **Not a compliance verdict.** The flow shows which obligations touch a system; whether it complies is validated by a person, not asserted by the tool.
- **Not a single-jurisdiction artefact.** Plural regulation from the first build, because the same system sits in several regimes.
- **Not a backend product.** A vault published as a static site with state in local storage, requiring nothing of the recipient.
- **Not a flat register.** Three predicates at three altitudes, and the mismatches are the interesting part.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Scenarios as MVP versus scenarios as sales collateral | Naming the demo as the deliverable is clarifying and risks the first version being judged as marketing rather than product, which is the exact criticism the corpus has already absorbed once |
| Three predicates versus a usable interface | Confirmation, validation and acceptance at multiple altitudes is correct and is a lot to show a first-time user without losing them in the first minute |
| Self-guiding experience versus needing guidance | The claim that using the graph is the best explanation is strong and untested, and a shareable artefact that arrives without a human alongside it has to carry the whole argument alone |
| Local storage versus what the user keeps | No backend means nothing to provision and also nothing to recover; a user who clears their browser loses the work the artefact just persuaded them to do |
| Plural regulation versus scope | Multi-jurisdictional coverage multiplies the graph build immediately, against a regulation brief that argued for covering only what the register cites |
| A moving legal target versus a shareable artefact | A vault sent to a prospect is a snapshot, and one of the regimes described here was repealed before it applied; a stale artefact in someone's inbox is a liability |
| Showing compliance status versus giving legal advice | The board-level conclusion that you are or are not compliant is the most compelling output and the one closest to a line the product should not cross |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| Which three or four scenarios ship first? | The ladder proposed here is a starting point and should be chosen against real personas rather than invented |
| How are the three predicates rendered? | Showing confirmed, validated and accepted per altitude without overwhelming a first-time user is the hardest design problem in the build |
| Who plays the compliance validator in the demo? | The role is new to the flow, and the airgapped demo has no real GRC function in it |
| What happens to a shared vault as the law changes? | Versioning, staleness warnings, or a mechanism for the artefact to know it is out of date |
| Which jurisdictions are in the first build? | The European instrument plus which of the state regimes, given the same system can sit in several |
| How are frameworks distinguished from statutes? | A voluntary framework carrying enforcement protection in one state needs a different node type from an obligation |
| Where does the compliant-or-not conclusion stop? | The boundary between showing which obligations apply and telling an organisation it complies |
