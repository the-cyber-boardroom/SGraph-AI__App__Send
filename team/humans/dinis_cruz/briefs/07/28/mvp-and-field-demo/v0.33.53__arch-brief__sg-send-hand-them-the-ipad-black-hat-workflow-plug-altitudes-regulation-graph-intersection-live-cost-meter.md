**from** Human (project lead)

*Tenth of 28 July. A workflow for a live conference, with a hard date. Event details are grounded and cited. Legal points are factual and are not legal advice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

## What This Is

The demo workflow to be built and taken into the field next week, and the moment three separate threads of the corpus become one artefact: **a partner is going to a large security conference, will meet a great many people, and the aim is a workflow that gives each of them something worth having, which sets a standard the team has to hold itself to, because it has to add value rather than merely impress; the form is a device handed over rather than a presentation delivered, so the interaction either walks somebody through or simply passes them the iPad and lets them go, and by the end that person should understand what the product does, understand the value proposition, have learned something, and have seen the platform work; the flow starts by picking a scenario matching the kind of agents they actually run, asks the plug questions directly, whether they can stop it, whether they know the side effects, what the procedure afterwards is, and then takes those answers and connects them into the risk graph and onward into the specific provisions their situation touches, which is possible for the first time because the regulation graph now exists and the instrument turns out to contain a great deal of material about exactly this; the register is then shown from each altitude in turn, operator, IT executive, data protection officer, chief executive, chief financial officer, by flicking between them on the same device, which makes the fractal register tangible in a way no diagram does; a takeaway follows, by message, screenshot, code or a vault created for them, because the point is that they leave with something they can act on internally; and running alongside the whole interaction is a live tally of what the analysis is costing in model usage, which turns the pricing conversation from a negotiation into an observation, since the number they watched accumulate is the number they would pay.** It is the tenth document of 28 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.53 scenarios-are-the-MVP brief, the v0.33.53 every-paragraph-is-a-graph brief, the v0.33.51 plug-profile series plan, the v0.33.49 demo and MVP workflow brief, and the v0.33.50 no-judgement brief). New contributions: **the hand-over as the acceptance test rather than a presentation, the three-way intersection of plug, altitudes and regulation becoming demonstrable, the persona flick as the tangible form of the fractal register, the live cost meter as simultaneously pricing conversation and proof of the consumption model, the takeaway as the conversion mechanism, and the explicit prohibition on the demo becoming an accusation.**

## The Deadline Is Four Days Away

Worth stating plainly because it changes what can be built. Black Hat USA 2026 runs from 1 to 6 August at the Mandalay Bay Convention Center in Las Vegas, with trainings from the first through the fourth, a summit day on the fourth that includes a dedicated artificial intelligence track, and the two-day main conference on the fifth and sixth. The Business Hall, where most of these conversations will actually happen, opens on the fourth. DEF CON follows immediately, from the sixth to the ninth, which extends the window.

So the event begins in four days and the hallway conversations begin in seven. The conference draws in the region of fifteen to twenty thousand people, and the Business Hall carries several hundred solution providers, which is the competitive context: everyone there is being pitched at continuously for three days. A device handed over that teaches somebody something about their own estate is differentiated precisely because it is not a pitch.

## The Test Is Whether You Can Hand It Over

The design constraint is stated as an option and should be treated as the acceptance test. The project lead: **"we almost either walk them through or just give the iPad to them and saying here it is."** And the outcome required. The project lead: **"by the end, the person understands what we do, understands the value proposition, and learns something, and sees the power of the platform."**

If the workflow only works when a knowledgeable person narrates it, it is a presentation with a touchscreen. If it works when handed to a stranger who then figures it out, it is a product. That distinction is worth enforcing during the build, because everything that needs explaining is a defect discovered cheaply now rather than expensively in a hallway. It is also the direct application of the position taken yesterday, that the best explanation of a graph is the experience of using one.

## The Intersection That Just Became Possible

The reason this is worth doing now rather than a month ago is that three separate lines of work have converged. The project lead: **"the intersection that is starting to materialise, which I think is very interesting, is this intersection between the risks that we propagate all the way to the top level execs, those semantic graphs, with activities like pull the plug, and how to pull the plug and watch the blast radius of pulling the plug on the agents they have already deployed, and then with regulations and with the graphs of the regulations."**

```
   PULL THE PLUG            can you stop it, blast radius, recoverability
        +
   REGISTER ALTITUDES       the same risk seen by operator, DPO, CFO, CEO
        +
   REGULATION GRAPH         which provisions the situation actually touches
        =
   one interaction that answers: what have you got, who owns it,
   what would stopping it cost, and what does the law say about it
```

The third element is the newest, and the memo notes the useful coincidence. The project lead: **"that act already has basically a lot of items from the pull the plug."** That is what makes the intersection more than a stack of features: the instrument independently cares about human oversight and the ability to intervene, so the regulation layer is not bolted onto the plug work, it corroborates it from an external authority.

## The Conversation Flow

The sequence is short enough to run in a corridor.

**Pick the scenario.** The project lead: **"we can start by asking or pick a scenario of the type of agents that they have."** This is the persona and scenario ladder already specified, and it is what keeps the interaction concrete rather than abstract.

**Ask the plug questions.** The project lead: **"do you have the ability to pull the plug? Do you know the side effects? What is the post procedure?"** Three questions, answerable from memory, no preparation required, and the second and third are the ones that produce the pause.

**Connect the answers into the graph.** The project lead: **"once we have those initial answers, we basically connect them to the risks all the way into the graph of the risks."**

**Then to the provisions.** The project lead: **"we connect them with the compliance entries that they are breaking."** The wording here needs care and is dealt with below, but the mechanism is the valuable part: a self-reported answer becomes a risk, and the risk cites an article of a real instrument.

**Show it from every altitude.** The project lead: **"we present them the view from each of the personas, so here's now the risk register for the operator, for the IT exec, for the DPO, for the CEO, the CFO, and we flick it on the iPad."**

## The Persona Flick Is The Fractal Register Made Tangible

This is the part most likely to land, and it is worth building carefully. The fractal register has been argued in the corpus for weeks: a register per accepting role, in that role's own language, with derived views filtered by relevance. It is a difficult idea to convey in prose and an easy one to feel when the same underlying situation redraws itself as you swipe between an operator's view and a chief executive's, with the language, the framing and the ownership all changing while the facts stay put.

One design note. The value is in the *translation*, not in the count of dashboards. What should be visible while flicking is that the operator's technical fact and the board's fiduciary exposure are the same thing seen from different heights, which is the five-whys translator the corpus already specifies. If it reads as five separate screens, the point is lost.

## The Live Cost Meter

The last idea in the memo is the most commercially useful and should not be treated as a detail. The project lead: **"that's the thing we need to have there on the side, the cost of the LLM usage during that activity, because that's the indication of what they need to pay."** And the pitch that falls out of it. The project lead: **"we say, look, what's your deal, would that cost you three quid or five quid?"**

It does three jobs at once.

**It answers the pricing question with an observation instead of a quote.** The most common objection in an early sales conversation is what this will cost, and the honest answer is usually a range that sounds evasive. Here the number accrued in front of them, on their own scenario, and they watched it happen.

**It demonstrates the commercial model rather than describing it.** The corpus has argued consumption pricing repeatedly. A visible meter is that argument made physical, and it also quietly proves the metering exists.

**It is a trust signal.** Showing your own cost while someone evaluates you is unusual, and it reframes the relationship from vendor and prospect to two people looking at the same number.

The obvious risk is that the number is small enough to undercut the perceived value of the whole platform, and the answer is that the meter measures the analysis, not the product. That distinction has to be visible on the screen or the demo argues against itself.

## The Takeaway Is The Conversion

The interaction has to leave something behind. The project lead: **"maybe have a takeaway that you can send to them on email or WhatsApp, or they can take a screenshot, or you give them a QR code, or we publish it somewhere, or even create a vault for them."** And the reason. The project lead: **"they want to follow it up, so they get something internally that they can then follow up, either more questions to ask or actions to take."**

Those options are not equivalent, and yesterday's argument applies directly: a takeaway behind a portal is not a takeaway. A screenshot is zero friction and zero follow-up. A code is one action, but only if it resolves to content rather than to a sign-in. A vault created for them is the strongest, because it is theirs, it persists, and it is the product. The ranking for the build is therefore to make the code-to-content path work first and the vault path second, and to treat any option requiring an account as a failure.

## What Must Not Happen

Two things could turn a good interaction into a bad one, and both are worth deciding before the event rather than during it.

**It must not become an accusation.** The phrase about the entries they are breaking is fine as internal shorthand and would be wrong on the screen. The person answered three questions from memory, standing up, about an estate the tool has never seen, with no evidence attached to anything. Whatever comes back is a hypothesis about their situation, not a finding about their compliance. The corpus position on this is settled and applies with force here: present what is, let the business judge it, and remember that most of these risks are already accepted by somebody. The correct framing is what applies to a situation like yours and what you would need to be able to say, rather than here is where you are in breach. Telling a room full of security professionals they are non-compliant, on the basis of three answers, is both wrong and the fastest way to lose them.

**It must not quietly collect their estate.** The audience is the single most privacy-attentive group the product will ever meet, and somebody typing details about their own agents into a stranger's iPad at a security conference is a real objection that will be raised out loud. The airgapped, no-backend, local-storage design already answers it, and the answer should be offered in the first fifteen seconds rather than defended on the third question. Handled well it is not a liability at all but the fastest possible demonstration of the sovereignty argument: nothing you enter leaves this device, and here is the architecture that makes that true.

## The Question It Is Designed To Answer

The memo is explicit that this is a test rather than a launch. The project lead: **"that then becomes the Black Hat pack, which we can then test and get feedback and see how far are we from actually having a product."** And the honest version of the question. The project lead: **"does it add value as it is, and do we already have a minimal viable product that could be sold?"**

Two acceptance criteria fall out of the memo and are worth writing down before the event so that the answer afterwards is not decided by enthusiasm. The first is the reaction the project lead describes in plain terms, the moment a person realises something about their own estate that they had not previously seen. The second is whether anyone asks what it costs to keep, which is the only reliable signal that the value was real rather than polite.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a presentation.** If it needs narration it has failed its own test.
- **Not a compliance assessment.** Three answers from memory produce a hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Not a data collection exercise.** Nothing leaves the device unless the person asks for a takeaway.
- **Not a product launch.** It is a field test with acceptance criteria, run against real strangers.
- **Not a pitch about consumption pricing.** The meter demonstrates it instead.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Four days versus the scope | The intersection described here spans three workstreams and the event starts on Saturday, so something will be thin, and choosing what in advance is better than discovering it in a hallway |
| Compliance citations versus interpretive honesty | Naming a provision is powerful and edges toward legal interpretation from three self-reported answers, which is exactly where the product should not appear to advise |
| The cost meter versus perceived value | A number small enough to be reassuring is also small enough to make the platform look cheap, and the meter must clearly measure the analysis rather than the product |
| Handing over the device versus the conversation | The hand-over is the honest test and it also removes the partner from the discussion, which is where qualification and relationship actually happen |
| A takeaway that persists versus one they will actually take | The vault is the strongest and the slowest; the screenshot is the weakest and the most likely to happen |
| Field feedback versus a self-selected sample | People who stop at a conference to try a demo are not representative buyers, and a strong reception should be discounted accordingly |
| Five altitudes versus corridor attention | The persona flick is the best idea in the demo and the one most likely to run out of time before it lands |
