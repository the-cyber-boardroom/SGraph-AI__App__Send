*Second of 28 July. The regulation graph that grounds the risk claims. External standards and regulatory dates are grounded and cited. Legal points are factual and are not legal advice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

## What This Is

A vault and a public website holding a fractal semantic graph of the AI regulations, built to sit underneath the risk work as its evidence layer: **the artefact is a graph of the AI and associated regulations, assembled by leveraging what already exists rather than by transcribing statute, since a great deal of structured legal material is already published and the task is to find the best of it, establish whether equivalent material exists for every regulation in scope, and see how deep the structure goes and how far the connections run; the reason to build it is not compliance reporting but evidentiary, because it becomes the source of truth that the risk acceptance graph links to, and specifically the thing that supplies the evidence to the pull the plug work, so that a claim made in a register is anchored to a named requirement in a real instrument rather than asserted, and the closer a risk sits to the actual article and to the consequence of not meeting it the stronger and the more explainable that risk becomes; piggybacking a standard is the point, because a risk traceable to a published obligation is one nobody has to take on trust; the regulations are also the right place to begin a risk journey with a customer, since they are already understood in a way the rest of this material is not; a subtlety worth capturing is that an obligation can apply to an organisation well before any penalty does, so a company can be out of step with a regulation without yet being exposed to its fines, and can cross into scope by changing a system without noticing that it has done so, which is itself a rateable risk with an owner and an interval; and the build reuses what has already been proven, files held in a vault, rendered into published artefacts, mapped according to how they will be used and then visualised, which also makes it a clean demonstration of provenance, since every claim can be traced to a source, a site, and a commit, and the graph does not need to be fully connected, because the plug work may only ever need a small part of it.** It is the second document of 28 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.51 plug-profile series plan, the v0.33.52 outbound maturity model, the v0.33.51 OSMM brief, the v0.33.40 RAMM brief, and the v0.33.51 distributed vault architecture brief). New contributions: **the regulation graph as the evidence layer beneath the risk register rather than a compliance product, regulations proposed as the opening move in a customer risk journey, the observation that obligation and liability have different start dates and that crossing into scope is itself a rateable risk, partial connection accepted as sufficient, and the vault-to-artefact-to-published-site path as a provenance demonstration.**

## The Artefact

The proposal is a vault plus a website. The project lead: **"the creation of a vault and a website that fundamentally provides a graph-based fractal semantic graph of the multiple AI and associated regulations that we start to map."** The build reuses the existing pattern rather than inventing one. The project lead: **"leveraging the capabilities that we already have developed, especially on the MVPs that we created, where we can then have the files on disk on the vault, we have a file on the vault that is then used to create the artefact that we can then publish."** So the chain is a file in a vault, mapped according to intended use, rendered to a published artefact, and visualised on a site.

## The Point Is Evidence, Not Compliance

The reason to build this is stated plainly and it is not what a regulation graph is usually for. The project lead: **"what's powerful about this is this is the graph that is going to provide the evidence to the pull the plug element."** The relationship between the two artefacts is explicit. The project lead: **"we will have a website that contains this, and then we'll have the pull the plug graphs, and this is what provides the evidence to do that."**

That inverts the normal arrangement. Most regulation-mapping products exist to tell an organisation whether it is compliant. This one exists so that a risk in somebody's register can point at a named obligation and say why it matters. The project lead: **"we can then underpin our entire risks on specific compliance risks, and what this provides us is a much stronger foundation for why we make certain claims."** And the closer the anchor, the stronger the claim. The project lead: **"the closer we are to the items and the requirements and the risks of not doing that, the better we will be, and the stronger we'll be, and the more explainability and strength we'll have."**

The reason this matters commercially is that it removes the weakest thing about any risk register, which is that somebody made the risks up. The project lead: **"because we piggyback off a standard, I think it provides strength to our risks because we're not making them up."**

```
   REGULATION GRAPH (vault -> site)          RISK REGISTER (pull the plug)
   article, requirement, consequence   <---  a risk cites its anchor
   published, versioned, in a commit         and inherits its authority

   the register stops asserting and starts citing
```

## Regulations Are The Right Place To Start A Risk Journey

The second argument is about adoption rather than architecture, and it answers a live problem in the demo work. The project lead: **"these regulations are a great anchor for us, and I think we should start in a way our risk journeys there, because that is something that is highly understood."**

Every other entry point the corpus has considered requires the customer to accept a novel framing first: the mandate to operate, the fractal register, the plug profile, the union of capabilities. A regulation requires nothing. A board already knows what it is, already has someone accountable for it, and already believes it matters. Starting there means the first conversation is on ground the customer owns, and the novel machinery arrives afterwards as the thing that connects the regulation to the actual exposure. This is the strongest answer the corpus has yet produced to the question of what the first question to a user should be.

## Leverage What Exists: There Is A Standards Stack

The instinct to reuse published material is correct, and the landscape is more developed than the memo assumes. The project lead: **"leverage what already exists, and then because there's a very lot of XML published, figure out the best of what is out there."**

What exists, in rough order of usefulness here:

**Akoma Ntoso** is an OASIS standard and an XML vocabulary for representing legislative, executive and judicial documents in a structured way, with its own media type and an explicit model for both content and metadata. It originated in 2008 in a United Nations programme and has been adopted by a range of parliaments and institutions.

**AKN4EU** is the European localisation of it, developed and maintained since 2018 by the interinstitutional committee responsible for common metadata and format standards, and described by the Publications Office as the machine-readable structured format for exchanging legal documents in the EU decision-making process. If a European instrument is available as structured XML, this is the shape it will be in.

**ELI**, the European Legislation Identifier, is the metadata schema and identifier system for European and member-state legislation, and **ECLI** is its case-law counterpart. These matter less for document structure and more for stable identity, which is exactly what a graph node needs.

**LegalRuleML, LKIF and CEN MetaLex** sit further up the stack, modelling norms and rules rather than documents, and are the place to look if the graph needs to express obligation and permission rather than structure alone.

There is also published research directly on the conversion problem, proposing property graph schemas derived from Akoma Ntoso XML for legislative systems, and on combining such graphs with language models to monitor legislation. That work is worth reading before designing a schema, because the mapping from a legal XML hierarchy to a queryable graph has been attempted and written up.

The honest caveat is that availability is uneven. The existence of a standard does not mean every instrument has been marked up in it, and establishing coverage is part of the work the memo already anticipates. The project lead: **"also interesting to see if that exists for all of them, because I think the AI Act actually seems to have quite a nice set of already graphs, but also it's interesting to see how deep it goes and the connections."**

## Obligation And Liability Do Not Start On The Same Day

The memo identifies a distinction that is easy to miss and is worth making a first-class feature of the graph. The project lead: **"although the AI Act only enforces high risk on it, you can say, look, you can still not be compliant to it, it's just that at the moment you're just not liable for the fines because you don't have a high risk AI system."** And the consequence, which is the sharp part. The project lead: **"you could actually have companies who flick that switch and not even realise that suddenly they now are complying to it."**

That intuition is correct and the current state of the European instrument makes it sharper than when the memo was recorded. The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 with staggered application. A Digital Omnibus, proposed in November 2025 and adopted through June 2026, deferred the obligations for stand-alone high-risk systems from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and for high-risk AI embedded in already-regulated products to 2 August 2028. So the window in which an organisation can be substantively out of step without facing the high-risk regime just became considerably longer.

Two features of that settlement turn the memo's observation into something concretely rateable. The first is grandfathering: systems placed on the market before the new dates are reported to fall outside the high-risk obligations unless they are substantially modified afterwards, at which point the clock resets. That is the switch being flicked, and it is flicked by an ordinary engineering decision rather than by a compliance one. The second is that scope itself moved, with the settlement reported to narrow which features count as high-risk safety components. Both mean an organisation's position can change without anyone in the organisation deciding that it should.

**A change that puts a system into scope is therefore a risk with an owner, an interval, and a plug**, and it belongs on a register in exactly the corpus's terms. The owner is whoever can authorise a substantial modification. The interval is however long it takes to notice. The plug question is whether the change can be reversed once shipped, and the recoverability answer is often that the assessment obligation cannot be un-triggered.

## Five Days From Now, Something Does Apply

One correction to the framing matters because of timing. The deferral is not a general pause, and 2 August 2026, which is five days after this brief, is reported to remain a live date on which transparency obligations under Article 50 begin to apply, the Commission gains penalty powers over general-purpose model providers, and national market surveillance authorities can fully investigate and sanction. Obligations for general-purpose model providers have applied since August 2025 even though the enforcement powers arrive later, which is itself a clean example of the obligation and liability distinction above. A further date follows on 2 December 2026 for machine-readable marking of generated content on legacy systems and for newly added prohibited practices.

So the accurate statement is not that the regulation is deferred. It is that different parts of it start at different moments, some of them imminently, and a graph that carries those dates as properties is materially more useful than one that carries only text.

## Partial Connection Is Enough

A useful constraint closes the memo, and it should be honoured rather than treated as a limitation. The project lead: **"we don't need the full blown of the graph, the graph cannot be fully connected to it, and we might only need a little bit of it from the pull the plug perspective."** The graph does not have to be complete, consistent, or fully linked to be useful. It has to cover the obligations that the risks in the register actually cite. That keeps the first build small and the scope honest, and it avoids the failure mode of every legal-knowledge-graph project, which is trying to represent the law rather than the part of the law somebody needs.

## Provenance Is The Second Demonstration

The build doubles as a worked example of the corpus's provenance argument. The project lead: **"it's a great case study of the whole meaning to connectivity, where we provide the provenance to say, oh, you can get it from this website, from this commit."** A risk cites an obligation, the obligation resolves to a node in a published graph, the graph resolves to a file in a vault, and the file resolves to a commit. That chain is checkable by anyone, which is the same force-of-proof property the evidence work has argued for throughout, applied to the one domain where an external authority already exists to be cited.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a compliance product.** It exists to give risks an evidentiary anchor, not to tell an organisation whether it passes.
- **Not a complete representation of the law.** Only the parts the register actually cites.
- **Not a transcription exercise.** The intent is to leverage published structured material, not to re-key statute.
- **Not legal advice.** Regulatory dates and mechanics here are factual statements from public sources and cited as such.
- **Not a single-regulation artefact.** The AI Act is the anchor case; the graph is designed for the associated regulations too.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Regulation as anchor versus regulation as ceiling | Starting where the customer already understands is the strongest adoption argument in the corpus, and it risks the register becoming a compliance list, which is exactly the overhead the product positions against |
| Citing an obligation versus interpreting it | A graph node can carry an article faithfully; whether a given system falls under it is a legal judgement the product must not appear to make |
| Standards exist versus coverage is uneven | The stack is real and the marked-up material is patchy, so the first build may involve more conversion than the leverage-what-exists framing suggests |
| Deferred dates versus a moving target | The timeline has already been amended once and the graph must treat dates as versioned properties rather than facts, or it will be wrong within months |
| Partial graph versus credibility | A deliberately incomplete graph is the right engineering call and an awkward thing to publish, since readers will test the edges that were never built |
| Evidence layer versus scope creep | Every risk will want an anchor, and the pressure to expand coverage will be constant and mostly unfunded |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What is in scope beyond the AI Act? | Which associated regulations, and in what order, given the graph only needs what the register cites |
| Is marked-up structured material actually available per instrument? | The coverage survey the memo already calls for, and the thing that decides build cost |
| What is the node and edge schema? | Whether to derive it from the legal XML hierarchy directly or from the published property-graph research |
| How are dates modelled? | Application dates, deferrals and grandfathering are versioned properties, and the timeline has already changed once |
| How does a risk cite an obligation? | The edge from a register entry to a graph node, and whether it carries an assertion of applicability or only a reference |
| Is crossing into scope a standing risk template? | A substantial modification that resets the clock, with an owner and an interval, looks like a reusable register entry |
| Where does the site sit? | Its own property, part of the existing site, or the first public vault-backed artefact |
