## What This Is

Two words that the business world uses interchangeably and that mean entirely different things, with the difference between them proposed here as one of the model's load-bearing primitives: **accepted describes an act performed by a person, the moment somebody with standing says they carry a risk, and under the established position every risk must be accepted because the risk is a fact that already exists whether or not anyone has signed for it; acceptable describes a threshold set by the business, the level below which it is content to stop funding remediation, which makes it the end state rather than the act, and which means the two are orthogonal rather than sequential, since a risk can be owned and still above the line, or below the line and owned by nobody at all; the reason this matters beyond vocabulary is that acceptable is simply risk appetite under a more useful name, and naming it this way makes it operational, because a stated acceptable level is the instruction that stops work, the sentence that says do not spend more on mitigating this because you are already below what the business has decided to tolerate; the regulation reaches the same place independently, since the AI Act requires that residual risk be judged acceptable and elsewhere requires testing against thresholds defined in advance, while never defining acceptable anywhere in its own definitions, which leaves the threshold to the organisation and makes the setting of it simultaneously a regulatory obligation and an unaided one; and that produces the entry point, because an organisation that has never defined its acceptable level cannot know whether it is finished, cannot know whether to keep spending, and is carrying a risk about its own risk management, which is both a real finding and the natural first question to ask anybody.** It is the thirteenth document of 28 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.46 risk-acceptance-redefined brief, the v0.33.49 interval-ladder brief, the v0.33.48 accept-first brief, the v0.33.53 every-paragraph-is-a-graph brief, and the v0.33.51 plug-profile series plan). New contributions: **the accepted and acceptable axes stated as orthogonal rather than sequential and rendered as four states, acceptable defined as the point at which funding remediation stops, the identification of acceptable as risk appetite renamed, the observation that the instrument mandates the judgement without defining the word, the undefined acceptable level treated as a first-class risk and as the opening question, and the proposal that the acceptance interval should be a function of the distance from the acceptable line.**

## The Two Words

**Accepted** is an act. Somebody with the standing to do it says: I carry this. It attaches to a named role, it carries a date, and under the corpus position it is not optional, because the risk exists as a fact regardless of whether anyone has signed for it. The only variable is the interval.

**Acceptable** is a threshold. It is a property of the business rather than of the risk, it describes a level rather than an event, and it is what determines whether more work is warranted.

The project lead's formulation of the second is the operational one and is worth keeping as the definition. The project lead: **"the acceptable risk is the moment that the business is happy to stop funding remediation activities."**

That phrasing is better than any textbook version because it is falsifiable. An organisation that cannot say when it would stop spending has not defined an acceptable level, whatever its policy documents claim.

## They Are Orthogonal, Not Sequential

The most common error is to treat these as two points on one line, where a risk is worked down until it becomes acceptable and is then accepted. They are two independent properties, and the four combinations are all real and all mean different things.

```
                        ACCEPTED (someone owns it)
                         no                    yes
                  +-------------------+-------------------+
   above the      | the dangerous     | the normal state  |
   acceptable     | state: unowned    | of a live         |
   line           | and above the     | programme: owned, |
                  | line              | and work continues|
                  +-------------------+-------------------+
   at or below    | quietly fine, and | THE END STATE:    |
   the acceptable | nobody has said   | owned, below the  |
   line           | so, so it cannot  | line, remediation |
                  | be relied upon    | can stop          |
                  +-------------------+-------------------+
```

Each quadrant implies a different action. Top right is healthy and means keep going. Bottom right is finished. Top left is the case the corpus already treats as critical, because an unowned risk sits with whoever happens to be holding it. Bottom left is the quiet failure: the exposure is small, but since nobody has said so, nobody can rely on it, and it will be rediscovered from scratch by the next person who looks.

## Where The Confusion Comes From

The project lead identifies the conflation precisely. The project lead: **"I think the traditional business acceptance is confused, or is used as acceptable."**

That is the whole problem in one line. When a business says a risk has been accepted, it very often means the risk was judged small enough to ignore, which is a statement about a threshold. It rarely means a named person has taken ownership of an exposure and committed to a review date, which is a statement about an act. Because the same word covers both, an organisation can believe it has done the second when it has only done the first, and nobody notices until something fires and the question of who owned it has no answer.

Separating the words separates the two failures, and they need different fixes. A missing acceptance is an ownership problem. A missing acceptable level is a governance problem.

## Acceptable Is Risk Appetite, Named Better

The project lead makes the connection explicitly. The project lead: **"this is fundamentally the definition of risk appetite, we just call it risk acceptable."** And the practical use follows. The project lead: **"this could be the anchor, so when we say don't do more risk mitigations because you're already below the level of risk that is acceptable by the business."**

Appetite is usually written as a paragraph in a policy and consulted by nobody. Restating it as an acceptable level attached to a specific deployment turns it into an instruction that can actually fire, and the instruction that matters most is the one that stops work rather than starts it. Knowing when to stop mitigating is worth more than knowing when to start, because starting is obvious and stopping is where money is wasted indefinitely.

This also sharpens something the corpus has held for a while. The established position distinguishes declared appetite, which mature programmes write down, from revealed appetite, which is reverse-engineered from the decisions actually recorded. In this vocabulary the declared appetite is the stated acceptable line and the revealed appetite is where remediation actually stopped, and **the gap between the two is the finding.** An organisation that stopped well above its stated line has an appetite problem it has not admitted; one that stopped well below it has been spending money it did not need to.

## What The Instrument Actually Says

The anchor is Article 9 of the European AI Act, which establishes the risk management system for high-risk systems. Paragraph 5 requires that risk management measures be such that the residual risk associated with each hazard, and the overall residual risk of the system, is **"judged to be acceptable"**.

One small correction to the memo for accuracy, since precision matters when citing an instrument: the paragraph refers back to paragraph 2, point (d), rather than to paragraph 2 as a whole.

Three further features of the same Article do real work here.

**It sets a hierarchy for getting there.** In identifying appropriate measures, the Article requires elimination or reduction as far as technically feasible through design and development first, then mitigation and control measures for risks that cannot be eliminated, then the provision of information and, where appropriate, training to deployers. That is eliminate, then mitigate, then inform. The middle step matters most to this corpus, because the instrument explicitly contemplates risks that cannot be eliminated, which is the residual the corpus has always insisted must be owned rather than wished away.

**It is continuous, not a gate.** The Article establishes the risk management system as an iterative process running across the whole lifecycle, with regular systematic review and updating. So the acceptable level is not set once at launch. The project lead reaches the same conclusion independently. The project lead: **"the risk acceptable is something that again should be reviewed normally."**

**It requires thresholds defined in advance.** Testing must be carried out against prior defined metrics and probabilistic thresholds appropriate to the intended purpose. That is a regulatory requirement to decide what acceptable means before you measure against it, which is precisely the discipline the memo argues for on independent grounds.

## The Instrument Mandates The Judgement And Does Not Define The Word

Here is the part that turns this from a definition into an opportunity. The Act requires residual risk to be judged acceptable. It does not define acceptable. The definitions Article covers a long list of terms, including risk itself, and acceptable is not among them.

That is exactly the category identified yesterday as the most interesting output of the definitional work: terms the text uses to carry weight without ever anchoring them, where every reader supplies their own meaning and none is wrong on the face of the text. Here the undefined term sits at the load-bearing centre of the risk management article.

The consequence is precise and commercially significant. **The obligation to make the judgement is imposed; the standard against which to judge is not supplied.** An organisation must decide where its line is, must be able to show it decided, and has no external definition to lean on. Academic commentary places this in the tolerable band of the as-low-as-reasonably-practicable framework long used in safety engineering, where risks are permitted only if reduced as far as is reasonably practicable, which is a helpful analogue and still leaves the judgement with the organisation.

That gap is the product. A tool that helps an organisation state its acceptable level, record who set it, connect it to the specific deployment, and demonstrate that remediation stopped at the right point is answering a question the instrument asks and declines to answer.

## Not Having Defined It Is Itself A Risk

The memo lands on the entry point and it is the strongest practical consequence. The project lead: **"you have to have a way to define what is this level of risk acceptable, how do you define that, and in fact, if you don't have that, that's a risk in itself."** And the opening move. The project lead: **"that's a good place to start, which is start to define what is the level of risk that is acceptable for the business for this particular AI deployment or AI agent."**

This is the same shape as the meta-risk identified in July about not knowing how many agents you have: a gap in the governance apparatus, stated as a rateable risk with an owner and an interval, which triggers and funds the work that closes it. It has three properties that make it a good first question.

It is answerable without any tooling, which means it can be asked in a corridor. It usually cannot be answered, which makes the asking itself the finding. And an organisation that cannot answer it does not know whether its current programme is finished, over-spending, or nowhere near done, which is a genuinely uncomfortable position to be shown.

## How It Maps To The Rest Of The Model

The memo asks for this explicitly, and most of it fits without strain.

| Established primitive | Where it sits |
|---|---|
| No deny, no wait, only how long | The **accepted** axis. Every risk gets an owner because it exists |
| Accept first, then reduce | Now clearly two moves: accept at the current level, then work toward acceptable |
| Accept the residual | The instrument's own middle step, for risks that cannot be eliminated |
| The level ledger | The dated record of movement down toward the acceptable line |
| Revealed versus declared appetite | Declared is the stated acceptable level; revealed is where remediation actually stopped; the gap is the finding |
| Unaccepted equals critical | The top-left and bottom-left quadrants, where nobody owns it |
| Recoverability | Where recoverability is zero the acceptable line must sit far lower, because nothing can be remediated after the event and the threshold has to do all the work |

One connection appears to be new and is worth testing. **The acceptance interval should be a function of the distance from the acceptable line.** A risk far above the line warrants a short interval, because the situation is expected to change and somebody should be looking. A risk at or below the line warrants a long one, because the only thing that would change it is drift. That makes the interval computable from two numbers already in the register rather than chosen by judgement each time, which is the same computed-not-claimed discipline the maturity models use.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a redefinition of acceptance.** The established position stands: every risk is accepted, and the only variable is the interval.
- **Not a claim to define acceptable for anyone.** The threshold belongs to the business, per the corpus's no-judgement position and the instrument's own silence.
- **Not legal interpretation.** The Article is quoted and characterised; whether a given system meets it is a judgement for others.
- **Not a scoring formula.** It argues acceptable must be explicit and computable; the scale is open.
- **Not applicable only to high-risk systems.** The distinction is general; the Article supplies an external anchor for it.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Two words versus adoption | The distinction is real and the language is close enough that customers will keep collapsing them, so the model has to survive being described sloppily |
| An explicit acceptable level versus the incentive to set it high | Asking an organisation to write down where it stops spending invites it to write down a number that justifies stopping now |
| Interval from distance versus judgement | Computing the interval from the gap is elegant and removes a judgement that sometimes ought to be exercised by a person |
| Undefined in the instrument versus filling the gap ourselves | Supplying the missing standard is the opportunity and edges toward saying what the law requires, which the product should not do |
| The meta-risk as an opening question versus its bluntness | Asking somebody whether they have defined their acceptable level is effective and can read as a trap, which the no-judgement posture has to defuse |
| Zero recoverability versus a threshold at all | For irreversible outcomes the acceptable line may have to be effectively zero, which makes the concept degenerate exactly where it matters most |
