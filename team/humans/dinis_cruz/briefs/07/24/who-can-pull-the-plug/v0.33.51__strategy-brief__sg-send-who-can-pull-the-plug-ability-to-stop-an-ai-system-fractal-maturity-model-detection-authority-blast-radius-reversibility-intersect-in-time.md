## What This Is

The governance of who can stop an AI system, mapped onto the risk mandate as a maturity model made of maturity models: **the question of who can pull the plug on an AI system goes to the heart of what RiskMandate does, because if nobody holds the mandate to pull the plug you have a risk, and if the system cannot easily be pulled you have a second risk, and the protocols for both are usually undefined; the right structure is not a flat checklist but a fractal maturity model, the same shape as the fractal risk registers, because a maturity model that lives only at the top is too abstract to act on and one that lives only at the bottom drowns the reader in detail, so each item at the top is itself a maturity model that decomposes downward until it finally lands on facts and evidence, which is exactly how the corpus already maps facts to evidence to risk; from the top-level risk that nobody has the mandate to pull the plug, powerful concrete risks fall out, that the system cannot easily be stopped, that stopping it takes an hour or ten hours or a day or five days, that it can only be stopped in office hours, and that the person or team with the authority is not available around the clock, which turns the whole question into an on-call problem of who holds the authority, to whom it has been delegated, and whether they can act without fear of losing their job and along a clear escalation path; the model then forces four things to intersect in time, detection, which is how fast you know you need to pull the plug and under what scenarios, decision, which is whether the authorised people can be assembled inside the available window, blast radius, which is how fast and how far the damage grows given that models execute and scale quickly especially when connected, and reversibility, which is whether pulling the plug is even physically possible and whether the changes can be reverted, because stopping is only half of it and the other half is restoring a good state through journaling and backups; the intersection is a Venn diagram where you are safe only if detection is fast enough, the decision can be made in time, and the damage does not go exponential before both happen, and the danger case is a steep cost range where an affordable window of one or two days of damage collides with a decision that cannot be made in one or two days; observability has to be loaded toward the plug specifically, since logs that exist but do not tell you where you are in the stopping decision are worthless, and there is a real detection floor because most hyperscalers report cost twelve to eighteen hours late, so twelve to eighteen hours of damage is the exposure before anyone even sees it; and the whole thing is a natural MVP and demo, because it can be asked as a single question and played through tabletop exercises from simple scenarios, access to the customer database, the calendar, email, upward to advanced ones, with the risks that fall out of those exercises connected back to reality through the digital twins.** It is the second brief of 24 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.49 fractal-registers brief, the v0.33.49 interval-ladder brief, the v0.33.50 core-primitives brief, the v0.33.46 who-holds-the-bag brief, and the v0.33.44 catastrophic-failure-and-containment brief). New contributions: **the ability to stop an AI system framed as a fractal maturity model of maturity models that decomposes to facts and evidence, the two symmetric risks of no mandate to stop and no ability to stop, the decomposition of stopping into timed sub-risks around availability and office hours, the four-way intersection of detection, decision, blast radius, and reversibility as the thing that has to line up in time, stopping-plus-reverting as a single act requiring journaling and backups, plug-loaded observability as distinct from generic logging, the twelve-to-eighteen-hour hyperscaler cost-reporting delay as a concrete detection floor, and the plug-pull question proposed as the MVP and demo anchor driven by tabletop exercises grounded through digital twins.**

## The Two Symmetric Risks

The memo starts from an external essay asking who can actually stop an AI system when something goes wrong, and turns it into two risks rather than one. The project lead: **"if you do not have somebody who has the mandate to pull the plug, you have a risk, and if you do not have a system that can be pulled the plug, you have a risk too."** These are genuinely different failures. The first is an authority gap, a system that could be stopped but has nobody empowered to say stop. The second is a capability gap, a system nobody can stop even if they decide to. The project lead names the second as widely underestimated. The project lead: **"there is a lot of systems that are not that easy to pull the plug, and the protocols are not well defined."** The external essay the memo responds to makes the same point in its own terms, that detection without the authority and the ability to act is governance theatre, a fire alarm rather than a fire department, and this brief is the structural answer to that argument.

## Why A Maturity Model, And Why Fractal

The reason this cannot be a checklist is the reason nothing in the corpus is a checklist. The project lead: **"one of the challenges of maturity models is that they are too high up to be that relevant, but they are too low down for a lot of people, because they have too much detail and too little detail, depends on how you look at it."** The resolution is the same relevance fade already used for registers. The project lead: **"the fractal element to maturity models, which is the same thing as the fractal element of our graphs."** So a maturity model is itself built of maturity models. The project lead: **"each item at the top level is itself a maturity model, and they can have maturity models all the way until they start to hit facts and evidence."** This is the maturity-model equivalent of facts to evidence to risk, and it means the plug-pull model is not a new mechanism but the existing register machinery pointed at one question.

```
   MATURITY MODEL OF MATURITY MODELS

   top:     "can we stop our AI systems?"        (board-legible, too abstract alone)
     |
     +-- "mandate to stop exists?"     -> its own maturity model
     +-- "ability to stop exists?"     -> its own maturity model
     +-- "detection fast enough?"      -> its own maturity model
     +-- "changes reversible?"         -> its own maturity model
              |
              +-- ... decompose until it lands on FACTS and EVIDENCE
                  (journaling on? backup tested? on-call rota? cost latency?)
```

## The Top-Level Risk Decomposes Into Timed Sub-Risks

From one abstract risk, the model extracts a set of concrete, rateable ones, and the sharp move is that most of them are about time. The project lead: **"there is nobody with a mandate to pull the plug, the system cannot easily be pulled the plug, or the system can only be pulled the plug in one hour, ten hours, a day, or five days, or the system can only be pulled the plug in office hours."** That last one turns governance into an operational availability question. The project lead: **"who has the authority to make the call, and who has the authority been delegated to make the call."** And the human reality behind the authority is named rather than assumed away. The project lead: **"in a way that they are not going to lose their job, and you have a very clear escalation path."** The clean mapping here is to the interval ladder from 17 July: there the acceptance interval implied a committed response, and here the time-to-stop is the same kind of number, a commitment about how fast the plug can actually be pulled, which is meaningless unless somebody is on call to pull it.

## The Four Things That Must Intersect In Time

The core of the model is that four separate capabilities have to line up inside the same window, and a gap in any one breaks the whole thing.

The first is **detection**. The project lead: **"how long will it take us to know that we need to pull the plug, which also raises the question of what are the scenarios that you are going to pull the plug."** Detection is not binary but a curve. The project lead: **"is it zero to one hundred, is there a linear progression, is there exponential progression."**

The second is **decision**, the ability to assemble the authorised people in time. The project lead: **"if we can detect what is going on very quickly, and we can assemble the people to make the decisions within a certain timeframe, and the damage being done does not go exponential, then you are within that good area."**

The third is **blast radius**, and it is what makes AI different from a slow-burning failure. The project lead: **"the models do have the ability to grow very fast, to execute and scale very fast, especially if it is connected."** This is expressed as a cost range, reusing the corpus's language of ranges. The project lead: **"if you have something that can exponentially grow and cause damage very fast, then that has a range of cost that is very steep."**

The fourth is **reversibility**, which the memo insists is half the job and usually forgotten. The project lead: **"it is not just pulling the plug, it is pulling the plug and reverting the changes."** With a concrete failure. The project lead: **"you make changes to a database, and you are corrupting a database, so what happens when you pull the plug and then you need to restore, do you have journaling of all the changes that were made, do you have backups."** And before any of that, a prior question the memo flags for a Wardley map. The project lead: **"can you even pull the plug, is the system physically able to pull the plug, or is the damage even bigger, sometimes it is systems that do not control, sometimes it is systems that belong to other people, and what are their SLAs."**

```
   THE SAFE ZONE IS THE INTERSECTION

   DETECTION time  +  DECISION time   <   time until BLAST RADIUS goes exponential
                                           AND the change is REVERSIBLE

   danger case: you can afford 1-2 days of damage,
                but you cannot make the decision in 1-2 days   -> gap
```

## Observability Loaded Toward The Plug

Generic logging does not satisfy this model, and the distinction is important. The project lead: **"you need to know what is going on with the ability to pull the plug, and that is very important, more than just having some logs, because sometimes it is very easy to have logs, but the logs actually do not mean anything."** So observability is scoped to the decision it must support. The project lead: **"we need to have logs that allow us to pull the plug, to understand where you are in that maturity model of pulling the plug."** There is also a hard, concrete detection floor that the memo names precisely, and it is the kind of checkable fact the corpus is built on. The project lead: **"most hyperscalers do not give you the costs in real time, you are delayed by twelve to eighteen hours, so if you are only seeing the cost of a model being executed twelve hours behind, then that is the amount of damage that could be done."** A second, deeper layer of observability is needed for explainability and reproducibility. The project lead: **"can you reproduce what happened, can you actually understand what was done, that means you need to store a lot more information, about the prompts, about what happened, so the logs have to be way more detailed than sometimes they are."**

## Where This Meets The Standards And The Regulation

The memo asks explicitly to connect this to standards and regulation, and the fit is unusually exact, which strengthens the commercial case because it means the model is not inventing a concern but operationalising one that regulators already mandate.

The EU AI Act makes the stop capability a legal requirement rather than a good practice. Article 14 requires high-risk AI systems to be designed so that natural persons can effectively oversee them, and it specifically requires that an assigned person be able to intervene or interrupt the system through a stop button or a similar procedure that brings it to a halt in a safe state. It splits responsibility between the provider, who must build the capability, and the deployer, who must operate it, and commentary on the article stresses that a merely declarative human-in-the-loop fails inspection, that the stop mechanism has to be tested rather than assumed, and that the obligation runs to the architecture and not only to the org chart. The compliance deadline for high-risk systems is August 2026. The plug-pull maturity model is, almost line for line, a way to measure and evidence Article 14 readiness: the mandate-to-stop is the assigned person, the ability-to-stop is the safe-state halt, the reversibility question is the safe state itself, and the plug-loaded observability is the detection-and-understanding capability the article requires. Legal points are factual and not legal advice.

The incident-response frameworks supply the operational spine. The long-standing structure separates detection and analysis from containment, eradication, and recovery, and the current revision maps those onto the detect, respond, and recover functions of the broader cybersecurity framework. Two of its established practices map straight onto this model. The first is that containment strategies should be pre-defined per asset tier so responders are not improvising under pressure, which is precisely the escalation protocol and the affordable-damage window the memo describes. The second is the discipline of measuring mean time to detect and mean time to contain, which are exactly the detection and decision intervals the four-way intersection depends on, and which turn the maturity model's abstract timing into numbers an organisation can actually track. Tabletop exercises, which the memo proposes as the demo mechanism, are a named practice in these frameworks for exactly this purpose, validating readiness and revealing procedural gaps before a real incident does.

## The MVP And Demo Live Here

The memo's strongest commercial claim is that this question is where the product should first meet a customer. The project lead: **"maybe our MVP and our demo starts here, because we can ask this question, and we can then go from advanced scenarios, or even simple scenarios."** The simple scenarios are named and are deliberately mundane. The project lead: **"access to the customer database, access to the calendar, access to email, access to other things."** And the method is simulation before integration, which fits the airgapped-first MVP from 17 July exactly. The project lead: **"we can do a lot of tabletop exercises, and we can then have risks coming off those tabletop exercises, and then we can connect reality using our digital twins."** This is a clean sequence: ask the plug question, run a tabletop on a simple scenario, harvest the risks that fall out, and only then reach for real telemetry through the twin to confirm or correct them, which is the mapping-error loop from 23 July arriving through a governance door.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a checklist.** A fractal maturity model of maturity models, decomposing to facts and evidence, not a flat scorecard.
- **Not an enforcement mechanism.** It measures and evidences the ability to stop; it does not itself stop anything, consistent with never being in line.
- **Not only about authority.** The ability to stop and to reverse is as much the risk as the mandate to stop, and the memo weights the capability gap heavily.
- **Not a single document.** A simple-scenarios library and a standards crosswalk are siblings, flagged not folded in.
- **Not dependent on integration to start.** It runs first as tabletop simulation, reaching for the digital twin only to connect back to reality.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Fractal depth versus knowing when to stop decomposing | Maturity models of maturity models can recurse without end; the discipline that stops at facts and evidence needs a rule, or the model becomes the over-detailed thing it was meant to avoid |
| Measuring stop-readiness versus never in line | The model rates the ability to stop but does not provide it; a customer who scores badly will ask us to supply the stop button, which is exactly the enforcement role the corpus refuses |
| The MVP anchor versus the airgapped MVP | This makes a strong case to start here, and the 17 July demo already anchors on registers flowing up; the two candidate MVP framings need reconciling into one |
| Tabletop risks versus real risks | Risks harvested from a simulation are hypotheses until the twin confirms them; presenting simulated risks as findings would repeat the credibility trap flagged on 23 July |
| The 12-to-18-hour cost delay versus its generality | It is a sharp, checkable fact, but it is a cost-reporting delay specific to hyperscaler billing, and other detection signals may be far faster, so it should not stand in for detection latency in general |
| Regulatory tailwind versus regulatory scope | Article 14 binds high-risk systems specifically; the model applies far more broadly, so leaning on the regulation sells the regulated segment while under-selling everyone else |
| Reversibility as our claim versus our reach | We can rate whether journaling and backups exist, but whether a revert actually restores a good state is a property of the customer's systems that a maturity score can assert but not guarantee |
