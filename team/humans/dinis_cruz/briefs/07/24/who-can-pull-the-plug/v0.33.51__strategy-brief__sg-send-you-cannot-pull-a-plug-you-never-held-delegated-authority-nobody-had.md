# You Cannot Pull A Plug You Never Held

**date** 24 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Board, Executive, Security, Risk, Public

*Piece 6 of the Who Can Pull The Plug series. Delegated authority nobody had. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

The failure that leaves an agent with no legitimate off-switch at all: **an agent often ends up able to do something because a person handed it a capability, and sometimes that person handed over an authority they did not themselves hold, granting access to a system they had no right to grant, wiring in a credential that was never theirs to share, connecting a tool whose use they could not personally have authorised, and when that happens there is no legitimate plug, not because the switch is technically missing but because no accountable person ever held the authority to stop the thing, so there is nobody with standing to pull it; this is a different failure from over-permissioning, where a real owner granted too much and can still revoke it, because here the defect is in the delegation itself, the chain of authority has a broken link, and the agent is acting on a mandate that traces back to no one who could give it, which means the risk has no owner, no acceptance, and nowhere to roll up to, and detecting it requires comparing what the agent can do against what the person who enabled it was actually entitled to do.** This is piece 6 of the Who Can Pull The Plug series (cross-ref: the series-plan document, the pillar, the v0.33.50 core-primitives brief, and the accountability-gap piece). New contributions: **delegated authority nobody held described as the absence of a legitimate plug, its distinction from over-permissioning, and the detection method of comparing agent capability against the delegator's own mandate.**

## Where The Plug Has No Owner By Construction

The previous piece described a plug whose owner fell through the gap between teams. This one describes something worse: a plug that has no legitimate owner by construction, because the authority the agent acts on was never anyone's to give. Someone connected the agent to a system they themselves had no right to connect it to. Someone shared a credential that was not theirs. Someone enabled a capability they could not personally have been authorised to use. The agent now acts, and when you ask who can stop it, the honest answer is that no one has the standing to, because stopping it would mean exercising an authority that the whole chain never legitimately held.

## Not The Same As Too Much Access

It is important to separate this from ordinary over-permissioning, because they look similar and are treated differently. Over-permissioning is when a real, accountable owner grants an agent more than it needs. That is a sizing error, and it is fixable by the owner, who still holds the authority and can revoke it. Delegated-authority-nobody-had is a legitimacy error. The grant should never have been possible, the person who made it was not entitled to, and no amount of revocation by the wrong person fixes a chain whose root was invalid. One is a dial set too high by the right hand. The other is a switch installed by the wrong hand.

```
   OVER-PERMISSIONING                 DELEGATED AUTHORITY NOBODY HAD
   a real owner granted too much       nobody entitled granted anything
   the owner can revoke it             no one has standing to revoke
   a sizing error, fixable in place    a legitimacy error, broken at the root
   the plug has an owner               the plug has no legitimate owner
```

## It Breaks The Acceptance Chain

The reason this matters beyond tidiness is that it breaks the chain the whole model runs on. An acceptance needs an owner, and an owner needs the authority to own it. When the authority behind an agent traces back to someone who never held it, there is no valid owner to accept the risk, no legitimate approver above them, and no honest path for the risk to roll up, because every level it would climb to would be accepting a thing that should not have been possible. The risk does not just lack a plug. It lacks a place in the accountability structure at all, which is why it tends to be invisible until it acts.

## Detecting It

Finding these cases is a comparison, and it is one the model is already shaped to make. On one side is what the agent can actually do, the union of its real capabilities. On the other is what the person who enabled it was themselves entitled to do, their own mandate. Where the first exceeds the second, the agent is exercising an authority its enabler never held, and that delta is the finding. It requires modelling both the agent and the human delegator, and keeping both current, but the shape of the check is simple: no one can pass on more than they hold, and any agent that has received more is operating on a plug that nobody can legitimately pull.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not over-permissioning.** That is a fixable sizing error; this is a broken chain of authority.
- **Not a technical missing switch.** The switch may exist; what is missing is anyone with standing to use it.
- **Not enforcement.** We detect and surface the illegitimate delegation; we do not sever it in the path.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Modelling the delegator versus the agent | Detection needs the human's own mandate modelled and current, which is harder than modelling the agent alone |
| Legitimacy versus practicality | Some useful delegations technically exceed the delegator's formal authority, and the model must distinguish a real defect from a paperwork gap |
| No legitimate owner versus needing one | Someone still has to stop a dangerous agent even when the chain is broken, so the model must escalate to a real authority to grant the missing legitimacy |
