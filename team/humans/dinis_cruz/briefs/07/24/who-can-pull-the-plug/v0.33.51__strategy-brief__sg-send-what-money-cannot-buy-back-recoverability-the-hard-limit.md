# What Money Cannot Buy Back

**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Board, Executive, Security, Risk, Public

*Piece 3 of the Who Can Pull The Plug series. Recoverability, the hard limit. The heaviest piece in the set. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

The dimension that decides all the others: **recoverability is how much of the damage can be undone after the plug is pulled, and it is the hard limit money cannot cross, because most risk is quietly recoverable, you stop the thing, clean up, restore from backup, refund the customer, and given enough time and resources the state is repaired, but a serious class of risk is not like that at all, a data breach cannot be un-happened once the data is outside the boundary, destroyed data with no clean backup does not return, a real-world release into the environment or a physical safety event cannot be recalled, and misstated financial records or lost trust are repaired slowly and only in part if at all; for these the entire value of a fast, precise plug is spent before the event, because after it the plug changes nothing that already happened, which means recoverability is not one factor among five but the one that reorders the others, since a risk that is catastrophic and fully recoverable and a risk that is smaller and irreversible are different objects that demand different treatment, and any model that scores only likelihood and impact while ignoring recoverability will rank them exactly wrong.** This is piece 3 of the Who Can Pull The Plug series (cross-ref: the series-plan document, the pillar, the speed piece, and the v0.33.40 RAMM brief). New contributions: **recoverability defined as the fifth and deciding dimension, the reordering claim that it outranks likelihood and impact for irreversible risk, and the shift to prevention where recoverability is zero.**

## Most Risk Is Quietly Recoverable

The reason recoverability is easy to overlook is that most of the time it is high. A server falls over and you fail over. A bad deploy ships and you roll it back. A customer is overcharged and you refund them with an apology. A dataset is corrupted and you restore last night's backup. In all of these the plug matters, the stop matters, but the deeper comfort is that the damage is reversible: with enough money, time, and effort, the world returns to roughly where it was. This quiet recoverability is the cushion that has let organisations treat the plug as the whole answer, because usually, once you have stopped the thing, you can fix what it did.

## A Class Of Risk Is Not

Then there is the other class, and it is defined by a single property: no amount of money buys the state back. A breach is the clearest case, because once the data has left the boundary it is out, and every dollar afterwards buys notification, remediation, and apology, not the un-happening of the disclosure. Destroyed data with no clean backup is gone, and the recovery budget rebuilds what can be rebuilt and mourns what cannot. A physical action in the world, a release into the environment, a safety event, an irreversible transaction, lands in reality and stays there. Reputation and trust, once broken in a particular way, are rebuilt slowly and never quite to the same shape. For all of these, the plug still exists and can still be pulled, but pulling it after the event is closing a door on an empty room.

```
   RECOVERABLE                        IRREVERSIBLE
   stop, clean up, restore, refund     stop... and the damage stays done
   money + time repairs the state      no budget buys the state back
   the plug is a real control          the plug is spent before the event
   treat with detection + response     treat with prevention, only prevention
```

## Recoverability Reorders The Others

This is why recoverability is not merely the fifth item on a list. It reorders the other four. Where recoverability is high, the plug profile is the story: who, blast radius, speed, side effects all determine how well you contain and clean up, and a fast precise plug genuinely wins. Where recoverability is zero, those four collapse in importance, because none of them changes the outcome once the event has occurred, and the only control that matters is the one that prevents the event from being possible at all. The same agent, the same capability, the same likelihood, can be a manageable operational risk or an existential one depending entirely on whether the worst outcome can be undone, and nothing but recoverability tells you which.

## Why Likelihood Times Impact Ranks It Wrong

The standard shape of risk scoring multiplies likelihood by impact, and it systematically misjudges irreversible risk. Consider two risks with the same score: one a large but recoverable outage, one a smaller but irreversible data disclosure. Likelihood times impact rates them equal, and an organisation working down a ranked list treats them the same. But they are not the same. The outage can be survived and repaired; the disclosure cannot be undone. A model that cannot see the difference will spend equally on both, or worse, prioritise the higher-impact recoverable one, and leave the irreversible one accepted by default. Recoverability has to be a first-class term in the model, not a footnote, precisely so that the irreversible risk cannot hide behind an ordinary score.

## Where Recoverability Is Zero, Only Prevention Counts

The practical consequence is a change of control strategy, not just of scoring. For a recoverable risk, detection and response are rational investments, because catching it and cleaning it up limits the damage. For a zero-recoverability risk, detection and response are close to worthless at the moment of truth, because by the time you detect it the irreversible thing has happened. Everything therefore moves upstream, to prevention: the controls that stop the agent from ever being able to reach the irreversible action, the scoping that removes the capability, the gates that make the destructive path impossible rather than merely monitored. The plug question, honestly followed, leads for this class of risk to the conclusion that the plug is beside the point and the capability should not have existed.

## The Queryable Field

All of this argues for making recoverability a first-class, queryable property of every accepted risk, not a sentence buried in a rationale. The single most valuable question an organisation can ask its own register is: show me every accepted risk whose recoverability is zero. That set is the list of things that cannot be undone, that no plug will save after the fact, and that therefore must be governed by prevention and by the most senior, most deliberate acceptance in the organisation. A register that cannot answer that query is a register that cannot see its own irreversible exposure, which is the exposure that matters most.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim that the plug is useless.** For the recoverable majority the plug is a real and valuable control.
- **Not fear.** Irreversibility is a fact about certain outcomes, stated plainly, not a threat about the vendor.
- **Not a scoring formula.** It argues recoverability must be first-class; the exact scale is an open question.
- **Not a counsel of despair.** The conclusion for zero-recoverability risk is constructive: prevent, do not merely watch.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Recoverability as a term versus scoring it | Naming it is easy; a scale that correctly ranks an irreversible risk above a larger recoverable one is hard |
| Prevention versus the never-in-line posture | Prevention often means enforcement, which is someone else's tool; we identify the need and rate the tool, we do not become it |
| Stating irreversibility versus grounded tone | This is the most frightening material in the series, and it has to land as fact, or it becomes the fear-selling it replaces |
| Partial recoverability versus a binary field | Many outcomes are partly recoverable, and a crude zero-or-one field will lose that, while a rich scale is harder to agree |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What is the recoverability scale? | Binary, banded, or continuous, and how it combines with likelihood and impact without being averaged away |
| How is partial recoverability captured? | Outcomes that can be mostly but not fully repaired, and how the register represents them |
| Who accepts a zero-recoverability risk? | The seniority and deliberation an irreversible acceptance should require, likely the highest altitude |
