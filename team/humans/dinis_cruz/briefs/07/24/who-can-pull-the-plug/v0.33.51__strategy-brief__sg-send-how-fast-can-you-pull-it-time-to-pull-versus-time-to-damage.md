## What This Is

The third thing to know about pulling the plug: **a plug is only real if it can be pulled before the damage is done, so speed is not a nicety but the whole test, and it is a race between two clocks, the time it takes to detect, decide, and pull, against the time it takes the agent to do the harm, and agents have made the second clock far faster than the first, because a system that takes hundreds of actions in the seconds it takes a human to notice one can finish the damage long before anyone reaches the switch; this is exactly what the shortest rung of the acceptance interval ladder means, since choosing to accept a risk only for an hour is a claim that you can pull the plug inside an hour, and if you cannot, you do not hold that plug however real it looks; and where the damage is instantaneous, the exfiltration completes in one request, the destructive command runs once, no plug speed is sufficient at all, which is the moment this dimension hands the reader to recoverability, because a stop that arrives after the irreversible thing has happened stops nothing that mattered.** This is piece 2 of the Who Can Pull The Plug series (cross-ref: the series-plan document, the pillar, the v0.33.49 interval-ladder brief, and the recoverability piece). New contributions: **speed as time-to-pull against time-to-damage, the one-hour rung read as a speed claim, and the point at which speed runs out and only recoverability remains.**

## Two Clocks

Every plug lives between two clocks. The first is time-to-pull: how long from the moment the agent starts doing the wrong thing to the moment the plug is actually pulled, which includes detecting the behaviour, deciding it is wrong, finding who holds the switch, and reaching it. The second is time-to-damage: how long from that same first moment to the point where the harm is done. A plug is useful exactly to the degree that the first clock beats the second, and useless when it loses.

```
   t0  agent starts doing the wrong thing
    |
    |----------- time-to-pull -----------> plug pulled
    |----- time-to-damage --> harm done
    |
   if time-to-damage < time-to-pull, the plug arrives too late
```

## Agents Broke The Second Clock

For most of the history of operational risk, the two clocks were compatible: a human or a slow process did the harm, and a human could notice and intervene on a similar timescale. Agents break that symmetry. A system that can make hundreds of decisions in the time it takes a person to read one alert compresses time-to-damage toward zero while time-to-pull stays human. The gap between them is the exposure, and it is a gap that did not exist at this size before, which is a large part of why the plug question has become urgent rather than academic.

## The One-Hour Rung Is A Speed Claim

The acceptance interval ladder already encodes this, and reading it through the plug makes it concrete. When someone accepts a risk only for the shortest interval, an hour, they are not describing a review cadence, they are making a claim about speed: that if this goes wrong, we can pull the plug inside an hour. If the honest time-to-pull is a day, the one-hour acceptance is a fiction, and the register should not let it stand. The interval a person is willing to accept and the speed at which they can actually stop the thing have to match, and where they do not, the mismatch is the finding.

## When Speed Runs Out

There is a hard edge to this dimension. Some damage is instantaneous: the exfiltration completes in a single request, the destructive command executes once and returns, the payment clears. Against instantaneous harm, no plug is fast enough, because the first clock cannot beat a second clock that reads zero. This is not a reason to abandon the plug, it is the signal that the plug was never going to be the control for this risk, and that everything now depends on what can be recovered afterwards. Speed is the dimension that, when it runs out, points directly at the next one.

## What Fast Actually Requires

Speed is not a single property, it is a chain, and it is only as fast as its slowest link. Detection has to be fast, which means the behaviour has to be observable at all. The decision has to be fast, which means someone must be authorised to make it without convening a meeting. And the pull has to be fast, which means the holder must be reachable and the mechanism ready. A plug that is instant to pull but takes a day to decide is a day-slow plug. Measuring speed honestly means measuring the whole chain, not just the final action.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a promise that RiskMandate makes the plug faster.** We measure and own time-to-pull; we are not in the path that executes it.
- **Not a claim that faster is always the answer.** Where damage is instantaneous, speed cannot help, and pretending otherwise wastes the effort.
- **Not only about the final action.** Speed is the whole chain from detection to pull, and the slowest link governs.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Measuring time-to-pull versus knowing it | Real time-to-pull is rarely documented, and estimating it honestly may require actually testing the plug |
| Fast decision versus careful decision | Pulling the plug fast conflicts with pulling it correctly, and the authority to act quickly has to be granted in advance |
| Speed versus instantaneous harm | For a class of risk no speed suffices, and the honest thing is to say so and move to recoverability |
