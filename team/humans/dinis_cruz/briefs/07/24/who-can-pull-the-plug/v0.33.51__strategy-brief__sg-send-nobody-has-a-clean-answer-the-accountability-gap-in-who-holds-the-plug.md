*Piece 5 of the Who Can Pull The Plug series. The accountability gap. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

## What This Is

Why the plug question so often produces a pause rather than an answer: **when you ask who can stop a production agent, the responsibility slides between hands, the model owner assumes the platform team holds it, the platform team assumes the business unit, the business unit assumes somebody in IT has a kill switch, and frequently nobody does, so the plug is not missing, its ownership is, and it has fallen into the gap between the people who each assumed someone else had it; this is the shape of a governance failure that agents did not create but did make impossible to ignore, because for years the same diffusion of accountability was survivable when consequences moved slowly, and it stops being survivable the moment a system can act faster than the argument about whose job it was can be resolved; the answer is not another committee or another document naming a responsible party in the abstract, it is a structural one, every plug has a single named holder who has both the authority and the capability to pull it, unowned plugs are visible rather than assumed, and a plug nobody will claim rolls up until someone with the standing to accept that gap actually does.** This is piece 5 of the Who Can Pull The Plug series (cross-ref: the series-plan document, the pillar, the v0.33.49 fractal-registers brief, and the altitudes piece). New contributions: **the handoff-of-blame described as the ownership gap rather than a missing plug, the diffusion of accountability named as the failure agents exposed, and the structural remedy of a single named plug-holder with unowned plugs rolling up.**

## The Pause Is The Finding

The most common answer to who can pull the plug is not a name, it is a pause followed by a guess about someone else. That pause is not ignorance of the plug, it is ambiguity about who owns it. The plug exists, technically, in several hands or in none clearly, and the ambiguity means that in the moment of an incident the first minutes are spent not pulling it but working out who is allowed to. The finding is the ambiguity itself, and it is present long before any incident, waiting to be discovered at the worst possible time.

## The Handoff Of Blame

The pattern is consistent enough to describe. The person who owns the model points to the team that runs the platform. The platform team points to the business unit that asked for the agent. The business unit points to IT, assuming a kill switch lives there. And IT, if asked directly, often finds that the switch everyone assumed exists was never actually assigned to anyone. Each handoff is individually reasonable, and the sum of them is a plug that belongs to everyone in theory and no one in practice. An outside governance argument makes the same observation from the enterprise side, and draws the same conclusion: this is not a new problem invented by AI, it is an old accountability failure that a fast enough technology finally exposed.

```
   model owner  -->  "the platform team has it"
   platform team -->  "the business unit owns it"
   business unit -->  "IT has a kill switch"
   IT           -->  "...nobody was actually assigned one"

   the plug exists. the OWNER fell through the gap.
```

## Why Agents Made It Unignorable

Diffuse accountability for the off-switch is not new, and organisations lived with it for a long time because the systems it applied to acted slowly. A slow process gave the argument about whose job it was time to resolve before much damage was done. An agent does not. It acts in the gap between checkpoints, faster than the handoff of blame can complete, so the ambiguity that used to be an inefficiency becomes an exposure. Nothing about the governance failure changed. The speed of the thing it governs did, and that is enough to turn a tolerable weakness into an urgent one.

## The Structural Answer

Naming a responsible party in a policy does not fix this, because the failure is not the absence of a name in a document, it is the absence of a name attached to a real, reachable off-switch. The structural answer has three parts. Every plug has a single holder, and that holder has both the authority to decide and the capability to act, because either without the other is not ownership. Unowned plugs are made visible rather than assumed, so the gap is seen before the incident, not during it. And a plug that no one will claim does not simply sit unowned, it rolls up to the next altitude until it reaches someone with the standing to accept that the gap exists, which converts a silent ambiguity into an explicit, owned decision.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim the plug is missing.** It exists; the owner is what fell through the gap.
- **Not a call for a new committee.** The remedy is a named holder and a structure, not another governance body.
- **Not blame.** The handoff is made of individually reasonable assumptions; the failure is systemic, not personal.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Single holder versus real organisations | Some plugs genuinely need more than one hand, and forcing a single name can be as false as naming none |
| Roll-up versus overload at the top | If too many unowned plugs roll up, the top of the organisation becomes a dumping ground rather than a decision point |
| Authority plus capability versus reality | The person with the authority often lacks the capability and vice versa, and closing that split is real work, not a data field |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| How is authority-plus-capability verified? | Confirming a named holder can actually pull the plug, not just that they are allowed to |
| What is the roll-up rule? | When and to whom an unowned plug escalates, and how the top is protected from overload |
| How are shared plugs represented? | The cases where ownership is genuinely joint, without recreating the diffusion the model exists to fix |
