# Sovereignty Bounties: Grant-Funded Migration Paths Off Proprietary Platforms, Milestones Paid On Customers Still There A Year Later, And The Vendor Who Sponsors Its Own Exit

**version** v0.33.51
**date** 24 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Policy, Partners, Board

**type** Strategy brief

*Follows the OSMM work. A funding instrument proposal at regional, national, and European level. External funding programmes, regulation, and migrations are grounded and cited. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A grant and bounty model for financing the open-source alternatives that let a customer leave a proprietary platform, proposed at regional, country, and European level: **the instrument pays companies to build and to service the open-source technology that moves a customer off a closed platform, structured as a maturity ladder of milestones rather than a single award, starting where there is no economic case at all and no commercial incentive exists, then paying at the milestone of being able to replicate some of the data, then at one customer successfully transitioned, then perhaps six months later at five customers who have stayed, and then at a year where the customers are still on the platform, so that what is being bought is not code delivered but customers surviving, which is the only thing that constitutes real proof that moving away is possible; the same instrument could be sponsored by the incumbent vendors themselves, and that is the sharpest version of the idea, because a company that funds a documented, working exit path from its own product acquires a strong and checkable claim that it does not hold its customers by lock-in, cannibalising a little of its own business preemptively in exchange for a customer base that stays by choice; the exercise produces a diagnostic that does not currently exist, because attempting the migration is what reveals where the lock-in actually sits, whether in the schemas, in the technology, or in the usability, and that answer is worth having independently of whether any given customer moves; the market failure it targets is circular, since the economic model for these migration services does not exist precisely because the companies and services do not exist, so paying for the open source seeds a market that cannot bootstrap itself, and the instrument is unnecessary wherever a market has already formed; and the pricing is a bounty, scaled so that the more complicated the migration the bigger the bounty, letting market economics set the price point, applied across large chunks of the software stack, with the whole thing amounting to a way of proving independence from a technology rather than asserting it.** It follows directly from the OSMM work (cross-ref: the v0.33.51 OSMM brief, the v0.33.51 OSMM assessment, the v0.33.49 open-source-as-strategy brief, the v0.33.48 vendor-sprawl brief, and the v0.33.49 commercialising-the-primitives brief). New contributions: **the sovereignty bounty as a funding instrument, the milestone ladder that pays on customer survival rather than on delivery, the vendor-sponsored variant as a checkable no-lock-in claim, migration attempted as the diagnostic that locates the lock-in, the circular market failure the instrument targets, and bounty size scaled to migration difficulty.**

## The Proposal

The instrument is a grant programme with an unusual object. It does not fund the maintenance of open-source software, and it does not fund a public body's own migration. It funds the companies that build and operate the path from a closed platform to an open alternative, and it pays them in stages as that path is demonstrated to work. The project lead: **"they should have projects that basically finance companies to develop and service open source technology and services that basically are able to move a customer from one closed source technology into the open source alternative."** The sponsor can sit at any altitude, and the memo names three. The project lead: **"at sort of regional level, country level, and even European level."**

## The Milestone Ladder Pays For Survival, Not Delivery

The structure is the most original part of the proposal, and its distinguishing feature is what triggers payment. Ordinary grant funding pays on deliverables: code shipped, a report filed, a milestone met. This pays on customers still being there. The project lead: **"have grants that support open source companies and open source projects that basically pay them for maybe the multiple stages."** The stages run from an attempt with no economic case, through technical capability, to durability. The project lead: **"if there's no economic there, to then you know the milestone of being able to replicate some of the data, to then the final milestone should be that you actually have one customer that survive, that is then transition, and maybe another one six months later that you have five customers, and that the customers have successfully stayed there, and maybe even like a year one where the customers are still in that platform after a year."**

```
   STAGE 0   attempt where no economic case exists      (nothing else will fund this)
   STAGE 1   replicate some of the data                 technical capability
   STAGE 2   one customer transitioned                  it works once
   STAGE 3   five customers, ~6 months, still there     it works repeatedly
   STAGE 4   customers still there at one year          it holds

   paid on SURVIVAL, not on delivery.
   the last two milestones cannot be gamed by shipping code.
```

That final property is what makes the ladder worth the administrative trouble. A migration that technically completes and then quietly reverses is not proof of anything, and a payment schedule that stops at go-live would fund exactly that outcome. Paying at six months and at a year buys evidence that the destination was habitable, which is the claim the whole instrument exists to establish. The project lead: **"the logic here is that it's again to have that sort of real proof that it is possible to move away from those technologies."**

## Attempting The Migration Is The Diagnostic

The second-order benefit may be larger than the first, and it is available even when a migration fails. Nobody currently knows, for most proprietary platforms, which layer actually holds the customer. The project lead: **"what this is going to allow is to understanding of where actually is the lock-in, where is the lock-in in a particular technology, in a particular service? Is it schemas? Is it the technology? Is it the usability?"** Those three answers imply completely different remedies. Schema lock-in is addressed by serialisation standards and is largely a documentary problem. Technology lock-in requires a substitute engine and is an engineering programme. Usability lock-in is the hardest of the three because it lives in habit and training rather than in artefacts, and no standard fixes it. A funded migration attempt produces that answer empirically, and the answer is a public good regardless of whether the customer ends up moving.

## The Circular Market Failure

The instrument is aimed at a specific and self-perpetuating gap, and the memo is precise about why the market cannot close it unaided. The project lead: **"that economic model doesn't exist by the simple fact that there isn't not those companies, not those services."** The circularity is the point: no migration services exist because no market exists for them, and no market exists because no customer can see a credible path, and no path is credible because no service has ever demonstrated one. Public money breaks the loop by paying for the first demonstrations, after which the ordinary economics can take over. The scoping consequence is equally explicit, and it keeps the instrument from becoming a subsidy where none is needed. The project lead: **"this doesn't need to happen where markets already exist and where things got there economically."** The ambition, once the loop is broken in one place, is breadth. The project lead: **"you can go around the block, and basically do this for large chunks of the software stack."**

## The Bounty Prices The Difficulty

Pricing is left to the market rather than fixed by the sponsor, with difficulty as the variable. The project lead: **"it's then market economics, like the more complicated is the bigger the bounty; it's sort of like a bounty system."** This has an elegant property: the bounty size becomes a published measure of how locked-in a technology is. A platform whose migration bounty clears at a low price is demonstrably easy to leave, and one that repeatedly fails to attract takers at any price has revealed something about itself that no marketing claim can offset. The price is the finding.

## The Vendor Who Sponsors Its Own Exit

The most striking move in the memo is that the sponsor need not be a government. The project lead: **"this could even be sponsored by the company themselves if they want to have a strong claim that they don't have a lock-in on the customer."** The logic is preemptive and self-interested rather than altruistic. The project lead: **"this will be a great way for companies to preemptively be involved in a way already cannibalising a little bit of their business, but making sure that the business has customers that are not based on lock-in, and that the customers have an easy way to actually move away from the platform, and is actually documented."**

This is the 17 July open-source thesis carried to its logical end. That brief argued that lock-in should move off the technology and onto quality, services, versions, maintainability, and certification, because a customer who stays by choice is the only durable kind. A vendor-funded exit path is that argument made checkable: it converts "we do not lock you in" from an assertion into an artefact a procurement can inspect, and it does so at a cost the vendor controls. The corpus has consistently held that retention earned is worth more than retention imposed, and this is the instrument that proves which one a vendor has.

## How This Completes OSMM

The connection to the maturity model is not thematic, it is mechanical, and it resolves a problem the OSMM work leaves open in three places.

**It funds the rehearsal that Level 3 requires.** OSMM Level 3, Operable, is not satisfied by an open licence on the engine alone; it requires that a non-vendor deployment has executed the ontology in earnest and that the exit is rehearsed rather than theoretical. Rehearsing an exit is expensive and nobody's budget owns it. The bounty is the budget.

**It manufactures the fork capacity that Level 4 asks for.** The Change-of-Control Stress Test has four legs, and three of them, copyright structure, trademark holding, and schema licence, are documentary and cheap. The fourth, a named party with the headcount and mandate to run the fork, cannot be produced by paperwork. OSMM's own Tension 6 concedes that fork capacity is the softest predicate and the one most open to generous self-assessment. A grant-funded migration company with paid staff and surviving customers is precisely a named party with headcount, and its existence is objective evidence rather than an assertion. The instrument does not merely score fork capacity; it brings it into being.

**It defeats the commercial disincentive the assessment identified.** The Foundry and Scrydon assessment concluded that both vendors sit one well-defined engineering step from Level 2, an OWL or SHACL serialisation with an executed round-trip test, that neither has a technical excuse, and that both have a commercial disincentive. A disincentive is not an obstacle that better arguments overcome. The bounty routes around it by paying a third party to build what the vendor will not, which is the only mechanism in the corpus that addresses a vendor's unwillingness rather than its inability.

There is a further alignment worth making explicit, because it suggests how the milestones should be specified. OSMM's predicates are ordered and monotone, and the bounty ladder is too. The natural move is to express the milestones as OSMM level transitions rather than as prose: the replication milestone is the Level 1 to Level 2 step, serialisation plus a round-trip test; the first transitioned customer is the Level 2 to Level 3 step, a non-vendor deployment executing in earnest; and the surviving-customers milestones are the Level 3 to Level 4 step, fork capacity evidenced by a going concern. That would make the payment trigger a graph query rather than an assessor's judgment, which is the same discipline RAMM and OSMM already apply to maturity.

```
   BOUNTY MILESTONE                    OSMM TRANSITION
   replicate some of the data     ->   Level 1 -> 2   serialisation + round trip
   one customer transitioned      ->   Level 2 -> 3   non-vendor execution, exit rehearsed
   five customers, still there     ->   Level 3 -> 4   fork capacity, evidenced
   still there at a year           ->   Level 4 -> 5   re-tested, drift-checked, provable
```

## The Regulatory Window Is Six Months Wide

The proposal arrives at an unusually favourable moment, and the timing strengthens it considerably. Under Chapter VI of the EU Data Act, switching charges between data processing services, including data egress fees, are prohibited outright from 12 January 2027, having been capped at direct costs during the transition from January 2024. Infrastructure providers must take reasonable measures to deliver functional equivalence after a switch, meaning a materially comparable outcome from the same inputs for shared features, and other providers must supply open interfaces free of charge with documentation sufficient for interoperable software to be built against them. Where no interoperability specification applies, data must at minimum be provided in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format. Notice periods are capped and exit assistance is contractually mandated.

The significance for this proposal is that the Data Act creates a legal right to switch without yet creating any evidence that switching works. Practitioner commentary on the regime has already noted that the compliance focus shifts from contractual terms to demonstrable capability. Functional equivalence is a legal standard that, as of today, essentially nobody has demonstrated empirically for a complex platform. A bounty programme that funds real migrations and publishes what breaks is the fastest available way to produce that evidence, and it would serve regulators, buyers, and honest vendors simultaneously. Six months before the switching-charge prohibition takes effect is the moment such an instrument is most useful and most fundable.

## What Exists Already, And What Does Not

Germany's Sovereign Tech Agency is the closest working precedent and is worth studying for its administrative machinery rather than its object. Operating since 2022 as a subsidiary of the federal disruptive-innovation agency and commissioned by the economics ministry, it has invested over twenty-four million euros in roughly sixty projects, funding foundational components such as cURL, GNOME, systemd, PHP, FFmpeg, and coreutils. It commissions defined work under public procurement rules with milestone payments and progress reports rather than making unconditional grants, it accepts applications globally on the reasoning that improving a dependency used domestically strengthens the domestic economy wherever the maintainer sits, it lowered its minimum contract size from one hundred fifty thousand to fifty thousand euros in 2024 to accommodate part-time maintainers, and it has since extended into fellowships, bug bounties, and a 2026 standards programme placing maintainers into IETF, W3C, and ISO work. An EU-level equivalent has been proposed and is modelled on it.

The gap is the important part. Every one of those programmes funds the **supply side**: keeping existing open-source infrastructure healthy, secure, and maintained. None of them funds the **substitution side**: building and proving the path by which a specific customer leaves a specific proprietary platform. That is a different object with a different success measure, and on present evidence nobody is funding it. The proposal here is not a duplicate of the Sovereign Tech Fund; it is the missing complement, and the existing agency's contract-and-milestone machinery is a ready-made administrative template for it.

## The Evidence That Migration Works, And Where It Stops

Public-sector migrations already underway supply both encouragement and a warning, and the warning is the more useful of the two. Schleswig-Holstein has moved close to eighty percent of state workstations outside the tax administration to LibreOffice, completed a migration of more than forty thousand accounts and over one hundred million emails and calendar entries off Exchange and Outlook onto Open-Xchange and Thunderbird, reported over fifteen million euros of annual licence savings against a one-off nine million euro investment for 2026, and has extended the same reasoning to AI with an internal assistant built on an open-weight model. Denmark's digital ministry has moved to LibreOffice, and France's gendarmerie has run a Linux desktop estate at scale for years. The European Commission's open-source observatory has described the German state as a leading public-sector adopter.

The warning is in the residual. The remaining fifth of Schleswig-Holstein's estate has not moved because specialised applications retain hard dependencies on the proprietary office formats, which is precisely the diagnostic the memo predicts: the office suite was never the lock-in, the applications built against it were. That is the answer to "is it schemas, technology, or usability" for one stack, discovered by attempting the migration and not by analysing it, and it is exactly the kind of finding a bounty programme would generate systematically rather than incidentally. It also sets a realistic expectation: the last twenty percent is where the money goes, and a milestone ladder that pays out at first transition would have declared victory well before the hard part.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a maintenance fund.** The Sovereign Tech model funds the health of existing open source; this funds the path off a proprietary platform.
- **Not a subsidy for functioning markets.** Explicitly scoped to where no commercial incentive exists, and unnecessary where one does.
- **Not payment for delivery.** The later milestones pay for customers still being there, which code shipped cannot satisfy.
- **Not necessarily public money.** The vendor-sponsored variant is the sharpest version and needs no state at all.
- **Not a claim that migration is easy.** The instrument exists because it is hard, and its diagnostic value is highest where it fails.
- **Not a replacement for OSMM.** It is the funding mechanism that makes OSMM's Level 3 and Level 4 predicates reachable in practice.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Vendor sponsorship versus who controls the scope | A vendor funding its own exit path chooses the scope, the pace, and the definition of success, and could deliver a token path that satisfies the letter while the real lock-in stays untouched; the claim is only worth anything if an independent party sets the criteria and publishes the failures |
| Paying for survival versus cherry-picking | A bounty paid on customers still being there rewards choosing the easiest customers, and the difficulty-scaled price is the intended defence, but difficulty is hard to price before the attempt rather than after it |
| Five customers is a small number | Survivorship at that sample size is weak evidence, and a programme that publishes it as proof of general feasibility will be fairly criticised |
| Seeding a market versus distorting one | Subsidised migration companies may undercut unsubsidised ones or collapse when the grant ends, and the instrument only works if a real economic model exists on the far side of the last milestone |
| Hardest where the evidence is weakest | The instrument is scoped to where no commercial incentive exists, which is also where migration is hardest and least likely to succeed, so cost is highest exactly where the proof is least likely to arrive |
| One migration is not a general claim | A funded migration proves a path exists for one customer with grant support, which is not the same as the path being affordable at scale unaided |
| The alternative may not exist yet | Where no open-source substitute exists at all, the grant is funding product development rather than migration, which is a much larger commitment wearing the same label |
| Public money funding exit from public suppliers | Financing departure from vendors that the same public bodies procure from is politically awkward and should be anticipated rather than discovered |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| Should the milestones be OSMM level transitions? | Expressing the payment triggers as graph predicates would make them computable rather than assessed, consistent with RAMM and OSMM |
| Who administers it, and at what altitude? | A Sovereign-Tech-style agency, an EU-level fund, or a national programme, and whether the existing contract-and-milestone machinery can be reused directly |
| How is difficulty priced before the attempt? | The bounty scales with complexity, but complexity is most accurately known after the migration, which is the wrong end |
| What counts as successfully transitioned? | Whether the test is the Data Act's functional equivalence standard, an OSMM level, or a customer's own acceptance |
| What independence does the vendor-sponsored variant need? | The governance that separates a genuine no-lock-in claim from a marketing exercise the vendor scopes itself |
| What happens after the last milestone? | Whether the funded company has a business once the bounty stops, and what the exit criteria for the programme are |
| Is there a window before January 2027? | Whether the Data Act switching deadline creates a fundable case for a pilot in the next six months |
| Which stack is the first target? | The layer where the circular market failure is most acute and a demonstration would be most legible |
