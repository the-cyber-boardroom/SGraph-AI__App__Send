# OSMM Assessment 001: Palantir Foundry and Scrydon

**version** v0.33.51
**date** 24 July 2026
**from** Research (executed for the project lead)
**to** Strategy, Publication, Architect

**type** Research brief (applied assessment)

*First application of the Ontology Sovereignty Maturity Model. Scored on public evidence only.*

---

## What This Is

The first assessment run against the OSMM, applied to the two vendors whose claims prompted the model: **both platforms score OSMM Level 1, Extractable, on the ontology, which is the finding that matters because their marketing places them at opposite ends of a sovereignty spectrum; Palantir Foundry reaches Level 1 on strong, documented evidence, a customer-triggerable JSON export of the full ontology working state, and fails Level 2 because that serialisation is Foundry-shaped rather than a W3C artefact, so it satisfies Readable and not Portable, which is the access-is-not-control distinction stated as a score; Scrydon reaches at most Level 1 on much weaker evidence, since no ontology export path, serialisation format, engine licence, or repository could be located publicly, and the model treats unevidenced predicates as unsatisfied by construction, so its score is bounded above rather than measured; on the Change-of-Control Stress Test the ranking inverts against intuition, because a Ghent-based startup roughly two years old with venture ownership is a materially easier acquisition target than a large listed US company, so on the specific axis Scrydon markets hardest, the durable survivability of its customers' ontologies, it currently presents more exposure rather than less; the two vendors are separated by CADA jurisdiction and by cost, which are real and which OSMM deliberately does not score, and are not separated by ontology sovereignty at all; and the actionable output is that both are one well-defined engineering step from Level 2, an OWL/SHACL serialisation with a round-trip test, and Scrydon is three cheap documentary steps from a Level 4 claim Palantir structurally cannot make.** It is the first instance called for by the model itself (cross-ref: the v0.33.51 OSMM brief). New contributions: **the two scorecards, the survivability inversion, and the ten counter-evidence queries executed.**

## Method And Its Limits

Scored against public artefacts only, as of 24 July 2026: vendor documentation, package registries, code hosts, regulatory and press sources. No vendor was contacted.

This matters asymmetrically and the asymmetry must be stated plainly. Palantir is a large listed company with exhaustive public product documentation, so its predicates are richly evidenced and it can be scored precisely. Scrydon is a startup with a marketing site and thin public technical documentation, so several predicates return no evidence either way.

The model treats an unevidenced predicate as unsatisfied, and that is a deliberate design choice rather than an oversight: Provable means demonstrable *without the vendor's cooperation*, so a predicate that cannot be checked from public artefacts has failed the fifth predicate by definition even if the underlying capability exists. But for predicates 1 to 4 the honest reporting is **bounded above, not measured**, and Scrydon's score could move on production of documents. Those documents are named at the end.

One more fairness note. Absence from search results is not proof of absence. Every Scrydon finding below should be read as "not publicly locatable as of this date," and the assessment should be re-run after direct enquiry.

## Scorecard: Palantir Foundry

**OSMM Level 1, Extractable.** High confidence.

| Predicate | Verdict | Evidence |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| **Readable** | **Pass** | Ontology schema definitions are stored in a JSON file; the working state can be exported from Advanced settings and re-imported, and edited in a text editor in between. Customer-triggerable, documented, no vendor assistance required. |
| **Portable** | **Fail** | The export is Foundry-shaped JSON, not OWL/RDF/SHACL. No W3C serialisation path exists. A third-party open-source library exists specifically to bridge Foundry's object/link/action types to OWL and SHACL, which is direct evidence that the native platform does not. |
| **Modifiable** | **Fail** | The reasoning engine is closed. The OSDK is a client SDK that explicitly treats Foundry as the backend, so it presupposes rather than replaces the runtime. Palantir open-sources substantial Java infrastructure under Apache 2.0, but not the ontology engine. |
| **Survivable** | **Fail** | Single-entity copyright, company-held trademark, no separately licensed schema, no fork target. All four legs fail. |
| **Provable** | **Fail** | Follows from the above. |

**Notes on the score.** Level 1 is a genuine pass and should not be read as damning: a customer-triggerable full-ontology export is more than several self-described open platforms demonstrably offer, and it is the reason the strong claim sometimes made against Palantir ("you cannot export the ontology") is simply false and should not be repeated.

The interesting failure is Portable, and it is the cleanest illustration of the model's central distinction. Palantir markets openness credibly at the *storage* layer (Iceberg, Parquet, Iceberg REST Catalog, bring-your-own-bucket) and the lock-in sits one layer up, in the semantic model, where the export exists but nothing else can reason over it. On the data layer Foundry would score roughly Level 3. On the ontology it scores Level 1. **That gap between data-layer and ontology-layer sovereignty is the single most useful thing this model surfaces**, and it is invisible to any framework that scores platforms rather than artefacts.

Survivability fails structurally rather than incidentally. Foundry's ontology is not a licensing decision Palantir could reverse cheaply; it is the product. A Level 4 claim is not available to them at any price short of open-sourcing the engine.

## Scorecard: Scrydon

**OSMM Level 1 at most, Extractable, unconfirmed.** Low confidence, bounded above.

| Predicate | Verdict | Evidence |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| **Readable** | **Unevidenced** | No documented ontology export path located. No schema documentation, format specification, or export API found in public materials. |
| **Portable** | **Unevidenced** | No W3C serialisation claim located. The platform advertises support for open standards (OpenAPI, MCP, A2A), but these are integration and transport protocols, not ontology serialisation formats, and do not discharge this predicate. |
| **Modifiable** | **Fail, on present evidence** | The homepage describes the platform as "European-native and open-source," but no repository for the platform could be located. The only published package is `@scrydon/sdk` on npm, a TypeScript **client** SDK that reaches the platform over an OAuth bearer path, which is the same architectural shape as Palantir's OSDK and is not the engine. |
| **Survivable** | **Fail** | Venture-stage private company, single-entity copyright by default, company-held trademark, no foundation, no fork target, no separately licensed schema located. |
| **Provable** | **Fail** | Follows by construction: none of the above is demonstrable without the vendor's cooperation. |

**Notes on the score.** There is an internal inconsistency in the vendor's own materials worth flagging neutrally, because it is the kind of thing a procurement will find. The homepage asserts the platform is open-source. The Foundry comparison page, the document written specifically to make the openness argument, never repeats that claim: its comparison table records openness as "Open formats (Iceberg), your perimeter, your operators," which is formats, perimeter and operations, not source. Its FAQ then concedes that switching costs are commercial and operational rather than format-level.

The most likely benign reading is that the platform is *built on* open-source components (StarRocks, Iceberg, and similar are named explicitly) and that the homepage compresses "built on open source" into "open-source." That is an extremely common elision and is not evidence of bad faith. But under OSMM it is exactly the elision the Modifiable predicate exists to catch: consuming open-source components confers nothing on the customer, because the customer cannot run the *platform* from them.

## The Survivability Inversion

The uncomfortable finding, and the one most worth publishing.

Scrydon's entire differentiation rests on durability of control: EU jurisdiction, your keys, your perimeter, no dependency on a US vendor. The Change-of-Control Stress Test asks a narrower question: *if this vendor changes hands, do you still hold the ontology?* And on that question the ranking inverts.

| Stress-test leg | Palantir | Scrydon |
|---|---|---|
| Copyright structure | Single entity | Single entity (assumed; no CLA located) |
| Trademark holder | Operating company | Operating company |
| Schema licence | None separate | None located |
| Fork capacity | None | None |
| **Acquisition likelihood** | **Low**, large listed US company, no plausible acquirer | **High**, venture-stage EU startup in the most actively consolidated sector in Europe |

Both fail all four documentary legs. The difference is the fifth row, and it runs the wrong way for the sovereignty pitch. The empirical record from the research is unambiguous on the point: the Dutch government blocked the Solvinity acquisition specifically because a US buyer would have brought CLOUD Act exposure to the national digital identity platform; Apple was reported to have considered acquiring Mistral; SUSE has changed hands four times and is currently in a sale process at up to $6bn where a US buyer is explicitly contemplated. European sovereignty startups are the acquisition target class, not the safe harbour from it.

So a European buyer choosing Scrydon over Foundry on sovereignty grounds is trading a jurisdictional exposure that is *known and stable* for one that is *lower today and materially more volatile*. That is a defensible trade, the CLOUD Act is a live risk and the kill-switch precedent is real, but it is a different trade from the one being marketed, and OSMM makes the difference legible. Note also SUSE's CEO answering exactly this question with "we are still a European company with shareholders in America," which is the corporate-nationality argument the 13 June thesis already identified as unreliable.

The inversion is not a criticism of Scrydon's intentions. It is a structural fact about venture-stage companies, and it is fixable, cheaply, in a way Palantir's is not.

## Counter-Evidence Queries, Executed

Per the model, queries seek the fact that would falsify a claimed level.

| # | Query | Foundry | Scrydon |
|---|-------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Object types with no W3C serialisation edge | All | Unknown; no serialisation documented |
| 2 | Round-trip tests older than last schema change | No tests exist | No tests located |
| 3 | Ontology definitions with no separate licence node | Confirmed absent | Confirmed absent |
| 4 | Engine dependencies that are source-available not OSI | Engine is closed | Engine not locatable |
| 5 | Copyright path terminating in a single entity | Yes | Yes (assumed) |
| 6 | Trademark held by operating company | Yes | Yes |
| 7 | Fork-capacity claims with no named party | No claim made | Claim implied by "open-source", no party named |
| 8 | Predicates that held before and do not now | N/A, first run | N/A, first run |
| 9 | Estates dragged below appetite by <5 object types | N/A at vendor altitude | N/A at vendor altitude |
| 10 | OSMM below RAMM-accepted threshold with no acceptance node | Buyer-side finding | Buyer-side finding |

Query 3 is the sleeper. **Neither vendor licenses the ontology schema separately from the code or the service agreement.** For a customer, this means the semantic model of their own business, the artefact the 13 June thesis identifies as the most concentrated representation of the organisation that will ever exist, has no independent licence status. It travels under whatever the master agreement says. That is a contract question a buyer can raise today, at no engineering cost to either vendor, and it is probably the highest-leverage single ask in this entire assessment.

## What Would Move Each Score

**Both, to reach Level 2, Portable.** Publish an OWL/SHACL serialisation of the ontology schema and an executed round-trip test on a second engine. This is bounded engineering work, weeks not quarters, and a third-party library already demonstrates the Foundry mapping is tractable. Neither vendor has a technical excuse; both have a commercial disincentive, which is precisely why a buyer should ask for it in a contract rather than wait for a roadmap.

**Scrydon, to reach Level 3, Operable.** Publish the platform repository under an OSI licence, or stop describing the platform as open-source on the homepage. The current position (asserting it on the front page, omitting it from the comparison table) is the least defensible of the three available and will not survive a technical procurement.

**Scrydon, to reach Level 4, Survivable.** Three documentary steps, all cheap, none requiring a line of code:
1. Adopt a DCO instead of a CLA, or publish the CLA so the relicensing rights are visible.
2. Transfer the trademark to a neutral foundation with charter-level transfer: this is the empirically load-bearing leg, per the NATS case.
3. License the ontology schema separately under CC0 or CC-BY.

**Palantir, to reach Level 4.** Not available. It would require open-sourcing the ontology engine, which is the product.

That asymmetry is the strategic opening and should be stated exactly as it is: **Scrydon can reach a level Palantir structurally cannot, for a few weeks of engineering and three legal documents, and currently has not.** Level 4 on the ontology would be a genuinely differentiated claim, checkable by anyone, and far more durable than a jurisdictional argument that an acquisition can void.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the evidence asymmetry flatters Palantir.** Scoring on public documentation rewards companies with large documentation teams. Palantir's Level 1 is well-evidenced partly because Palantir documents exhaustively. Scrydon may satisfy more predicates than it can be shown to satisfy, and the assessment must be re-run after enquiry before it is used externally.

**Tension 2: OSMM does not score what Scrydon is actually selling.** Jurisdiction, cost, CADA level, and the absence of CLOUD Act exposure are real, material buyer concerns, and the model deliberately brackets all of them. A buyer for whom CLOUD Act exposure is the binding constraint is rationally right to prefer Scrydon on evidence this model does not examine. Presenting an OSMM tie as "no difference between them" would be a misuse of the instrument.

**Tension 3: we are scoring a competitor with our own model.** Tension 2 of the OSMM brief, now live. This assessment is only publishable if our own score is published alongside it, with our own failing queries shown, and on present structure we also fail the trademark leg.

**Tension 4: Level 1 is being read as a low score.** It is a real capability, and for Foundry a well-implemented one. The model's staircase is steep by design, which is defensible, but the presentation must not let a technically accurate Level 1 read as an accusation of closedness.

**Tension 5: the acquisition-likelihood row is a judgment, not a measurement.** Every other row in the stress test is documentary. That one is an inference from base rates, and it is the row doing the most rhetorical work. It should be flagged as such wherever the inversion is presented.
