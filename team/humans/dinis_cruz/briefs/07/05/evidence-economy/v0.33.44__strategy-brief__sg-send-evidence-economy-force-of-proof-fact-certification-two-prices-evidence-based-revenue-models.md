# The Force of Proof: An Evidence Economy Built on Executive Accountability

**date** 5 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)

## What This Is

The commercial thesis that falls out of connecting accountability to evidence: **once executives are personally accountable for accepting a risk, and the graph can connect their accountability statement all the way down to the evidence under it, a force of proof appears, because for the first time the demand for correct evidence is cheap to make and impossible to wave away, so the blind spots execs used to accept, back when evidence was too expensive to collect and did not scale, stop being acceptable, and the pressure for correct data flows downwards through the accountability chain; this splits the register into two pillars that were previously blurred, the party accepting the risk and taking accountability for it, and the party that has to certify the facts, to confirm and warrant that the evidence used for the decision is true, and the trigger that makes both parties reach for their wallets is the width of the risk band, because being asked to accept a massive margin of error forces the question of what it would cost to narrow it; two prices therefore become visible at once, the cost of getting good and accurate data, and the cost of underwriting the risk being taken, and once that financial flow exists there is a commercial model for everyone well poised to supply certified evidence, news organisations, research institutes, researchers, and even departments inside companies, who now trade on facts and evidence rather than attention, on evidence-based revenue models, with a guarantee of correctness attached because there is now a direct line from their statement to the decision it grounds.** It is the eighth brief of 5 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.44 news-backed evidence vault brief, the v0.33.38 risk-acceptance-psychology brief, the v0.33.38 confidence-margin brief, and the v0.33.42 commercial-model brief). New contributions: **the force of proof from accountability plus graph traceability, the split into risk-acceptor and fact-certifier pillars, the two prices of good data and of underwriting, and the certified-evidence economy with a correctness guarantee.**

## Accountability Creates a Force of Proof

The mechanism starts with a chain, not a market. The project lead: **"once you start to get execs to accept risk, you have this accountability, basically a physical chain, this passes to that one, and that person has to be accountable, and it follows upwards."** The new thing is that the chain is now traceable end to end. The project lead: **"because we can graph it, we can now connect a senior exec accountability statement all the way down to the evidence, where it becomes critical that the evidence is right or wrong."** When an accountability statement is linked to the evidence beneath it, the truth of that evidence stops being an academic matter and becomes the thing an accountable person is personally exposed to, which is what turns a demand for proof from a nicety into a force.

## Why Now: Evidence Used To Be Too Expensive To Demand

Executives have always wanted proof; what changed is the cost of getting it. The project lead: **"senior execs really demand evidence, and in the past evidence was hard to collect, too expensive, it did not scale, so a lot of execs accepted those blind spots."** The blind spots were an economic compromise, not a lack of will: when collecting and connecting evidence was expensive and unscalable, accepting some unverified ground was the only affordable option. Graphing the chain collapses that cost, so the compromise that justified the blind spots no longer holds, and evidence that could previously be assumed now has to be shown.

## The Data Flows Downwards

The consequence propagates down the chain under its own pressure. The project lead: **"there is real pressure on the people now having to be accountable, real pressure for the data to be correct, and that means the data is going to flow downwards."** An accountable executive who is exposed to the correctness of the evidence will push that exposure down to whoever supplies it, so the demand for correct data becomes a gradient running the length of the accountability chain, from the boardroom statement to the measurement at the bottom. That gradient is the force that creates the market.

## Two Pillars: The Risk-Acceptor and the Fact-Certifier

The important structural move is separating two roles the register has so far treated as one. The project lead: **"there are two fundamental pillars, the person accepting the risk, taking accountability for the risk, and the person that has to certify the facts, to confirm and certify that the facts and the evidence used for those decisions are true."** The risk-acceptor owns the decision and its consequence; the fact-certifier owns the truth of the inputs to that decision. These are different liabilities held by different parties, and pulling them apart is what makes an evidence market possible, because the fact-certifier becomes a distinct, payable role rather than an invisible assumption inside the acceptor's decision.

## The Wide Band Is the Trigger

What makes the acceptor demand certification is the honesty of the confidence band. The project lead: **"that risk buffer is quite interesting, because suddenly you say, hold on, you asked me to make a decision on a risk band that is massive."** A wide margin of error, surfaced rather than hidden, is precisely what provokes the accountable party to refuse to sign until the band is narrowed, and narrowing a band means buying better evidence. So the confidence band from the corpus is not just an epistemic device; it is the thing that converts unease into a purchase order, the moment where get-more-data acquires a price tag.

## Two Prices Appear

At that moment two financial values become legible that were previously buried. The project lead: **"you start to price the cost, so there are two interesting financial values straight away, how much does it cost to get good and accurate data, and how much are we underwriting of the risk that we are taking."** The first price is the cost of evidence, what it takes to narrow the band to where the acceptor will sign. The second is the cost of underwriting, the value of the risk being carried while the band stays wide. Putting both on the table lets the acceptor make the trade explicitly, pay to narrow the band or pay to carry the risk, which is a decision they can only make once both numbers exist.

## The Evidence Economy

Once evidence has a price and a buyer, everyone able to supply it has a business model. The project lead: **"as soon as you have a financial flow like this, you have a commercial model for the people well poised to provide that evidence, whether news organisations, research institutes, researchers, or even departments inside companies."** The suppliers are broader than journalism: research institutes, individual researchers, and even internal departments who sit on the receiving end of these evidence requests can all be paid for supplying certified ground. The project lead: **"this creates interesting business models for journalists, for research organisations, for people inside companies who are now going to trade on facts, trade on evidence, evidence-based revenue models."** This is the demand side of the news-backed evidence vault from earlier today: that brief built the supply of grounded evidence and the author micro-payment, and this one explains who buys it and why, the accountable executive who can no longer afford a blind spot.

## The Guarantee: Certified, Not Just Provided

The direct line from statement to decision changes what an evidence supplier is selling. The project lead: **"there should be a cost, a requirement, in fact a guarantee that the data is actually correct, because now there is a direct line between those statements and the decisions being made."** Because the evidence is wired to a decision that someone is accountable for, supplying it carries a warranty, not just a delivery, so the fact-certifier is selling a guarantee of correctness and bears a corresponding liability if the guarantee fails. That warranty is what distinguishes trading on evidence from merely publishing it, and it is the reason the fact-certifier pillar can command a real price and not a token one.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim that execs never wanted proof.** They always did; what changed is that proof became affordable to demand and trace.
- **Not one role.** The risk-acceptor and the fact-certifier are distinct, separately liable, and separately paid.
- **Not free evidence.** Certified evidence carries a cost, a warranty, and a liability, not just a delivery.
- **Not journalism only.** Research institutes, researchers, and internal departments are suppliers too.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Certification versus liability | A guarantee of correctness is valuable but exposes the certifier to real liability if it fails |
| Two prices versus willingness to see them | Making the cost of underwriting explicit is powerful but uncomfortable for those who preferred it hidden |
| Independent evidence versus paid evidence | Paying suppliers for evidence must not quietly bias what the evidence says |
| Internal departments as suppliers | Paying an internal team to certify facts can conflict with their other incentives |
