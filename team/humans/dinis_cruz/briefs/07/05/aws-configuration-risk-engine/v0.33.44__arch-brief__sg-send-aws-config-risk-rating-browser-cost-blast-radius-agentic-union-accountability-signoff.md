**date** 5 July 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Architect, @Dev, Product, Strategy

*Part two of a two-part brief. This is the browser rating layer; the companion v0.33.44 dev-brief is the Python engine that produces the JSON objects this layer rates.*

## What This Is

The rating and risk-connection layer that turns the engine's JSON objects into an owned, signed-off risk picture: **the browser layer that consumes the engine's JSON and computes the risk on the client, built on the project lead's own split of the problem into three natures, a deterministic part driven by formulas, a data-driven part driven by statistics, and a connected part where risks link into a graph; its defining move is that not knowing is a first-class output, because there are many moments where the honest answer is that more data is needed, and the rating carries a margin of error, from no risk at all to severe, exactly as the confidence-band brief describes; cost is modelled as blast radius, so a permission to launch EC2 becomes a computable ceiling on spend per region, bounded by the account's vCPU quotas, and the damage window is set by how long that spend stays invisible, since billing data lags by up to a day and only a watch on the launch event, not the bill, closes the window; the agentic case assumes the union of all reachable capabilities can occur, so a human invoker and an agent invoker are both rated against everything the grant transitively reaches rather than the nominal permission; and the layer then connects the risks, visualises the multi-node complexity, resolves who is and is not accountable, and drives what must be signed off, which makes an AWS configuration a concrete demonstration of the Risk Mandate workflow.** It is the second brief of 5 July (cross-ref: the v0.33.38 confidence-band brief, the v0.33.40 agent-authorization brief, the v0.33.35 no-deny risk-register brief, and the v0.33.44 engine dev-brief). New contributions: **cost-as-blast-radius with a per-region spend ceiling and a billing-lag damage window, the agentic union applied to cloud invocation, and the accountability-and-sign-off resolution over the connected graph.**

## The Handover: JSON In, Rating Out

The layer begins where the engine ends. The engine emits typed, evidenced JSON objects and decides nothing about how risky they are; this layer reads those objects and does the rating in the browser. The project lead: **"the actual calculation of the risk is done on the browser, on the UI that we're building for Risk Mandate."** Keeping the rating client-side fits the sovereignty posture, since the configuration facts can be rated without the plaintext leaving the client, and it keeps the seam clean: the deterministic, testable work is upstream in the engine, and the judgment, the uncertainty, and the human decision are here.

## Three Natures of the Problem

The project lead frames the work as three distinct kinds of computation, and the architecture follows that split. The project lead: **"an element which is very deterministic, an element which is all about data, and then the element where we connect the risks."** The deterministic element is the formulas that always resolve the same way. The data element is the statistical calculations, the quantities you can derive from the configuration and public facts without deep business context. The connected element is where individual risks are linked into a graph, and it is where the multi-node picture, the accountability, and the sign-off emerge. The first two can run without human input; the third is where the workflow and the people come in.

## Not Knowing Is a First-Class Output

The most important property of the rating layer is that it is allowed to say it does not know, and treats that as an answer rather than a failure. The project lead: **"there's a lot of moments where we won't know how risky it is, more data is needed."** The rating is not a point, it is a band, the margin of error the corpus already defined. The project lead: **"an open S3 bucket can be from no risk at all to crazy risk and lack of compliance."** A band that stays too wide for comfort is exactly the signal that triggers get-more-data, so the width of the band is itself information, and the layer surfaces it rather than collapsing it to a false precision. This is the confidence-band mechanic applied to cloud configuration: the honest uncertainty is shown, owned, and used to drive the next action.

## Cost as Blast Radius

The clearest worked example of blast radius in this domain is cost, because it is computable without business context. The project lead: **"if you have the permission to create EC2 instances, the potential to create a huge amount of cost is massive."** The question the layer asks is bounded and calculable. The project lead: **"what is the maximum amount of cost that can be done, and you should map this out per region."** The ceiling is real and per-region: AWS meters on-demand usage against a per-region vCPU quota, so the maximum compute, and therefore the maximum burn rate, that a given permission can bring into existence is a number the layer can compute from the account's quotas rather than guess. Cost becomes a first-class, deterministic blast-radius measure attached to a permission.

## The Damage Window: Why the Bill Is the Wrong Alarm

Blast radius is only half the picture; the other half is how long the damage runs before anyone sees it. The project lead's working figure was that the data arrives slowly. The project lead: **"AWS usually takes 16 hours to give you the data, so how much damage can be done in 16 hours."** The grounded reality is if anything worse for anyone relying on billing: AWS cost data refreshes at least once every 24 hours, and every billing-based detector, budgets, billing metrics, and anomaly detection, shares that same pipeline latency, so none of them reliably sees a spend spike within hours. The design conclusion falls straight out: to shrink the damage window you must watch the launch event, not the bill. Resource-launch API calls such as RunInstances flow through CloudTrail to EventBridge within minutes, so a running cost estimate keyed off the launch event closes the window that billing leaves open. The layer models the window explicitly, because the risk of an over-broad EC2 permission is the product of the burn rate and the time it stays invisible.

## The Agentic Assumption: Rate the Union, Not the Grant

Once an agent can invoke a permission, the rating has to change its assumption about what will be used. The project lead: **"with an agent you need to assume the union of all the capabilities can occur."** This is the union-of-possible authorization from the agent-authorization brief, applied to cloud: you do not rate the nominal permission, you rate the transitive closure of everything that permission can reach, because an agent under untrusted influence should be assumed to explore the whole of it. And the invoker is not only an agent. The project lead notes both the human element and the agentic one, so the same permission is rated against a human invoker and against an agent invoker, with the agent case taking the union as its floor. Hope that the agent will not find and use more is not a control; the rating assumes it will.

## Connecting the Risks and Resolving Accountability

The connected element is where the layer earns its place in the Risk Mandate workflow. Individual rated risks are linked into a graph, the multi-node complexity is visualised rather than listed, and the graph is then read for ownership. The project lead: **"we find who's accountable, who's not accountable, and what risks need to be signed off."** The output is a concrete set of decisions attached to concrete configuration facts. The project lead: **"the CI pipeline has the privilege to do this, and that agent has the ability to do this, at this period in time."** Accountability is resolved against the graph, not asserted, so the layer can say precisely which principal holds which reachable capability and therefore who must sign which acceptance, for which interval.

## A Demonstration of the Whole Workflow

Taken end to end, the AWS case is a showcase for the product, not just a feature. The project lead: **"this could be a great demonstration of the power of the Risk Mandate workflow."** It exercises the full loop on a domain everyone understands: the engine grounds the facts, the layer rates them with an honest band, cost gives a computable blast radius, the agentic union sets the assumption, the graph resolves ownership, and the no-deny, accept-for-an-interval, sign-off mechanic drives the decision. It is the frontier's make-it-visceral move applied to cloud, the same way the browser-extension closure makes agent risk tangible, and it develops the core components on a real, familiar problem.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a point score.** The rating is a band with a margin of error; a wide band is a signal, not a defect.
- **Not a billing alarm.** Billing data lags by up to a day; the window is closed by watching the launch event.
- **Not a nominal-permission rating.** For agents, the layer rates the union of everything reachable.
- **Not the deep business-context model.** It flags where business context is needed rather than fabricating it.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Computable cost versus real impact | Cost blast radius is calculable and vivid, but the worst exposure is often data, not spend |
| Union assumption versus noise | Rating the full closure is safe but can overstate risk if every reachable capability is treated as certain |
| Client-side rating versus heavy computation | Rating in the browser preserves sovereignty but bounds how much can be computed locally |
| More-data as an answer | Honest uncertainty is a feature, but a layer that too often says not-yet-known can feel evasive to a buyer |
