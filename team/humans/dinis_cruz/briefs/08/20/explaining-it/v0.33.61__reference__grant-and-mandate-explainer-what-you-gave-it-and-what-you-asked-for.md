# Two Words: What You Gave It, And What You Asked For

**The grant** is everything the credential you handed over makes possible.

**The mandate** is the one thing you actually wanted done.

They are almost never the same size. Everything on this site is about the distance between them.

## What This Looks Like Today

Four ordinary arrangements. None of them involves anybody being careless.

| The agent | The grant: what you gave it | The mandate: what you asked for |
|---|---|---|
| An assistant running on your laptop, under your own login | Every file your account can open. Every credential sitting in your shell and your keychain. Every cloud tool you are already signed in to. Every repository on the disk. The ability to run anything you can run | Refactor one module |
| An agent pointed at a production database | Every table, every row, every customer record in it, and the ability to alter a schema or drop a table | Correct three rows in one table |
| A coding assistant holding a code host token | Every repository that person could reach, read and write, for as long as the token lives | Commit to one branch of one project, this afternoon |
| An agent connected to your mail or your documents | The whole mailbox. The whole drive. Everything anybody has ever shared with you | Summarise last week's messages from one team |

Read the two columns again. **The left column is what is possible. The right column is what anybody intended.**

The reason for the gap is almost never negligence. In most of these cases the narrow version was not on offer. A database has users, not rows. A token's scope list stops a long way short of one branch for one afternoon. An assistant runs as you because that is what running a program on your computer means. **The broad grant is usually the only grant the tool knows how to issue.**

## The Same Thing, With Keys

**A contractor is given the keys to a bank branch to replace a broken light.**

The building is empty. The terminals are unlocked and still signed in. The safe is open. Nothing about the keys says lamp. The keys say building.

**Or, smaller. You give somebody your house keys so they can fetch your passport.**

They can now open every drawer, read every letter, take anything they like, and burn the place down. A locked box in the study does not change that, because they have the house, the privacy, and the whole afternoon.

> **A safe inside a house you handed the keys to is a delay, not a boundary.**

## Why It Usually Works Out, And Why That Does Not Carry Over

Here is the part the analogy hides.

The contractor arrangement is fine in the real world, and it is fine for reasons that have nothing to do with the keys. The contractor has a name. An employer who can be sued. A licence that can be taken away. Insurance. A reputation that follows them to the next job. And a very clear understanding that if the safe empties, somebody will come looking for them personally.

**Every one of those sits outside the key, and none of them exists for a software agent.**

There is no name, only a session. No licence to withdraw. No insurer. No reputation, because the session ends and nothing carries forward. No deterrence, because nothing lands on anybody. And where there is one contractor in one building for one afternoon, there can be four thousand agents at once, each finished before you reach the end of this paragraph.

So the honest version of the story is this:

> It is the contractor with the keys to the branch, except that nobody knows their name, nobody can sue them, they will never work here again so their reputation buys nothing, there are four thousand of them, and they are already done.

## And They Do Not Have To Be Dishonest

The failure people brace for is theft. The failure that actually happens is more ordinary than that.

Somebody telephones the contractor and says the owner has asked him to let a van in round the back. The contractor is not a thief. The request sounds reasonable, and letting the van in is well within what the keys already allow. Vetting him more carefully would not have changed anything.

**Agents are talked into things the same way.** The access is already in their hands. Anything that can put words in front of them can ask.

## The Gap Has A Name

The part of the grant that the mandate never needed is **excess authority**.

```
   +-----------------------------------------------------+
   |  THE GRANT                                          |
   |  everything the credential makes possible           |
   |                                                     |
   |    +--------------------+                           |
   |    |  THE MANDATE       |    EXCESS AUTHORITY       |
   |    |  what you asked    |    nobody asked for it    |
   |    |  for               |    and nobody accepted it |
   |    +--------------------+                           |
   |                                                     |
   +-----------------------------------------------------+
```

It can be counted. It is everything in the left-hand column of that first table which the right-hand column does not require, and in every row above it is most of the column.

And it has one more property worth sitting with for a moment.

Somebody decided the mandate. Somebody said out loud, or wrote down, what they wanted done. **Nobody decided the excess.** It arrived bundled with the credential. There was no meeting where it was weighed, no person who accepted it, no review date, and in almost every organisation no record that it exists at all.

That is what this site is about.

## Editorial Note, Not Part Of The Page

**Purpose.** A first-contact explainer for the grant and mandate vocabulary, aimed at a visitor with no context and a few minutes. It introduces three ideas and stops: the two terms, the asymmetry between them, and the name for what is left over.

**What is deliberately absent.** Every remedy. No bounded credential, no scoped token, no broker, no comparison with any industry that solved this. That omission is deliberate and is at the project lead's direction, so that the page sets the problem up cleanly and the rest of the site carries the answer. **A reader who finishes this page should feel the gap, not be told what to buy.** Anybody extending it should preserve that division rather than helpfully adding a solution paragraph.

**What must not be cut if it is shortened.** The section on why it usually works out. Without it the analogy reassures instead of warning, because a reader's intuition silently supplies the contractor's name, employer, licence and insurance and concludes the arrangement is fine. The bank keys on their own are a comfortable story. The bank keys plus the missing controls are the argument.

**Where the material comes from.** The vocabulary is from `v0.33.61__strategy-brief__grant-is-not-the-mandate-the-gap-between-them-is-the-exposure-nobody-accepted.md`, and the analogy selection, the honest-holder test and the missing-controls list are from `v0.33.61__strategy-brief__analogies-only-work-if-you-name-what-is-missing-hotels-solved-the-bounded-grant-the-law-already-assigned-the-loss.md`. That second brief also carries the business and legal framings, the analogies to avoid, and an audience-by-audience guide, none of which belong on a first-contact page.

**One line held back deliberately.** The companion brief establishes that under the doctrine of apparent authority the excess has already been allocated to whoever issued the credential. It is the strongest sentence available and it needs a legal caveat travelling with it, which is too much weight for this page. It belongs on the page written for executives.
