# Put The Money On Screen: Show What The Next Action Costs, Not Just What The Last One Did, And Abstract Two Seams Rather Than One

**version** v0.33.56
**date** 6 August 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** The team implementing the transcription tool

**type** Dev brief

*Eighth of 6 August. Provider capabilities are checked and cited, and the check settles a design question. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

A brief with a visible half and a structural half, where the structural half is settled by a fact worth knowing before any refactoring starts: **the visible half is to put spend prominently on screen, showing the budget available and the amount spent this session in large figures that open into detail when tapped, and the addition worth making is that the most useful number is not either of those but the cost of the action about to be taken, because a total already spent is information and an estimate before committing is a decision, and the workflow brief written earlier today makes that estimate computable by summing declared step budgets; the structural half is to refactor away from a single provider toward a generic billing layer, and there are two seams there rather than one, since who serves the inference and who is paying against which balance are the same thing only because one key currently does both, and separating them now costs nothing while separating them later means unpicking an abstraction built on a false equivalence; the memo asks whether all of this can be done with keys from another provider, and the answer is no for precisely half of it, because the current provider exposes a per-key endpoint returning the limit, the reset and the remaining balance and documents it as the way to monitor before requests begin failing, while the other provider has no official endpoint for remaining prepaid credit at all, with developer requests for one outstanding across at least seven months and reports of authorisation failures on the billing endpoints even with an administrative key, and no facility for per-key spending caps; so the abstraction cannot be to ask the provider what is left, it has to be that we keep the ledger and the provider is merely where the tokens come from, which is the architecture already decided this morning and is now forced rather than chosen; and the same finding carries a warning, because the per-key spending cap currently doing real containment work does not exist at the other provider, so moving providers without building the replacement loses a control rather than merely changing a supplier.** It is the eighth document of 6 August (cross-ref: the v0.33.56 workflow state machine brief, the v0.33.56 token-gateway brief, the v0.33.56 payments-not-a-billing-system brief, the v0.33.53 voice-note build brief, and the v0.33.53 hand-them-the-iPad brief). New contributions: **the estimate-before-acting number as the one that matters, the two-seam abstraction, the finding that the alternative provider exposes neither balance nor per-key caps, the consequence that the local ledger is forced rather than chosen, and the containment control that would be lost in a naive provider swap.**

## What Goes On Screen

The instruction is clear. The project lead: **"the UI needs to be very visual on the current budget we have available and the budget spent on this session, and these numbers need to be quite big, and you need to be able to then click on it and see the details."**

So: two figures, prominent, tappable into a breakdown. That is the right starting point and it is the live cost display argued for on 28 July, where showing what something actually cost was identified as a trust signal rather than a feature, because it answers the pricing question as an observation instead of a claim.

## Add The Number That Is Actually Actionable

One addition, and it is the more valuable of the three.

A total already spent tells the user what happened. **An estimate before committing lets them decide.** The workflow brief written earlier today makes that estimate available: once steps carry declared budgets, their sum is the maximum a run can cost, computable before anything executes.

So the display should carry three things rather than two:

```
   AVAILABLE          what is left
   THIS SESSION       what has been spent
   THIS ACTION        up to X, before you press the button
```

The third is what turns a meter into a control. It is also what makes the workflow variants from the earlier brief purchasable, because a user choosing between a single-model run and a consensus run needs the price difference at the moment of choosing, not afterwards.

## The Meter Has Three Jobs

Worth separating, because they pull the design in different directions.

**Trust.** Showing the cost openly is the argument that nothing is hidden, and it is why the figure should be visible without being asked for.

**Control.** The user can stop, or choose a cheaper path. This is served by the estimate, not the total.

**Learning.** Over a few sessions the user comes to understand what things cost, which changes what they ask for. This is served by the breakdown behind the tap.

If the three conflict, control wins, because a user who feels able to stop is a user who keeps going.

## Four Practical Notes

**Do not show false precision.** A cost of four thousandths of a penny rendered to six decimal places reads as absurd and undermines the trust the meter exists to build. Round to something a person can hold in their head, and keep the exact figure in the detail view.

**Decide whether the meter ticks during generation.** A number climbing in real time while a response streams is either reassuring or alarming depending on the person, and it is not obvious which. The safer default is to show progress during the run and settle the cost at completion, with the estimate visible beforehand so nothing is a surprise.

**Session needs a definition that survives.** The memo defines it as everything until the browser reloads. That is fine today and breaks the moment the persistence work discussed this morning lands, at which point a session either becomes durable or the concept needs renaming. Better to define it now as a bounded run of work with a start and an end than to inherit a definition from a browser event.

**Reloading from the provider is fine and should not be on the hot path.** The memo allows it. The project lead: **"if some data needs to be reloaded from OpenRouter, we can do it."** Fetch it on load and after a settlement, not before every action, or the meter becomes the slowest thing in the interface.

## The Refactor Has Two Seams, Not One

The structural instruction. The project lead: **"the code should start to refactor away from OpenRouter and start to refactor to a generic billing system, OpenRouter is just one."**

Correct, and there are two different things being abstracted which are currently indistinguishable because one credential does both:

| Seam | Question it answers | Today | Tomorrow |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inference provider** | Who serves this model call | The router | The router, a direct provider, our gateway, a dedicated instance |
| **Credit source** | Who is paying, against what balance | The same key | Our ledger, a customer's own key, a payment presented per request |

They are the same thing only because a single key currently carries both. Every direction the platform is heading separates them: this morning's architecture has a credential resolving to our own balance while routing to whichever provider serves it, and the payment work has a request paying for itself with no credential at all.

**Building one abstraction over both now means unpicking it later.** Two interfaces, even if the first implementation satisfies both with the same object, costs almost nothing today.

## The Other-Provider Test, Answered

The memo asks a good question. The project lead: **"what would be good to do here already is to see if we can do all this, and I think we can, only with OpenAI keys."**

The answer is that half of it works and the more important half does not.

**The current router exposes what the interface needs.** Its documented key endpoint returns the configured limit, the reset schedule, and the remaining amount on that key, alongside the account balance, and the documentation explicitly recommends polling it to track remaining credit and usage before requests start failing. That is precisely the two numbers the memo wants on screen, available from the provider.

**The alternative provider does not.** There is no official endpoint returning remaining prepaid credit. Developer requests for one are outstanding across at least seven months, and a June 2026 report describes billing endpoints returning authorisation failures even with an administrative key. The usage dashboard is documented as aggregated rather than a real-time balance display. Guides exist for retrieving the figure and are explicit that it is not officially supported.

**And per-key spending caps do not exist there either.** Institutional guidance states plainly that it is not currently possible to set alerts or limits per key, and recommends monitoring the dashboard instead. Limits are set at project level.

So the experiment the memo proposes is worth running and will fail in a specific, informative way.

## Which Means The Ledger Is Forced, Not Chosen

That failure is the finding, and it is good news for the architecture.

If the balance cannot be read from the provider, then **the abstraction cannot be a question asked of a provider**. It has to be that we hold the ledger, meter each call ourselves from the usage the response reports, and treat the provider as the place tokens come from rather than the authority on what remains.

That is exactly the design settled this morning: usage events appended, balance derived, threshold as policy. What was argued there on grounds of contention and settlement turns out also to be the only arrangement that works across more than one provider. **The generic billing system the memo asks for and the ledger the platform work specified are the same component.**

The practical implication for this build is that the interface should be written against that ledger, not against a lowest common denominator of two providers' billing endpoints. Where a provider happens to expose a balance, use it to reconcile. Where it does not, the local figure is authoritative anyway.

## And One Control Would Be Lost

A warning that follows directly, and it should not be discovered after the migration.

The per-key spending cap on the current router is doing real work. It bounds what a compromised or runaway client can spend, which is the containment control this corpus has argued for repeatedly, and the 28 July build brief already relied on the same key-level restrictions to make privacy tiers enforceable rather than advisory.

**That control does not exist at the alternative provider.** Swapping providers without building the replacement removes a ceiling and leaves nothing in its place, at which point the only limit is the account balance.

So the sequence matters: the local enforcement has to exist before the provider that enforced it is removed. That is one more argument for building the ledger first and the provider abstraction second.

## Build Order

1. **Three figures on screen**, against the current provider, using its key endpoint. No refactoring. Ship this first because it is visible and cheap.
2. **A local usage ledger**, appended from each response's reported token counts, reconciled against the provider figure where available. This is the component everything else needs.
3. **Enforce the ceiling locally**, so the control exists independently of the provider.
4. **Split the two seams** behind interfaces, with the current provider satisfying both.
5. **Add a second provider** for inference only, keeping credit against the local ledger. This is the test the memo asks for, and it will now pass.
6. **Add our own gateway** as a third implementation of the same interfaces.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not two numbers.** Three, and the estimate is the one that changes behaviour.
- **Not one abstraction.** Provider and credit source are separate seams.
- **Not provider-reported balance.** The ledger is local and authoritative; provider figures reconcile.
- **Not a straight provider swap.** A control is lost unless it is rebuilt first.
- **Not a rewrite.** The first step is visible and touches nothing structural.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Prominent cost versus willingness to use | Visible spend builds trust and makes people hesitate, and the estimate is what keeps hesitation from becoming abandonment |
| Local ledger versus provider truth | Ours is authoritative and theirs is what gets billed, so a reconciliation gap will appear and needs an owner |
| Two seams versus a simple codebase | It is the right structure and it is two interfaces where the team currently has none |
| Estimates versus accuracy | An estimate that is regularly wrong is worse than no estimate, and output length is unpredictable |
| Showing detail versus overwhelming | The breakdown teaches and can also read as an invoice for a two-minute task |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| How is the estimate presented when it is a ceiling? | Up to a figure is honest and reads as more expensive than the likely outcome |
| Does the meter tick during generation? | A deliberate decision rather than whatever the implementation happens to do |
| What is a session, once state persists? | Needs defining now rather than inherited from a browser event |
| Who owns the reconciliation gap? | Local ledger against provider invoice, and what happens when they differ |
| Where does the local ceiling live? | In the client is convenient and unenforceable; enforcement needs to sit where the call is made |
| Which second provider, and for what? | Inference only at first, since credit stays local regardless |
