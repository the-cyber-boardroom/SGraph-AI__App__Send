# Building The Meter Ourselves: Resale Is Prohibited, Which Forces Us Into The Request Path, And That Has A Price Worth Naming

**version** v0.33.56
**date** 6 August 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Engineering, Architecture, Commercial, Board

**type** Architecture brief

*Second of 6 August. The policy trigger and the streaming constraints are verified and cited. Records a correction to two earlier briefs. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

An architecture for metering and selling model consumption, prompted by the discovery that the arrangement the corpus designed in July is not permitted: **the router's terms prohibit selling, reselling, sublicensing, transferring or distributing the service, and separately prohibit accessing it for the purpose of reselling API access to models, with the community reading being that access may not be exposed to end users at all and with enforcement already visible as blocked requests, which invalidates the commercial mechanism designed on 27 July where the margin was expressed as the credit limit on a key issued to each customer, and invalidates the fulfilment workflow specified yesterday which provisioned exactly such a key; the consequence is unavoidable and the memo states it without enthusiasm, that a service which meters consumption must sit in the request path, and the corpus should say plainly that this does not contradict the never-in-line position, because that position is about a governance product declining to be an enforcement point and this is infrastructure whose whole function is to be the meter; being in the path carries a cost that deserves naming rather than discovering, since the privacy modes designed for the transcription product rested on the customer's key going directly to the provider, and a gateway sees everything by construction, so the strongest claim the product currently makes has to be rebuilt on retention rather than on architecture; the ledger should not reserve at all, because the cost of a call is unknown until it completes so any reservation must be pessimistic and would refuse affordable requests, and the better shape is to append usage, derive the balance, permit it to go negative, and make the cutoff a per-customer policy that supports settlement on a threshold; and the streaming proxy is where margin dies rather than where it is technically hardest, because the platform bills for the full function duration and explicitly does not stop a stream when the client disconnects, so an abandoned generation costs both the upstream tokens and the compute that carried them.** It is the second document of 6 August (cross-ref: the v0.33.56 payment-to-funded-key brief, the v0.33.52 first-product commercial brief, the v0.33.52 voice-note architecture brief, the v0.33.50 core-primitives brief, and the v0.33.55 vaults-as-substrate brief). New contributions: **verification that resale is prohibited and the resulting correction to two briefs, the distinction between never-in-line as product positioning and in-line as infrastructure necessity, the privacy consequence of the gateway, the append-and-settle ledger with natively negative balances, and the finding that billing continues after client disconnect.**

## The Trigger, Verified, And Broader Than One Policy

The memo reports a change and it checks out, in stronger terms than described. The project lead: **"it looks like OpenRouter's recent policy changed and they don't allow that anymore."**

The router's terms prohibit customers, and their end users, from selling, reselling, sublicensing, transferring or distributing any or all of the services, and separately prohibit accessing the service for the purpose of reselling API access to models or otherwise developing a competing service. Community discussion in April 2026 reads this as meaning access to the API may not be exposed to end users, and the operator has confirmed active enforcement, describing blocked requests as arising from compliance with upstream provider terms and stating that enforcement mechanisms are updated continuously.

**There is also a second matter worth recording accurately**, because it bears on a different design decision and is easy to overstate. The default posture logs metadata only, meaning timestamps, model, token counts and latency for billing and operations, and does not retain prompt or completion content. Two separate opt-in settings change that, and they are frequently conflated. Private input and output logging stores content for the customer's own review in an isolated, access-controlled project, encrypted at rest and retained for at least three months, under a licence described as revocable and confined to displaying that content back to the customer. A distinct setting, offered in exchange for a one percent usage discount, grants a worldwide, perpetual, irrevocable licence to host, store, modify and distribute the content for the operator's own commercial purposes, including licensing or selling it in anonymised form, and enabling it also enables chat logging automatically.

So this is a trade rather than a default, which makes the practical position narrower than it first appears: the default is acceptable, the observability setting is acceptable given a retention policy and access control, and the discount setting is incompatible with anything a customer would object to seeing resold.

The memo's own reaction is the right one. The project lead: **"it's actually probably good because it is good that it happened before I implemented this stuff."**

## What This Invalidates

Recorded plainly, because two briefs in this corpus are now wrong in their central mechanism.

**The commercial model of 27 July.** That brief established that the margin would be implemented as the issued key's credit limit, with the customer receiving a key funded at a proportion of what they paid, and described this as making the commercial model a property of the key rather than software anybody had to write. That mechanism is not available.

**The fulfilment workflow of yesterday.** That brief specified a payment webhook triggering a function that provisions a key, creates a secret, and delivers a reference to the customer. The delivery half survives; the provisioning half does not, in the form described.

What survives from both is more than it appears: the payment handling, the deduplication and idempotency reasoning, the send-based secret distribution, the persistence ladder, and the observation that persistence changes the business. What changes is the thing being delivered. Instead of somebody else's key with a spend cap, the customer receives **our own credential against our own ledger**, which is the subject of the rest of this brief.

## Being In Line Is Now Forced, And That Is Not A Contradiction

The memo names the consequence and its own discomfort. The project lead: **"the other one is the provision of the tokens, and basically the ability to just make the request and then stream the request back, which is the bit I least fancy, but I don't think we have a choice here, because if we're going to provide the service, we have to be in line."**

That is correct, and the corpus needs to say why it does not contradict a position held since July.

**Never in line is a statement about the governance product.** The risk work has consistently refused to be an enforcement point, refused to sit in the request path of a customer's agents, and positioned itself as the layer that rates whoever does the stopping rather than doing it. That refusal is about authority: a governance system that blocks requests acquires responsibility for availability and becomes something its customers must route around.

**A meter is definitionally in the path.** You cannot charge for consumption you do not observe. There is no architecture in which usage is metered without the meter being traversed.

These are different products with different postures, and the corpus should hold both without embarrassment. What matters is not confusing them: the token gateway is infrastructure, and the risk platform must not acquire an in-line dependency on it in a way that puts the governance product's availability in the hands of the metering product.

## The Price Of Being In Line Is Privacy, And It Is Already Spent

This is the consequence that deserves the most attention, because it undoes a claim the corpus has been making.

The transcription product's architecture, designed on 27 July, offered three processing modes: a routed default, a provider-restricted tier, and a browser-local option. The whole design rested on the customer's own key travelling to the provider, with the corpus never holding the content. The privacy tiers were a differentiator and, on 28 July, guardrails on the issued key made the middle tier enforceable rather than advisory.

**A gateway sees everything.** Every prompt, every completion, every document a customer chats about passes through infrastructure the corpus operates. That is not a policy choice, it is what a meter is.

Three consequences follow and none is fatal, but all need deciding before this ships:

**The claim changes from architectural to operational.** Previously the answer was that we cannot see your content because it never comes to us. Now it becomes that we do not retain your content, which is a promise rather than a property, and a weaker thing to say to the audience that has been most receptive.

**Retention becomes the design.** What is logged, for how long, and what is required for billing are now product decisions with privacy consequences. Billing needs token counts and costs; it does not need prompt or completion text. That separation should be built in from the first version rather than retrofitted, because a log that never contained content cannot leak it.

**The browser-local tier becomes more valuable, not less.** It is now the only mode where the claim remains architectural, which is an argument for building it sooner rather than treating it as a later refinement.

## The Two Systems

The memo separates them correctly and they have very different characteristics.

```
   BILLING                            GATEWAY
   accounts, credits, ledger          authenticate, authorise
   payment intake                     forward the request
   credit grants                      stream the response back
   balance and history                emit a usage event
   reporting and visibility           enforce limits

   not in the hot path                IS the hot path
   can be eventually consistent       cannot be slow
   failure delays a purchase          failure stops every product
```

That right-hand column is the new operational reality: **an outage in the gateway is an outage in every product that depends on it.** The corpus currently has no component with that property. It is the strongest argument for keeping the gateway extremely simple and for making the billing system incapable of taking it down.

## The Ledger: Append, Settle, And Make The Cutoff A Policy

The obvious design borrows from card pre-authorisation: estimate a maximum, reserve it, then settle the real cost. It is the wrong choice here, and the reason is worth setting out because it changes the whole shape of the hot path.

Two facts make reservation expensive. **The cost of a call is unknown until it completes**, because output tokens cannot be predicted, so any reservation must be pessimistic. And a pessimistic reservation refuses requests the customer could comfortably afford, which is a bad experience produced entirely by an implementation detail.

**The better design has no reservation at all.** Every call appends a usage event. The balance is derived from those events rather than maintained as a contended value. Whether to serve a request is a policy decision evaluated against a balance that may be slightly stale, and **the balance is permitted to go negative**.

```
   request -> serve it -> append usage event
                              |
                              v
                    balance derived, eventually
                              |
                              v
              policy: serve while balance > threshold
                      threshold may be positive OR negative
```

That is how utilities and cloud providers have always billed: consume first, settle afterwards, with a credit limit rather than a wallet. It has four properties the reservation model lacks. There is **no contended object**, because every write is an append under a unique key. There is **no retry logic**, because there is nothing to contend for. There is **no synchronous read-modify-write on the hot path**, so latency is a read at most. And **the cutoff becomes a business decision rather than a mechanism**: a trusted customer may run to minus fifty pounds, a new one may not go below zero, and changing that is a policy edit rather than an engineering change.

Native support for negative balances is what makes threshold billing possible at all. Daily, weekly or monthly settlement against an accumulated balance is the normal shape of business-to-business supply, and it cannot be expressed in a system that treats zero as a hard floor.

**The cost is a bounded credit exposure, and it should be quantified rather than assumed.** The worst case is roughly the customer's maximum spend rate multiplied by the staleness window of the derived balance. That number is knowable, it is small at current volumes, and it is the thing to watch as volume grows. Two ordinary controls bound it further: a rate limit per credential, and a tighter threshold for customers without a payment history.

In the corpus's own vocabulary this is clean. **The negative threshold is an accepted risk**, with a stated level, a named owner and a review interval, and the level per customer is exactly the appetite judgement the business should be making rather than the storage layer imposing. Prepaid for new accounts and settlement on threshold once established is the standard progression, and both are the same ledger with a different policy.

## The Streaming Proxy Is Where Margin Dies

The memo identifies the technical worry. The project lead: **"the only limitation I see here is a bit of the lambda functions that might cause a couple little blips."** The concern is right and the shape of the problem is different from expected: the difficulty is economic rather than technical.

The capability exists. Response streaming is available through function URLs, the streaming invocation API, or a gateway proxy integration, and was extended to all commercial regions in April 2026. It is supported on managed Node runtimes, with other languages requiring a custom runtime or the web adapter, and payloads may reach two hundred megabytes with the first six uncapped and the remainder rate-limited.

The problem is this. **Streamed responses are not interrupted when the invoking client connection breaks, and the customer is billed for the full function duration.** For a proxy carrying a model generation, a user who closes the tab thirty seconds into a three-minute response still costs the full three minutes of compute, and the upstream provider has already been paid for the tokens. **The loss is doubled and it is invisible unless somebody measures it.**

Several timeout layers also need aligning, and the documentation is specific: gateway REST endpoints have a five-minute idle timeout for regional and private configurations and thirty seconds for edge-optimised ones, so edge-optimised endpoints should be avoided for this use entirely; the default integration timeout is short and needs raising; and the HTTP gateway variant does not support response streaming at all, only the REST variant.

Three consequences for the design. Set function timeouts from the longest generation actually permitted rather than generously. Consider whether a long-running compute shape suits a streaming proxy better than a per-invocation one, since paying for wall-clock time is exactly what a proxy does most of. And instrument abandonment from the first day, because it is a cost line that will not appear anywhere else.

## Billable Units Beyond Tokens

The memo widens the model and the widening is right. The project lead: **"the billable units can be things like, fundamentally they're going to be tokens, but we could also charge per transaction, charge per bandwidth, charge for all sorts of other metrics, especially any metric that we have across ourselves."** With the example that matters most. The project lead: **"as we add vaults, the vaults will have a size, there should be a cost to it, which is the same cost that we should be passing on."**

That generalises the ledger from a token meter to a consumption meter, and it is worth building for from the start because retrofitting a second unit type into a single-unit ledger is painful. The design implication is small: a usage event carries a unit type, a quantity and a rate, and tokens are simply the first unit.

It also produces a coherent commercial story across the portfolio. One account, one balance, one history, consumed by transcription, by vault storage, by sends, and by whatever comes next. That is a materially better customer experience than separate billing per product, and it is only available if the ledger is generic on day one.

## The Fractal Property, And Where It Stops Being Free

The memo wants systems that can call systems. The project lead: **"this is going to be an open source solution that should be fractal, so one system can call another system, that fundamentally is selling tokens."**

The structure works and has a real commercial use: a partner runs their own instance, buys capacity from ours, and sells to their customers, which is the reseller shape the partner and freelance models have wanted since July.

Two cautions. **Margin stacks multiplicatively**, so three layers each taking a quarter is a markup approaching a doubling, and at some depth the end price stops being competitive with going direct. And **the meter must be authoritative somewhere**: if A resells B's capacity, a dispute about what was consumed needs one record that settles it, which argues for usage events flowing upward through the chain rather than each layer keeping its own count.

The curation idea belongs here too and is better than it sounds. The project lead: **"I do think that OpenRouter can get a bit overwhelming, I'm not sure we need to have 300 models."** A curated list is not a limitation; it is a product decision that also constrains cost estimation, simplifies the rate table, and makes the reserve calculation tractable. It is also, as the memo suggests, exactly the sort of thing a fractal layer would differentiate on.

## Stablecoins: The Right Problem, And The Question Is Timing

The memo raises this and the underlying problem is real and already documented in this corpus. The project lead: **"look at how we can actually add stablecoin support for micropayments."** With the reason. The project lead: **"the Stripe integration is actually quite painful in some ways."**

The specific pain was quantified on 27 July: a fixed fee of around twenty pence per transaction means a one pound top-up delivers the customer only fifty-nine percent of what they paid, which is why the minimum was set at five pounds. That fixed floor is precisely what card rails cannot remove and what alternative rails are designed to avoid.

The honest framing is about which customer. **For a person buying transcription credits, the fixed fee is an inconvenience solved by a larger minimum top-up.** For an agent paying per call in fractions of a penny, it is a hard barrier with no workaround, and the memo has already identified that market. The project lead: **"every agent should have a budget, and this is almost a way to control the agents."**

So this is infrastructure for the agent market rather than the human one, which suggests prototyping it against a real agent workload rather than blocking the human path on it. There is also a regulatory dimension in a European context that should be established before anything customer-facing exists rather than after.

## The Architecture, Assembled

```
   +-------------------+        +---------------------------+
   |   CUSTOMER APP    |        |   PAYMENT PROVIDER        |
   |  (voice tool,     |        |   checkout, webhook       |
   |   vault app, ...) |        +-------------+-------------+
   +---------+---------+                      |
             | our credential                 | verified, deduped
             v                                v
   +===================+        +---------------------------+
   |   GATEWAY         |        |   BILLING                 |
   |   authn / authz   |        |   accounts, credits       |
   |   RESERVE ---------------->|   LEDGER (strong)         |
   |   forward + stream|        |   grants, top-ups         |
   |   RECONCILE ------------->|   usage events (eventual) |
   +=========+=========+        +-------------+-------------+
             |                                |
             v                                v
   +-------------------+        +---------------------------+
   |  MODEL PROVIDERS  |        |  CUSTOMER VAULT           |
   |  (many, curated)  |        |  their logs, their history|
   +-------------------+        +---------------------------+

   hot path: app -> gateway -> provider, with a reservation
   cold path: everything else, eventually consistent
```

Putting the customer's own usage history into **their** vault is the move that distinguishes this from every other metered API. The memo reaches for it. The project lead: **"providing maximum visibility to the user on all the requests made, have all the logs available, which is again where the vaults come into play."** A customer who holds their own consumption record, in a store the operator cannot read, is a claim nobody else in this market can make, and it partially answers the privacy cost incurred by being in the path.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a resale of somebody else's service.** That is what is prohibited and what is being replaced.
- **Not a contradiction of never in line.** That position is about the governance product; a meter is the path by definition.
- **Not privacy-preserving by architecture.** The gateway sees content; the claim becomes about retention, and the local mode becomes more important.
- **Not eventually consistent everywhere.** The reservation must be strong; everything else can lag.
- **Not a token meter.** A consumption meter, with tokens as the first unit and vault size close behind.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Being the meter versus the privacy claim | The strongest thing the transcription product said was architectural and is now operational, which is a real loss whatever the retention policy says |
| A single gateway versus portfolio risk | Every product acquires a shared dependency with no precedent in this estate, and its availability becomes everyone's problem |
| Build it ourselves versus the reason we did not | Reselling was chosen because it avoided exactly this work, and the work has not become smaller, only unavoidable |
| Reserve then reconcile versus friction | Pessimistic reservations mean a customer with credit can be refused a large request they could actually afford |
| Fractal resale versus end price | Margin stacks multiplicatively, and at some depth the customer is better off going direct |
| Stablecoins versus the customers we have | It solves a real barrier for a market that does not yet exist, and building it now competes with the market that does |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What does the gateway retain? | Token counts and cost are needed for billing; content is not, and the separation should be structural |
| Where does the gateway run? | Per-invocation billing suits bursty work and a streaming proxy is the opposite of bursty |
| What is the reservation estimate? | Pessimistic enough to be safe, tight enough not to refuse affordable requests |
| What is the concurrency limit per credential? | A cheap guard that bounds any reservation error |
| Is the ledger generic from day one? | Retrofitting a second unit type is the expensive version of this decision |
| How is abandonment measured? | Billed duration after client disconnect is a cost line that appears nowhere else |
| Does the risk platform depend on this? | It should not, or the governance product inherits the gateway's availability |
| What is the regulatory position on alternative rails? | Established before anything customer-facing, not after |
