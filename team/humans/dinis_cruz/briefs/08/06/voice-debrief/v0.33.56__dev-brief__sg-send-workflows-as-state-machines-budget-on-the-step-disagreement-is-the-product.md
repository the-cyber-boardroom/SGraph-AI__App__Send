# Workflows As State Machines: The Budget Belongs On The Step, And Where Two Models Disagree Is Worth More Than Their Average

**version** v0.33.56
**date** 6 August 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** The team implementing the transcription tool

**type** Dev brief

*Seventh of 6 August. A build brief with a stated sequence. The ensemble claim is grounded and cited, including a result that argues for a specific design choice. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

## What This Is

An implementation brief with two parts that must be built in order, the second depending entirely on the first: **the first is to lift the existing workflow out of code and declare it as a state machine in a file, with each step naming what it needs, what it produces, what is available to the step after it, and what budget it may spend, which is worth doing while the workflow is still trivial because a single linear path is the cheapest thing to model and the model is what everything after depends on; the field that matters most is the budget, because a per-step ceiling declared before execution is the containment control the outbound maturity model identified in July as cheap and unimplemented, it makes a workflow's maximum cost knowable before it runs rather than discovered afterwards, and it maps directly onto the bounded payment scheme established earlier today in which a client signs a maximum the server deducts against; the second part is parallel execution, running two transcription models and consolidating them with a third call, and the technique is genuinely established, since combining multiple recognisers to exploit the independence of their errors is a classical method and recent work replacing voting arbitration with a language model reports significant improvements over it, with late-fusion ensembles reporting gains between one and fourteen percent; but the design as described has a specific weakness, because the consolidating model sees only text and never the audio, so where the two transcripts disagree it cannot adjudicate and can only choose what sounds more plausible, which is precisely the wrong instinct for proper nouns, numbers and technical terms, and there is a published case where a text-only model correcting recogniser output roughly doubled the error rate; the recommendation that follows is to treat disagreement as the output rather than something to smooth away, marking the spans where the models differ instead of silently picking one, which is more useful to the reader and more honest about what is known; and once workflows are declared with models and budgets attached, the same job becomes purchasable at several price points, which turns a workflow definition into something closer to a product line than a configuration file.** It is the seventh document of 6 August (cross-ref: the v0.33.56 402-is-the-protocol brief, the v0.33.52 outbound maturity model, the v0.33.55 demo-review brief, the v0.33.54 pre-approve-the-ladder brief, and the v0.33.56 payments-not-a-billing-system brief). New contributions: **the budget field identified as the load-bearing part of the declaration, declared transitions as a safety property, the finding that a text-only consolidator can degrade output, disagreement reframed as the product rather than a problem, and workflows as purchasable variants.**

## Part One: Declare The Workflow

The instruction is to lift what exists into a declared form. The project lead: **"map the workflow that we have already as a state machine, if this then that, then this, then that."** With the reason for doing it now. The project lead: **"we already have a very simple workflow."**

That is the right moment. The current path is linear and short: an audio file produces a transcription, which produces a summary, which produces an infographic. Modelling a trivial workflow is cheap; modelling a complicated one after it has grown is not, and everything in part two depends on the model existing.

The declaration lives in a file rather than in code. The project lead: **"they should be defined in a JSON file, and each step defines what's needed, what's available for the next step, even what budget you have for the next step, how everything fits together."**

A step therefore carries, at minimum:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `id` | Referenced by transitions and by the execution record |
| `requires` | What must exist before this can run |
| `produces` | What it makes available to later steps |
| `model` | Which model, pinned by identifier rather than by alias |
| `budget` | The maximum this step may spend |
| `next` | The permitted transitions, and their conditions |
| `on_failure` | What happens when it does not complete |

That last field is not in the memo and should be. A declared workflow with no declared failure path becomes an undeclared workflow the first time something times out.

## The Budget Field Is The Load-Bearing One

Worth separating out, because it connects this build to three other pieces of work and is easy to treat as a nicety.

The outbound maturity model of 27 July defined one of its five preconditions as freedom, meaning autonomy, elapsed time and budget without a human gate, and argued that spend and time ceilings are the two cheapest containment controls available and that almost nobody implements them. A per-step budget in a declared workflow **is** that control, applied at design time rather than enforced at runtime by a policy engine.

Three consequences follow immediately.

**A workflow's maximum cost is knowable before it runs.** Sum the step budgets and you have a ceiling you can quote, display, and refuse to exceed. That is the live cost meter of 28 July, computed rather than observed, and it is what makes a price sayable in advance.

**It maps directly onto the payment work of this morning.** The bounded payment scheme authorises a maximum which the server deducts against. A step budget and a signed ceiling are the same number in two places, which means a workflow can be paid for per step with the client's exposure bounded by its own signature.

**And it is the pre-approval pattern from 31 July at a smaller scale.** That brief argued that the decision to spend should be taken at the moment it is cheapest, before commitment. A declared budget per step is exactly that, made by whoever wrote the workflow rather than by whoever is watching the meter.

## Declared Transitions Are A Safety Property

A second reason to do this that the memo does not state. The project lead describes the purpose as determining **"specific sequences of events and actions that should only happen in specific areas."**

That is a containment statement. A workflow whose transitions are declared cannot take an undeclared one. It cannot call a model that is not listed, cannot skip a step, cannot loop where no loop is defined, and cannot spend where no budget is allocated. **The constraint is structural rather than policy-based**, which the corpus has consistently preferred: on 2 August the demo's finite scenario tree was identified as a safety property rather than a demo convenience, because a system that can only produce outputs from a known set cannot be induced to produce something unexpected.

The same argument applies here and is worth stating to the team, because it makes the declaration a security artefact rather than a configuration file.

## Part Two: Parallel Models, And The Technique Is Real

The proposal. The project lead: **"we have two models that do the transcription, and then one extra model, so one call to put them together, so you fundamentally have three LLM calls."** With the reasoning. The project lead: **"we should get an increase in accuracy because the third LLM is only processing the two and basically just consolidating the two, it's not doing audio transcription."**

The technique is established rather than speculative. Combining the outputs of multiple recognisers is a classical method, formalised as recogniser output voting error reduction, which treats several systems as independent knowledge sources and combines them to produce a result with a lower error rate. The underlying principle is that ensembles **exploit the independence of the errors each model makes** to reduce variance and therefore error.

The specific variant described here is also documented. Recent work proposes a unified framework in which multiple recogniser outputs are reconciled by a language model, explicitly replacing voting or other arbitration logic, and reports significant improvements in transcription accuracy over the traditional methods. Late-fusion ensemble work on standard benchmarks reports improvements between one and fourteen percent over strong single models.

So the design is sound in principle and there is a real caveat inside it.

## Where It Fails: The Consolidator Cannot Hear

The memo frames the third model's ignorance of the audio as a simplification. The project lead: **"it's not doing audio transcription."** That is true and it is also the limitation.

**Where the two transcripts agree, the consolidator adds nothing. Where they disagree, it cannot check.** It has no access to the signal, so it selects on plausibility, and plausibility is exactly the wrong criterion for the words transcription most often gets wrong: proper nouns, technical terms, product names and numbers. A fluent-sounding wrong answer beats an awkward-looking right one every time.

Two pieces of evidence make this concrete.

The literature distinguishes text-only language model post-processing from speech-aware post-processing, and reports a case where a text-only model attempting to correct recogniser output on a noisy set **roughly doubled the word error rate**, rising from around thirty-one percent to sixty-five percent, and was described as unable to correct the errors on its own. Improvement came from giving the correcting model signal-derived information rather than text alone.

And this corpus has its own evidence, in the material it is built from. The transcripts feeding these briefs have rendered a well-known author's surname as an unrelated pair of words, an interface name as a different string, and a widely known project acronym as something else entirely. Each was caught only because surrounding context made it obvious. **Two models would plausibly mangle an unfamiliar proper noun in two different ways, and a text-only consolidator asked to pick would pick confidently and wrongly.**

The mitigation is not to abandon the design. It is to change what the third call is asked to do.

## Disagreement Is The Product

The reframing worth building around. The classical method produces, as a by-product of combination, an estimate of confidence derived from how much the systems agreed. That signal is more valuable than the merged text.

So the consolidating step should be asked to **locate disagreement, not to eliminate it**:

```
   model A  ->  "...the OWASP top ten and the Ericsson study..."
   model B  ->  "...the OAS top ten and the Erickson study..."
                        |
   consolidator ->  merged text, WITH the disagreeing spans marked
                        |
   output    ->  "...the [OWASP | OAS] top ten and the
                  [Ericsson | Erickson] study..."
```

That output is better than either transcript and better than a smoothed merge, for three reasons. The reader is told where the transcript is unreliable, which is information a single model cannot provide at all. The marked spans are exactly the places a human should look, which makes review cheap and targeted. And nothing is asserted that neither source said, which is the failure mode of an unconstrained merge.

A sensible policy: let the consolidator resolve freely where the difference is punctuation, casing, filler or obvious formatting, and require it to mark rather than choose where the difference is a content word. That is a prompt instruction, not an engineering problem.

There is also a cheap consequence worth taking: **the proportion of disagreeing spans is a quality score for the file**, available with no extra call. A recording where the models agree everywhere is probably clean. One where they diverge constantly is probably poor audio, and the user can be told so before they trust the output.

## Pick Models That Fail Differently

Following from the same principle. The gain comes from error independence, so two models from the same family, trained on similar data, will make correlated mistakes and consolidating them will produce confident agreement on the same wrong answer. That is worse than one model, because it manufactures false confidence.

The selection criterion is therefore not accuracy but **diversity of failure**, which should be measured rather than assumed: run both over a set of files with known-hard content and check whether their errors overlap. If they mostly do, the second model is buying very little for the cost of a second call.

## Workflows Become Purchasable

The memo reaches this and it is the commercial consequence. The project lead: **"this becomes a cost based approach."** And the shape. The project lead: **"maybe have three or four or five different workflows, each one can be very visual and descriptive, it starts to explain what actually is happening under the hood, and then it has costs."**

Once the models and budgets are declared, the same job exists at several price points, and the difference is explicable rather than arbitrary:

| Workflow | Steps | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| Quick | One model, transcript only | Cheapest |
| Standard | One model, plus summary and infographic | Current behaviour |
| Consensus | Two models, marked disagreement, summary, infographic | Higher, and defensibly so |
| Consensus plus | Two models, plus several infographics to choose from | Highest |

That is a better pricing story than a single rate, because the customer can see what they are buying. It is also, in the corpus's terms, the cost meter made a purchasing decision instead of an observation.

## The Execution Trace Is Provenance

The memo's closing idea, and it is worth more than presentation. The project lead: **"we can use even these flows to show visually what actually happened."**

A declared workflow plus an execution record is a complete account of how an output was produced: which models ran, in which order, what each cost, how long each took, where they disagreed, and which path was taken. Overlaying the record on the definition gives the visualisation asked for, and the same artefact is the provenance record described this morning as the differentiator that survives metering being commoditised.

It belongs in the customer's vault, alongside the output it explains.

## Build Order

The memo asks for sequence and it matters, because part two is unbuildable without part one and the tempting order is the reverse.

1. **Declare the current workflow** in a file and execute from the declaration. No behaviour change, and this is the whole foundation. Done when deleting the file breaks the tool.
2. **Add budget and failure fields**, enforce the ceilings, and surface the computed maximum cost before a run.
3. **Add a read-only visualisation tab** beside chat and debug, rendering the definition.
4. **Overlay the execution record** on that view, which produces the what-actually-happened output.
5. **Add a parallel step type**, with two transcription models and a consolidator instructed to mark rather than resolve content disagreements.
6. **Publish two or three named workflows** at different price points.

Editing workflows in the interface is deliberately absent. The memo agrees. The project lead: **"eventually we allow the UI to edit this, but for now we can hardcode that."**

## Do Not Build A Workflow Engine

A caution for the team, because this brief describes something that could quietly become a general orchestration platform with a visual editor, a rule language and a scheduler.

It should not. What is needed is a small declaration format, a runner that walks it, and a renderer. Three or four hardcoded workflows, a file each, no editor, no conditional expression language beyond what the existing branches require. The corpus's own discipline applies: one thing modelled completely beats everything modelled thinly, and the split between what ships now and what follows should be chosen deliberately rather than discovered.

If a general engine turns out to be needed, it will be obvious, and it will be much easier to build from three working examples than from an abstraction designed before any of them existed.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a rewrite.** The first step declares existing behaviour and changes nothing.
- **Not an orchestration platform.** A declaration format, a runner and a renderer.
- **Not an editor.** Hardcoded files for now, by the memo's own instruction.
- **Not a silent merge.** The consolidator marks content disagreements rather than choosing between them.
- **Not accuracy for its own sake.** The second model buys a reliability signal as much as a better transcript.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Marking disagreement versus a clean transcript | It is more honest and more useful, and some users want one clean text and will find brackets annoying |
| Three calls for one transcript | The literature supports the gain and the cost is threefold, so it should be a purchasable option rather than the default |
| Error independence versus model choice | The gain depends on models failing differently, which is not what model selection usually optimises for |
| Declared budgets versus real variance | A ceiling that is too tight fails legitimate work, and one that is too loose is not a control |
| Declaring the workflow versus the pace of change | Declarations are the point and they add a step to every change while the tool is still moving quickly |
| Visual workflows as a feature | Explaining what happens under the hood is a genuine differentiator and an invitation to build an editor nobody asked for |
