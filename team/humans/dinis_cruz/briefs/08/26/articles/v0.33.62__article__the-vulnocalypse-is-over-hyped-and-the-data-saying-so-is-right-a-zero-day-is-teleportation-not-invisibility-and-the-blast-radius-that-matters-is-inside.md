# The Vulnocalypse Is Over-Hyped And The Data Saying So Is Right: The Vulnerabilities That Are Not Exploited Are The Signal, A Zero-Day Is Teleportation Rather Than Invisibility, And The Blast Radius That Matters Is Inside

**version** v0.33.62
**date** 26 August 2026
**from** Dinis Cruz
**type** Article

*Written from three voice memos recorded after reading a recent document that makes a detailed, data-driven case that the AI vulnerability apocalypse is massively over-hyped, and that the statistics used to justify it are wrong because they do not use a correct definition of zero-day. First person, because it is an opinion piece rather than a brief. The document is not named and its figures are not quoted; this piece agrees with it, adds what twenty-odd years of doing this suggests, and goes into the questions it leaves open.*

---

I have just read a document that does something rare in this industry: it takes the loudest current fear, the Vulnocalypse, the idea that AI is about to find and weaponise vulnerabilities so fast that exploitation overwhelms everybody's ability to respond, and it checks the numbers. Its conclusion is that the fear is massively over-hyped and that a lot of the statistics used to justify it are simply wrong, because they rest on a definition of zero-day that does not mean what people think it means.

I agree with it. What I want to do here is add two things the document does not do. The first is history, because for those of us who have been doing security for twenty-plus years this is a variation on something we have lived through several times, and the pattern is instructive. The second is the set of questions the document does not go into, which are, I think, the ones that actually tell you where the risk from AI is going to land. Because I do think there is something there. It is just not where the apocalypse crowd is pointing.

## The Word Zero-Day Has Been Bastardised

The document's central finding is worth restating because everything else depends on it.

Much of the reporting behind the apocalypse counts a zero-day from the day a national body records the vulnerability. That recording typically happens ten, twenty or thirty days after the fact. Counting from that date inflates the results spectacularly, because it reclassifies a large population of ordinary n-day exploitation as zero-days.

A true zero-day is a vulnerability exploited when there are zero days between the exploit and the patch. Either there is no patch, or the patch came out that same day. From the next day it is an n-day, and n-days are a different phenomenon with a different economics. This is not pedantry. It is the difference between an attacker who had something nobody else had and an attacker who used something everybody had and most people had not applied. Those two attackers have different business models, different tooling, and different responses to any change in the cost of discovery, which is the thing AI changes. Collapse them into one number and the number cannot answer the question being asked of it.

## We Have Seen Mass Exploitation Before

Here is the history the document does not need but the debate does.

Those of us who were around for the Summer of Worms in 2003 remember what an actual mass exploitation moment looks like. Specific vulnerabilities, and specific tools as they were introduced, changed the market overnight. For a few weeks that August there was a whole pile of worms floating around, Blaster and its siblings, exploiting whole classes of servers and bringing large pieces of infrastructure down, not in theory, in practice, on a weekday. Then came Metasploit, which was a very powerful capability and dramatically easier to turn a vulnerability into a reliable, weaponised payload than anything before it. We used to joke that companies patched at the speed of Metasploit, and the joke encoded a principle that has been true ever since: **the risk of exploitation rises when an exploit exists**, and it rises again when the exploit is packaged so that somebody without the skill to write it can run it.

The same shape repeats with every advance in tooling. Static analysis arrived and there was a spike of vulnerabilities discovered and fixed. Dynamic testing, the same. Interactive testing, the same. Each time, somebody predicted that discovery would outrun the ability to respond, and each time the industry absorbed the spike, fixed a great deal, and moved on. AI-assisted discovery is a variation on that pattern, a large one, and it deserves to be treated as one rather than as the end of the world.

## This Is Not A Spectator Sport

The thing the apocalypse framing consistently misses is that this is a two-way ecosystem. Every time we increase the capability to understand vulnerabilities, or to exploit them, we increase the defender's ability to improve their game by at least as much. The capability does not belong to one side.

The clearest evidence is what happened to the research community itself. There was a time when the big security conferences were viewed by industry as a problem: talks were cancelled, researchers were threatened with lawsuits, disclosure was treated as an attack. That is not where we ended up. Some of the largest players came to understand that these people were, hopefully, friendlies, and that although it is embarrassing to have a vulnerability disclosed in public, it is enormously better to learn about it outside an attack than to discover it only because you are being actively exploited, which is a far more dangerous way to find out.

So the industry has operated, for two decades, in a state of balance between what attackers are exploiting, what researchers are discovering, and what companies are patching. The balance moves. It has never broken. Anybody who has done a serious security review and found something genuinely crazy has asked the same question in the room: why has this not been exploited? Why are we the first to find this? And the answer, most of the time, is that it is harder than it looks. Building a payload that is reliable and does not get detected is not straightforward. Attackers, for the most part, run commercial organisations, and for them exploit development is an investment with a return, and a great many vulnerabilities do not clear the bar.

## The Question The Data Does Not Ask

Which brings me to the first question the document leaves open, and it is the one I find most interesting. The debate asks how many vulnerabilities get exploited. The question that actually tells you something is the opposite one: **why have the vulnerabilities that could be exploited not been?**

After a vulnerability is exploited for the first time, the exploit exists and the technique is known to at least one party. Whether it is used again depends on exactly two things: the attacker's business model, and whether the vulnerable thing can be reached and made to cause damage. That second thing is exploitability, and it is almost entirely absent from the conversation about vulnerabilities.

A vulnerability exists in code. Exploitability exists in a deployment. Can the attacker reach it? Is it behind three network boundaries or on the edge of the internet? Does the thing it protects have any value to anybody? The same CVE in a database deep inside an estate and in a service facing the public are not the same risk, and no score attached to the CVE can tell you which one you have. People who patch for a living know this, which is why in practice the attention goes to the issues connected to a threat actor rather than to the issues with the highest score. The data records the score. It does not record the connection.

There is an important property here. Companies will ignore impacted systems and roll the dice on patching, and many do. But once a company is actually exploited we can assume it does something. So a vulnerability that is exploitable, has been exploited somewhere, and keeps showing up in the wild is saying something very specific: **whatever is preventing its exploitation is not the defenders.** The only logical conclusion is that the attacker's business model has not reached it. Nobody can make money from it, or there is no political or ideological reason to bother, and so it sits. That is the strongest signal in the whole dataset, and the dataset is not built to show it.

## The Data Is Skewed, In Directions We Can Name

I do not think we have good data on exploitability, and the skew runs in several directions at once.

**Most vulnerabilities that are discovered never get a CVE.** Imagine the numbers if every finding from a penetration test, a bug bounty, a security review or an internal audit became a record. The count would be far higher and far more representative. The CVEs that exist are mostly there because a company had to publish one. That is a sample selected by obligation rather than by discovery, concentrated in the ecosystems whose disclosure norms produce records. Mapping vulnerabilities from CVE data maps where the records are, not where the vulnerabilities are.

**Most exploitation is never reported.** The requirements are changing a little, but most companies still report an incident only when they must, and historically the data we had was always a small subset of what happened. I have been involved in plenty of incidents that were never reported anywhere, and every practitioner of my vintage can say the same. The CVEs used in those incidents were never counted as exploited.

**Attackers hide which vulnerability they used.** This is the one people forget. It is against an attacker's business model to make it known that they are exploiting a particular vulnerability, because the moment it is known it gets patched and the asset stops producing. The clever ones try not to leave clues about which CVE was used, and they try hardest while it is still in its zero-day state, because that is when the technique is most valuable. I would put forward the hypothesis that a good deal of exploitation of known CVEs is carried out in ways designed not to be recognised as exploitation of that CVE, and it could only be disproved if most companies reported their incidents and their forensics in full, which they do not.

Put those together and consider the figure that circulates in this debate, that only a small percentage of CVEs, perhaps five percent, are ever exploited. That figure counts exploitation that was detected, attributed to a specific CVE, and disclosed. Each of those filters loses most of what passes through it. The reported figure is not an estimate. It is a floor with an unknown multiplier above it, and in that sense it is a smaller number than it looks and a larger one than it says.

## Where A Graph Shows What A Table Hides

This is where my own way of working on these problems becomes relevant, because the skew I have just described is exactly what a graph of graphs makes visible and a table makes invisible.

A CVE in a table is a row with a score. A CVE in a graph is a node, and a node carries no meaning on its own; what it is emerges from the edges you can trace from it. Trace them and the structure appears. There is an edge to the code it lives in and an edge to each deployment of that code. Each deployment has edges to the networks it sits on and to the assets it can reach. Some deployments have an edge to an attacker, meaning somebody with a motive who can get there; most do not. There is a patch event with a date, possibly one or more exploitation events with dates, and the interval between them is the zero-day to n-day delta that I think is the most informative measurement we could be making. A vulnerability patched here, exploited there, and still being exploited at A, B and C months later has a shape, and the shape tells you how hard it is to exploit and who is bothering.

Now draw the same graph for the CVE record as it actually exists. The deployment edges are absent, because the record does not know where anything runs. The attacker edges are absent, because nobody records motive. The exploitation events are present only for the fraction that was detected, attributed and disclosed. And the node exists only because somebody was obliged to create it. The graph shows the record for what it is: a node with most of its edges missing, and the missing edges are exactly the ones that carry the risk.

I have argued elsewhere that the gap is the finding, and this is a clean instance. The absence of a path from an attacker to an asset is the reason a vulnerable node is not exploited, and it is the most valuable fact about that node. A graph shows the absence. A table cannot, because a table has no place to put an edge that is not there.

It also answers the WordPress question. There are enormous numbers of vulnerable installations on the internet, exploitable, known, and unexploited for years. Why? Because they have no assets worth attacking. Their nodes have no edge to anything an attacker wants. Those targets should be removed from the measurement, and we should be measuring only the population that has value to an attacker, because that is the only population that behaves like a market. Log4Shell was the opposite case: widespread, trivially exploitable, and in front of a great many valuable assets. All three edges present, and the result was what you would expect.

And it is the reason I keep saying that vulnerabilities need context. **I do not care how many vulnerabilities exist.** I care how many of them impact the assets I need to protect, are realistically exploitable, and are usable by the threat landscape that is actually hitting my organisation. It is very easy in this industry to talk about movie-plot exploits, the crazy chains that yes, exist, and that the attackers you actually face will never use. Which is why the first thing you have to understand well when you protect an organisation is who your attackers are, what skills they have, what they use, and how they operate. Everything else is a count of nodes without edges.

## A Zero-Day Is Teleportation, Not Invisibility

There is a way of thinking about this that I like a great deal. A zero-day is not an invisibility cloak. It does not make the attacker invisible. What it does is let them teleport, straight into the middle of the organisation, and that is a considerable thing. But teleporting into the middle of the vault where the gold is kept is not the same as getting the gold out. You still have to move through the building. You still have to carry it.

That is why security is layers, and why having the vulnerability is only the start of an attacker's problem. They need to know what to do with it, and they need to know a great deal else besides. I have never liked the line that defenders have to be right every time and attackers only have to get lucky once. It is backwards. For a successful attack the attacker has to get ten things right, in sequence, and at every single one of those ten the defender has an opportunity to break their model and protect the asset that actually matters, whether that is money, customer data, resilience, or availability.

The name of the game on defence, for me, has always been to build a structure that relies on the attacker making a mistake. A mistake is a call that is not supposed to happen, something weird, something that lets you know they are there. Multiple layers, each one a place where the weird thing shows up. A system that is secure has several such layers, does not fail catastrophically, and can absorb a whole range of attacks, which it had better be able to do, because ordinary users put it under strain every single day without meaning to.

## What AI Actually Changes

So, with all that as the ground, what does AI change?

Discovery, obviously. Researchers will find more, and the document is right that this is coming. But notice what more discovery does. We already know that the more researchers find, the more secure applications become, because most companies, once they know about a vulnerability, fix it, and they frequently fix the whole category around it rather than the single instance. A lot of that fixing happens silently, never gets a record, and never appears in anybody's statistics, which is one more reason the data understates how much is discovered and repaired. More discovery is healthy. It is the mechanism by which software has been getting better for twenty years.

AI will also change threat modelling, documentation, and the understanding of side effects and root causes, and it will make exploit creation more effective and the tools better. I do not dispute any of that. But I think the biggest advantage goes to the defender, because the defender's problem has always been understanding. AI will let us understand how our systems actually work, refactor them, restructure them, and build the things we have always known we should build and rarely had the capacity for: air gaps, multiple zones, real segmentation. That is how security is done in the physical world, in ships, in buildings, in anything that has to survive a breach of one compartment, and it has been out of reach in software mostly because nobody could hold the whole system in their head. Now something can.

It also lets us pursue an idea I think is going to matter a great deal, the minimal viable company: the set of services that must always be up, that can never be allowed to go down, that get protected very heavily while everything else gets protected proportionately. That is where the focus should go, and it is another reason vulnerabilities need context, because the only vulnerabilities that matter are the ones on the path to that set.

None of which lands on the externally facing estate, because we already handle the edge. We have a way to deal with it, it is called patching, and the signal I described above says the edge is governed by attacker economics rather than by the supply of exploits. Cheaper exploits shift those economics at the margin. They do not overturn them. Some attackers who were not bothering will start. It is not an apocalypse.

The thing that has changed is not the supply of exploits. It is the supply of attackers, and specifically where they are standing.

## The Blast Radius That Matters Is Inside

Every estate I have seen has an inside that is ridiculously more vulnerable than its edge. Applications that have not been patched in years, because patching them is hard, because there is no test environment, because the team that built them has left, because the change is risky and the thing is not internet-facing so why bother. These are the non-functional reasons vulnerabilities do not get fixed, and they are far more common than not knowing about the vulnerability. The inside is protected by exactly the absence of edges I described: no attacker can reach it, so its exploitability is low, so it sits.

Now put an agent inside it.

Not a malicious agent, necessarily. A motivated one. An overenthusiastic one that has been asked to get something done and has been given, as they almost always are, a grant of capability far wider than its mandate. That agent is running inside the network, it is highly motivated, and it is facing all of those applications that were never patched because nobody could reach them. The missing edge, attacker to asset, is no longer missing. It was created by the deployment, by the defenders themselves, with the best of intentions, to get some work done.

This is where I think the real impact of AI on exploitation is going to be. Not on the edge, where the economics already govern, but on the inside, where agents that go malicious, or go out of control, or simply go further than anybody intended, meet an estate of vulnerabilities that were never exploited only because nothing motivated could reach them. When that meeting happens, all the statistics about exploitation rates are irrelevant, because they were collected in a world where the inside had no attackers in it.

Which is why the whole thing collapses into blast radius reduction. The question stops being how many vulnerabilities exist and becomes what this agent can reach and what it may do, which is the gap between its grant and its mandate. That gap is the agentic blast radius, and it is the only number that predicts what happens when a motivated agent meets an unpatched inside. It is also the thing the layered, compartmented, minimal-viable-company shape of defence is built to bound, which is why I think the two halves of this argument are the same argument.

## The Fix Is Transparency, Enforced

I will end with what I think should be done, because it is not what the apocalypse documents recommend.

The market for vulnerabilities is not efficient, and the reason is that only a small subset of what is discovered and exploited is ever reported. It is against the commercial, political and organisational self-interest of almost every party to report, so almost nobody does, and so we end up without the data needed for any of the analyses above. We cannot measure exploitability, we cannot measure the zero-day to n-day delta, and we cannot see the inside, because the inside is exactly where nobody is obliged to say anything.

So the fix is to force a great deal more transparency on the state of applications and on the state of real vulnerabilities, and to require companies, legally, to disclose the vulnerabilities they discover, whether or not they were obliged to create a record for them. Only then does the market have prices that mean something, and only then can the data support any conclusion about what is coming.

Until then, the honest position is this. The document is right: the zero-day numbers are inflated by a bad definition and the apocalypse is over-hyped. The remaining data shows a small fraction of known vulnerabilities being exploited, and it is skewed in directions we can name but not size. The vulnerabilities that are not exploited are the signal, and they say the edge is governed by attacker economics that cheaper exploits will shift at the margin and not overturn. A zero-day teleports an attacker in; it does not carry the gold out, and a defence built in layers relies on them slipping at one of the ten things they have to get right. The real change is that we are putting motivated actors inside the perimeter for the first time, in large numbers, on purpose, into an estate that was never built to have anybody motivated inside it. That is not a vulnerability apocalypse. It is a blast radius problem, and it is ours to bound.
