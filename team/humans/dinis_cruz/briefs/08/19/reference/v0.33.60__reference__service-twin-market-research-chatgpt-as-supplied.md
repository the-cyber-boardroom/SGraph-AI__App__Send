# Service Twin Market Research: Who Already Offers Credential-Less, Action-Bound Execution for AI Agents?

## Executive finding

As of **19 August 2026**, there is now a recognisable market forming around the architecture you describe, but I could not find a single generally available product that implements the whole **Service Twin / mandate execution broker** model end to end.

The important finding is that the market has converged on **pieces of the architecture from both directions**:

1. Products such as **Infisical Agent Vault, Arcade, Composio and Vercel Sandbox** solve the credential boundary: an agent can cause an authenticated operation while the underlying provider secret is kept outside the agent or model context. Infisical describes Agent Vault explicitly as an open-source HTTP credential proxy where the underlying secret is never exposed to the agent; Arcade similarly injects credentials only at execution time. citeturn4view1turn4view4turn14search6
2. Newer products/projects such as **Permission Protocol, Delego, Inviolet and TrigGuard** solve increasingly large pieces of the *authority* boundary: exact-action authorisation, intent binding, short-lived signed mandates, human/policy approval, cryptographic receipts and offline verification. citeturn20view0turn23search0turn25view0turn19view2
3. What I did **not** find is a mature service that combines those two halves into the exact primitive in your brief:

> **agent → signed action mandate → semantic/provider-specific interpreter → broker-owned credential → constrained execution → broker-signed execution receipt**

That final distinction matters. Permission Protocol and Delego can tell you, cryptographically, *whether this action was authorised*. Infisical and Arcade can keep the service credential away from the agent while an action occurs. But no examined product currently gives you, as one first-class primitive, a portable signed mandate followed by broker-side semantic execution and then a **signed receipt from the party that actually performed the external effect**. citeturn20view0turn23search0turn4view1turn4view4

There is therefore genuine product whitespace here.

My overall assessment is:

| Product / project | Credential never exposed to agent | Action-level semantic control | Signed mandate / intent object | Signed portable receipt | Executes external operation itself | OSS / self-host | Subscription-free route | Overall fit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **Delego + Infisical Agent Vault** | ✅ | ✅/◐ | ✅/◐ | ✅ authorisation trail | ◐ | ✅ | ✅ | **Closest open-source composition** |
| **Arcade** | ✅ | ✅ | ◐ internal policy/context | ❌ not evidenced | ✅ | ◐ | ◐ free tier, then subscription | **Closest integrated commercial runtime** |
| **Permission Protocol MCP Guard** | Depends on downstream server | ✅ | ◐ approval authority | ✅ | ❌ forwards to server | ✅ MIT | ✅ limited free | **Closest receipt/authority layer** |
| **Inviolet** | ◐ documentation is ambiguous | ✅ in supported surfaces | ✅ | ◐ audit/decision evidence | ◐ | unclear | unclear | **Closest explicit “mandate” vocabulary/model** |
| **Infisical Agent Vault** | ✅ | ◐ immature | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ proxy execution | ✅ MIT | ✅ self-host | **Closest credential-isolation substrate** |
| **Composio** | ✅ for managed execution | ✅ tool/action layer | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | ◐ SDK/tooling OSS | ◐ free then $29/month | **Good managed action runtime** |
| **TrigGuard** | N/A | ✅ | request/context | ✅ Ed25519 | ❌ caller executes | unclear | private beta | **Excellent authorisation primitive, not broker** |
| **Nango** | ✅ in proxy/function paths | ✅ if encoded in functions | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | ◐ Elastic-licensed | ◐ | **Useful integration substrate** |
| **Pipedream Connect** | ✅ in managed execution paths | ✅ actions | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | ◐ action sources public | ❌ production plan | **Good connector runtime, weaker architectural match** |
| **Vercel Sandbox credential brokering** | ✅ | ◐ network/request rules | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ egress proxy | ❌ | ✅ Hobby | **Strong specialised credential boundary** |

Here, **◐ means the capability exists only partially, through configuration/custom code, or in a form materially different from your definition**. The scoring is my architectural assessment based on the vendors' currently published material, rather than a vendor claim.

The particularly interesting discovery is that **Delego's documentation almost states your market thesis verbatim**: it describes itself as intent-bound action authorisation that sits between an agent and a credential broker because credential brokers answer whether a credential may be used, but not whether the proposed operation is the thing the human actually authorised. Delego is Apache-2.0 and explicitly says it is *not* itself the credential vault or execution proxy. citeturn23search0turn23search4

That is unusually strong evidence that the architectural gap identified in your brief is real rather than merely a different name for an established product category.

## Market map

The easiest way to understand the current market is as four layers rather than one category.

| Layer | Existing products | What they actually solve | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Credential isolation** | Infisical Agent Vault, Vercel Sandbox, Arcade, Composio | Agent/model does not need to possess the provider's long-lived credential | A valid authenticated request can still be semantically wrong |
| **Action authorisation** | Delego, Permission Protocol, TrigGuard, Inviolet | Evaluate proposed action/context immediately before execution | Often do not themselves hold credential or perform the external effect |
| **Semantic execution** | Arcade, Composio, Nango, Pipedream | Provider-specific tools translate structured actions into API calls | Usually authorise using platform/tool permissions rather than signed single-use business mandates |
| **Evidence** | Permission Protocol, Delego, TrigGuard | Signed/tamper-evident authority decisions | Usually proves the decision, not independently that the external effect was successfully performed |

This market structure is very close to the distinction in your brief between a gateway and an interpreter.

**AWS AgentCore Identity is a useful counterexample.** AWS now has agent-specific identity, token vaults, credential providers and workload identities, but its documentation explicitly describes agents obtaining OAuth access tokens or API keys from AgentCore Identity. In other words, it improves credential lifecycle and attribution but does not implement your stronger invariant that the agent must never receive a service credential. citeturn14search9turn14search15turn14search18

Likewise, its on-behalf-of token exchange can broker downstream access without exposing the application's client secret, but the downstream access token is still ultimately vended for use by the agent/runtime. citeturn14search5

That makes the distinction in your architecture quite concrete:

```text
Credential-broker model

agent
   │
   │ "give me a credential"
   ▼
identity / credential broker
   │
   │ short-lived credential
   ▼
agent
   │
   └──────────────────────────► service


Service-Twin model

agent
   │
   │ identity + mandate + proposed action
   ▼
execution broker
   │
   ├─ verify identity
   ├─ verify mandate
   ├─ interpret arguments
   ├─ enforce business constraints
   ├─ consume one-time authority
   ├─ retrieve/use provider credential internally
   │
   └──────────────────────────► service
                                  │
                                  ▼
                         signed execution receipt
```

The first architecture is becoming common. The second is not.

There are nevertheless two striking signals that the industry is moving directly towards your formulation.

First, an **IETF Internet-Draft published in March 2026, “Credential Broker for Agents (CB4A)”**, explicitly describes the strongest isolation mode as one where the agent never sees, holds or transmits the real credential and where the broker can inspect and filter every request. That is almost the lower half of your Service Twin architecture, although an Internet-Draft is a protocol proposal rather than a commercial implementation. citeturn15search16

Second, a separate **May 2026 IETF draft on signed execution receipts** defines cryptographic receipts for AI-agent tool calls. There is also active research on deterministic pre-action authorisation producing signed decisions/receipts. This is strong evidence that the two primitives your brief combines—credential-less execution and independently verifiable execution evidence—are becoming recognised separately at the protocol layer. citeturn15search2turn15search6

The novelty is therefore not any one primitive. It is **making them one atomic transaction**.

## Closest candidates

**Delego is probably the most important open-source finding for your architecture.** It calls itself “intent-bound action authorization for AI agents” and explicitly positions itself *between* the agent and whichever credential broker holds the user's secrets. Its architecture is:

```text
agent → Delego → credential broker → service
```

Delego performs deterministic policy evaluation, can require approval for sensitive operations, binds authorisation to the particular proposed action so that an approval cannot simply be redirected to another operation, and writes a signed, tamper-evident audit ledger. The project is Apache-2.0 and exposes an open wire specification. citeturn23search0turn23search4

That is remarkably close to the **authorisation half** of Service Twin. The important caveat is equally explicit: Delego says it is *not* a credential vault or proxy; execution is delegated through a `BrokerAdapter`. Its current documentation also says that its protocol's authorisation-token profile is optional and not yet implemented in the current release line. citeturn23search0turn23search8

So Delego does not eliminate your product idea. Quite the opposite: it effectively identifies the missing product boundary itself.

**Infisical Agent Vault is the strongest open-source match to the other half.** Infisical introduced Agent Vault in April 2026 as an open-source credential proxy/vault specifically for agents. The design places a forward HTTP proxy between the agent and external APIs; the proxy terminates the connection, injects the real credential and forwards the request, so the underlying provider secret does not enter the agent's environment. Infisical provides a binary/Docker deployment model and documents self-hosted operation. citeturn4view1

It is especially relevant to your threat model because Infisical explicitly identifies the network boundary as critical: setting `HTTPS_PROXY` is not enough by itself, because a compromised process could bypass it; direct outbound access needs to be blocked so authenticated traffic can only leave through the broker. citeturn4view1

Agent Vault's open-source code is MIT-licensed outside its enterprise directory. citeturn4view2

However, **Infisical itself calls Agent Vault a research preview**, and the published roadmap makes clear why it is not your product yet. The present abstraction is primarily credentials, services, agents and routing/injection rules; deeper firewalling and request-parameter inspection are described as future capabilities. It therefore does not currently provide a first-class signed mandate such as “create exactly this PR against this branch, touching only these three files, once”, nor did I find broker-signed portable execution receipts. citeturn4view1

In other words:

> **Delego understands “was this exact action authorised?” while Agent Vault understands “how can this HTTP call be authenticated without giving the agent the credential?”**

Put the two together and you are surprisingly close to your proposed product.

**Permission Protocol is the closest product I found to your receipt/evidence model.** Its MCP Guard is an MIT-licensed open-source proxy between an MCP client and MCP servers. It sees the precise MCP tool and arguments, applies policy, can hold consequential operations for an identified human signer, and produces a signed receipt bound to that invocation. citeturn20view0turn21view0

Its current product language is unusually aligned with your document: it says every action receives a portable, tamper-evident receipt, and its GitHub/CI product can bind approval to an exact commit, environment and change. citeturn19view0

The open-source MCP implementation hashes the complete tool-call request, records agent ID, tool, decision, policy, target server and timestamps, and places the proxy directly in the MCP transport. citeturn21view0

The important distinction is that MCP Guard **forwards an authorised call to the real MCP server**. Permission Protocol is therefore an authority proxy, not the holder and interpreter of the service credential. Its own architecture diagram is essentially:

```text
agent/MCP client → MCP Guard → MCP server
```

not:

```text
agent → mandate broker holding GitHub credential → GitHub
```

citeturn20view0turn21view0

There is also an identity difference. In the open-source README, `agent_id` is a configuration value used in receipts; I found no evidence in the OSS path of the agent proving possession of its own cryptographic private key in the manner your enrolment/mandate architecture requires. citeturn21view0

The commercial model is also worth noting because it is almost the inverse of what you proposed. The OSS developer tier is **$0 indefinitely for one signer and one connected repository or MCP client**, including cryptographic receipts. Team is currently **$100 per named signer per month**—or $125 monthly rather than annual billing—with actions and receipts deliberately unmetered. Enterprise starts at $150,000/year. citeturn19view1

So Permission Protocol is attractive as an OSS component but **does not pursue pay-per-execution economics**.

**Arcade is the closest integrated commercial execution runtime.** Arcade's architecture keeps OAuth tokens isolated and injects credentials only when a tool executes, rather than returning the token to an agent or MCP client. It then applies authorisation at the action/resource level, and provides pre- and post-tool-call hooks for contextual policies. Its tool layer deliberately maps higher-level agent intentions onto provider API operations. citeturn4view4turn4view5

That addresses a surprising amount of the Service Twin thesis:

```text
agent
  ↓
Arcade tool
  ↓
policy/context evaluation
  ↓
credential injected inside execution boundary
  ↓
provider API
```

Arcade even articulates the distinction between the user's underlying permissions and the smaller subset granted to an agent—for example, a user may have full Gmail rights while an agent can be denied a delete capability. citeturn4view5

Its weakest match to your specification is cryptographic authority/evidence. Arcade publishes rich auditability—agent, user, scope, policy decision and OpenTelemetry output—but I found no evidence of a portable issuer-signed mandate from an external principal, one-time mandate consumption, or an Ed25519/JWS-style execution receipt intended to be verified independently after the fact. citeturn4view4

The OSS story is also qualified. Arcade publishes an MIT-licensed open-source MCP/tool framework with thousands of tool definitions, but that does not mean the complete hosted token vault, policy plane and execution service are open source. citeturn2search1

Commercially, Arcade gets quite close to usage pricing but still has a subscription floor. Its current Free tier has no platform fee and includes 2,000 tool calls and 2,000 auth events per month. Team is **$25/month plus $0.01 per tool call and $0.10 per authentication event**. Enterprise supports cloud, VPC and air-gapped deployment. citeturn4view3

Of the managed offerings I examined, **Arcade is the one I would use as the benchmark against which to position a Service Twin product**.

**Inviolet is unusually close to your explicit mandate concept.** Its mandates are short-lived signed JWTs that bind an actor to an intent card and a requested scope. They include issuer, unique identifier, audience, expiry, organisation, intent, scope and optional prompt correlation; they can be independently verified through published JWKS and revoked. Every tool call presents the mandate to the gateway before policy evaluation. citeturn25view0

That is the nearest commercial documentation I found to the object you call a **mandate**.

Inviolet also goes beyond generic network policy in at least one domain. Its database path includes column-level semantic enforcement and can rewrite or block queries according to the elements permitted by the active grant. citeturn25view3

But the credential boundary is problematic for your stricter definition. Inviolet says it integrates with Vault, Conjur and other PAM systems and says the agent “never holds the real credential”; at the same time, its documented `/mandate/dispense` flow says the gateway can return **both the signed mandate and the resulting Vault/Conjur token in the response**. citeturn24view0turn25view0

That means I would **not classify Inviolet as proving your Service Twin invariant without clarification from the vendor**. A short-lived Vault token may be vastly safer than a long-lived service secret, but your brief is explicitly stronger: *the agent must receive no credential capable of exercising authority against the destination at all*.

Its pricing page was not retrievable during this research and the GitHub link exposed by its documentation did not resolve, so I could not establish an open-source licence or a subscription-free commercial route. That uncertainty itself makes it a weaker near-term candidate than Delego or Infisical.

**TrigGuard is perhaps the closest philosophical match to the signed-receipt half, but currently it is explicitly not the executor.** Its protocol accepts a structured action plus context and idempotency key, deterministically authorises it, signs the permit using Ed25519, publishes verification keys and supports offline verification. citeturn19view2

Its documentation is very clear about the separation:

> the signed receipt authorises execution; the downstream runtime is responsible for enforcing that receipt before committing the effect.

TrigGuard even describes its execution proxy as optional. citeturn19view2turn19view3

As of 19 August 2026, its hosted `/execute` gateway is also explicitly marked **private beta and not yet publicly deployed**, so it cannot presently be treated as an off-the-shelf production Service Twin. citeturn19view2

**Composio is a mature managed alternative if your immediate requirement is “agent can use SaaS accounts without seeing OAuth tokens”.** Its tools correspond to individual external actions with schemas, and execution happens against a connected account on the backend; Composio's proxy execution path injects credentials server-side so application code need not receive the raw credential. citeturn6search4turn6search10

That makes it useful for a practical pilot, but it does not expose your portable mandate/receipt model. Its general API proxy also allows requests to endpoints within an allowed integration domain, which is broader than a strictly interpreted business capability if that proxy is exposed to the agent. citeturn6search10

Composio's current pricing changed for new users from **15 August 2026**. Its Free plan is hard-capped rather than requiring a card, with substantial free tool-call capacity for users bringing their own apps; Pro currently starts at **$29/month** with usage-based overages. citeturn6search0

Its main SDK/tooling repository is MIT-licensed, but I found no basis for saying the complete hosted authentication/execution control plane can simply be self-hosted from that repository. citeturn5search28

**Nango and Pipedream occupy the integration-platform end of the spectrum.** Nango can inject credentials into proxy requests and can run provider-specific functions that model high-level operations, making it possible for *you* to implement a tightly constrained service action. Its Cloud plans currently start at Free, then $50/month and $500/month, with small per-proxy/per-function-run usage rates after included allowances. citeturn8view1

Nango's free self-hosting path is limited to Auth and Proxy; Functions and MCP are not included in the free self-hosted tier, while full self-hosting is an enterprise product. Its repository is Elastic-licensed rather than an OSI-approved open-source licence, despite the company's broader “open source” positioning. citeturn7view2turn9search0

Pipedream Connect similarly stores credentials and can execute thousands of prebuilt actions server-side so they are not exposed to an AI model. Its individual action source code is publicly available. citeturn12search3turn12search7

Its current commercial model meters API usage via credits plus external users, but production Connect usage requires a paid plan; development can be free. citeturn13search1turn14search0

Neither product currently looks like a Service Twin protocol rather than an integration runtime.

Finally, **Vercel Sandbox is a surprisingly strong specialised example of the credential property alone**. Its sandbox firewall can broker credentials at the egress boundary, attaching secrets to outbound traffic without allowing them to enter the sandbox. As of **5 August 2026**, Vercel made the full egress firewall—including credential brokering and request proxying—available on its Hobby tier. citeturn14search6turn14search16

That is an elegant demonstration that the fundamental isolation model is practical today, but it remains a sandbox/network primitive rather than an authorised-business-operation broker. It knows domains, traffic and transforms; it does not intrinsically know “this is the one approved PR with this exact diff”.

## Commercial and open-source reality

Your preferred commercial model—**open source underneath, self-hostable, and hosted consumption available without a monthly commitment**—is actually less common than the security pattern itself.

The current landscape looks approximately like this:

| Candidate | Genuine OSS component? | Can avoid SaaS subscription? | Hosted pay-per-execution only? | Current commercial shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Infisical Agent Vault** | **Yes, MIT** | **Yes, self-host** | No published pure execution tariff | Research preview; commercial path via Infisical |
| **Delego** | **Yes, Apache-2.0** | **Yes** | N/A | OSS project rather than a mature hosted execution service |
| **Permission Protocol MCP Guard** | **Yes, MIT** | **Yes, limited free/self-run** | No | $0 developer; $100/signer/month Team |
| **Arcade** | OSS tool framework; managed runtime not established as OSS | Free development tier | **Almost**, but $25/month Team floor | $0 dev; $25 + auth/tool usage |
| **Composio** | MIT SDK/tooling | Free SaaS tier | No | Free then $29/month + usage |
| **Nango** | Source available, Elastic licence | Limited self-host | No | Free cloud / $50+ plans + usage |
| **Pipedream** | Public action code | Development only | No | Production Connect paid |
| **Vercel Sandbox** | No | Hobby free | Usage characteristics depend on Vercel account | Infrastructure rather than broker product |
| **TrigGuard** | Protocol material/SDK surfaces available | unclear | Not yet | Hosted execution API still private beta |
| **Inviolet** | Could not verify | Could not verify | Could not verify | Pricing not independently established in this review |

The closest way to obtain your desired economics **today** is therefore not a SaaS product at all. It is to run an OSS composition yourself and pay only for the compute/storage/network underneath it. Infisical Agent Vault and Delego are especially attractive because both components have permissive licences. citeturn4view2turn23search0

There is a useful strategic signal here.

The commercial vendors generally charge for one of three things:

**connections/authentication**, as with integration platforms; **tool executions**, as Arcade does; or **human governance seats/signers**, as Permission Protocol does. citeturn4view3turn19view1turn8view1

I did not identify a mature general-purpose vendor whose primary tariff is simply:

> `£0 platform fee + £x per authorised external effect`

That leaves the **commercial model as well as the architecture** relatively open.

Your proposed receipt-based charging mechanism is particularly natural because an execution can be the atomic billing event. I would nevertheless keep the security receipt and billing ledger logically separate: the execution receipt should remain valid and verifiable regardless of whether billing infrastructure is available or changes later.

There is also a subtle advantage in making **self-hosting the default OSS deployment rather than the enterprise edition**. Permission Protocol's on-prem/single-tenant deployment is an enterprise feature, while Arcade reserves advanced VPC/air-gapped arrangements for enterprise; Nango's complete self-hosted feature set likewise moves into enterprise licensing. citeturn19view1turn4view3turn7view2

Your architecture has an unusually strong reason not to follow that pricing convention: the execution broker is deliberately the place containing the usable provider credentials. Customer-hosted credential custody is therefore not simply a procurement preference; it materially changes catastrophic-compromise blast radius.

## What is still missing

After comparing the products, I think your brief contains **six properties that remain meaningfully differentiated**.

The first is **cryptographic agent identity rather than an API-key session or user-selected label**. Several products identify an agent operationally; Inviolet binds an actor into a signed mandate, while AWS has explicit workload identities. But I did not find a general commercial execution broker whose request path is fundamentally “prove possession of this agent private key, then prove that the presented signed mandate names that key”. Permission Protocol's OSS MCP Guard, for example, exposes an `--agent-id` used for receipts rather than a cryptographic proof-of-possession protocol. citeturn21view0turn25view0turn14search9

The second is **an independently issued mandate that is different from the broker's policy decision**.

That distinction is important:

```text
principal / workflow authority
        │
        │ signs
        ▼
     MANDATE
        │
        │ presented by agent
        ▼
execution broker
        │
        │ verifies + consumes
        ▼
      ACTION
```

Most present systems instead look like:

```text
agent request
    │
    ▼
vendor policy engine
    │
    ▼
ALLOW / DENY
```

Inviolet comes closest to the former because its signed mandate is a first-class JWT with scope, audience, expiration and revocation. citeturn25view0

The third gap is **mandatory semantic interpretation of the operation**.

Permission Protocol checks MCP tool names and arguments. Arcade exposes provider-specific action tools and contextual hooks. Inviolet can enforce column-level database semantics. These are significant steps beyond an HTTP gateway. citeturn20view0turn4view4turn25view3

But your GitHub example goes further. A real `create-approved-pr` capability may need the broker to establish facts such as:

```text
repository      == org/project-x
base branch     == main
head commit     == sha256:...
changed paths   ⊆ {src/a.ts, src/b.ts, tests/a.test.ts}
number of files <= 3
no submodule changes
no workflow-file changes
workflow stage  == "implementation"
mandate use     == 0
```

That means the broker may have to fetch repository state, calculate or inspect the diff, normalise paths, understand GitHub's API semantics, perform the sequence of GitHub API operations required to create the branch/commit/PR, and only then consume the mandate.

That is **not something a generic reverse proxy can infer safely**. It really is, as your document says, a provider-and-capability-specific interpreter.

The fourth gap is **single-use authority with atomic execution**. Inviolet has `jti`, expiry and revocation, and TrigGuard takes idempotency keys, but I did not find a generally available service documenting the exact transaction property:

```text
validate mandate
     ↓
reserve mandate use
     ↓
execute external effect
     ↓
commit use + receipt
```

with replay and crash recovery explicitly bound to the external provider result. citeturn25view0turn19view2

This becomes extremely important once “usage_limit: 1” means something consequential such as one payment, one production deployment or one repository mutation.

The fifth—and in my view most important remaining differentiator—is the distinction between an **authorisation receipt** and an **execution receipt**.

Permission Protocol can prove:

> a particular exact action was authorised under a particular decision.

TrigGuard can prove:

> its policy engine issued a signed PERMIT for a particular request.

Delego can maintain a signed action-authorisation ledger. citeturn20view0turn19view2turn23search0

Your broker needs to sign a stronger statement:

> **I, broker B, actually used credential set C's authority to perform operation O at provider P, for agent A, pursuant to mandate M; the provider returned result R at time T.**

That is not equivalent.

A useful receipt could look conceptually like:

```json
{
  "receipt_id": "r_01...",
  "broker": "did:key:broker-public-key...",
  "agent": "did:key:agent-public-key...",
  "mandate_hash": "sha256:...",
  "mandate_use": 1,

  "service": "github",
  "capability": "pull_request.create",
  "resource": "acme/payments",

  "request_commitment": "sha256:...",
  "verified_constraints": {
    "base_branch": "main",
    "changed_paths_hash": "sha256:...",
    "changed_file_count": 3
  },

  "executed_at": "2026-08-19T...",
  "provider_result": {
    "type": "pull_request",
    "provider_id": 184
  },

  "outcome": "executed",
  "signature": "..."
}
```

The provider result itself would not magically become cryptographically true merely because the broker signs it—the broker remains a trusted executor—but a third party could verify afterwards **exactly what the broker claims to have done, under which authority, without relying on mutable internal database records**. The emerging IETF work on signed tool-execution receipts shows that this problem is now receiving protocol-level attention. citeturn15search2

The sixth gap is **a complete system in which the credential-free property cannot be bypassed**. Infisical correctly calls this out: if an agent can connect to GitHub directly instead of using the broker, it can potentially exercise some alternative credential path or bypass the policy layer. Network confinement must make the broker the only authorised route. citeturn4view1

That changes the important security invariant from:

> “The agent is instructed to use the broker.”

to:

> **“The agent is structurally incapable of directly exercising provider authority.”**

That is the important “by construction” property in your brief.

## Recommended path and strategic conclusion

There are two distinct answers depending on whether the aim is to **buy something now** or determine whether there is still room to **build the Service Twin product**.

For a managed solution today, **Arcade is the strongest product to evaluate first**. That leads me to a fairly strong conclusion:

**I would not abandon the Service Twin / mandate-broker idea on the grounds that it already exists. I would narrow the product claim.**

“Agents can use services without seeing credentials” is rapidly becoming a feature offered by several vendors. citeturn4view1turn4view4turn14search6

“Agents require policy before tool calls” is also becoming a product category. citeturn20view0turn19view3

“Agents produce signed authorisation receipts” is now emerging as another category. citeturn19view0turn19view2

The defensible product boundary is the conjunction:

> **A cryptographically identified agent never receives destination authority. It presents a principal-signed, context-bound and optionally single-use mandate for one business operation. A provider-specific execution broker independently interprets and validates that operation, uses the destination credential entirely inside its own boundary, performs only that effect, consumes the mandate and signs a portable receipt for what it actually executed.**

I found **no generally available product in this research that documents that complete contract today**.

And the commercial whitespace is nearly as clear as the architectural one: among the closest managed products, I did **not** find a mature general-purpose offering combining that model with **zero recurring platform fee and pure pay-per-successful-execution pricing**. The practical subscription-free route today is OSS/self-hosting, particularly **Delego + Infisical Agent Vault + a small provider-specific execution/receipt layer of your own**. citeturn23search0turn4view2

For naming, the market evidence actually strengthens the concern already in your brief. The terms now being used by adjacent projects are **execution authorisation**, **credential broker**, **authority proxy**, **intent-bound authorisation**, **mandate**, and **execution gateway**. citeturn19view3turn15search16turn20view0turn23search0turn25view0

Of the alternatives in your document, **“Mandate Broker”** or, more precisely, **“Mandate Execution Broker”** now looks technically stronger than “Service Twin”: it names the two genuinely differentiated things—the authority object and the fact that this component, rather than the agent, performs the effect—and avoids competing with the already-established meaning of digital twin.
