# The Topology: Derivation Removes The Customer Index As An Asset, And Then You Cannot List Your Own Users

**version** v0.33.58
**date** 14 August 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Engineering, Architecture

**type** Architecture brief

*Seventh of 14 August. Extends the per-conversation design into a full vault topology including administrative state. Contains one trade that should be decided deliberately rather than discovered in an incident. Offered to be built on and challenged.*

---

## What This Is

The vault layout once administrative and customer-management state is included alongside conversations, and the trade that arrangement creates: **four kinds of vault appear and they differ in kind rather than in scope, being the per-user conversation vault, a per-user control record holding entitlements and preferences, a small number of shared service vaults holding prompts and configuration, and an operational registry, and the mistake to avoid is treating the last three as one thing because their access patterns, their sensitivity and the functions that touch them all differ; the largest structural win is that deriving the vault identifier from a keyed hash removes the customer index from the system entirely, since nothing needs to be looked up to find a user's data, which means there is no mapping table to compromise and therefore no single artefact that discloses who every customer is; the cost of that win is real and should be chosen rather than discovered, because a one-way derivation means you cannot enumerate your own users, and enumeration is needed for retention sweeps, migrations, incident scoping and bulk erasure, so a registry becomes an operational necessity that should be designed as a deliberate, guarded, append-only artefact rather than reconstructed under pressure; control data belongs in a separate vault from conversation data for a specific reason, namely that the most valuable product property available here is handing a user the read key to their own conversation history, which becomes impossible if the same vault also holds their plan, their internal notes and their quota; shared service vaults are read on every request and written rarely, which is the caching case rather than the contention case, and anything genuinely written per request should append and derive rather than mutate; and the enumeration leak survives all of this, because a party with storage-level visibility can count objects even without reading them, so the number of customers is disclosed by construction and that should be an accepted risk with an owner rather than an unexamined property.** It is the seventh document of 14 August (cross-ref: the v0.33.58 WhatsApp bridge brief, the v0.33.56 vault-architecture brief, the v0.33.56 plugins brief, the v0.33.55 vaults-as-substrate brief, and the v0.33.56 sgit per-path indexes brief). New contributions: **the four-kind topology, derivation identified as removing the customer index as an asset, the enumeration cost named as a deliberate trade, the separation of control data from conversation data derived from the read-key property, and the counting leak stated as an accepted risk.**

## Four Kinds, Not One

The topology that follows from a vault per user, with the distinction that matters most stated first: **these differ in kind, not in scope.**

```
   PER USER                                SHARED
   ------------------------------          ------------------------------
   conv-<derived>                          service-config
     turns/                                  prompts/
     media/                                  models, limits
     thread                                  blocklist
        |                                       |
     read by: generate                       read by: generate, ingest
     written by: ingest (write-only),        written by: administration
                 generate                    rarely
     THE USER MAY HOLD THE READ KEY          cached aggressively

   ctrl-<derived>                          registry
     plan, entitlements, quota                append-only list of
     consent, retention preference            derived identifiers
     internal notes, flags                       |
        |                                     read by: operations only
     read by: control functions               written by: ingest,
     NEVER shared with the user                  write-only
```

Four kinds, four access patterns, four different answers to who may read them. Collapsing them into one vault per user would be simpler to build and would forfeit every property below.

## Derivation Removes The Customer Index

This is the largest structural win in the design and it is easy to miss because it shows up as an absence.

If the vault identifier is a keyed hash of a service secret and the user identifier, then **finding a user's data requires no lookup.** The function derives the identifier and fetches. There is no table mapping people to storage locations.

Which means there is no such table to steal. In a conventional design the customer index is among the most sensitive assets in the system: it discloses who every user is, even to somebody who cannot read a single message. Here **that artefact does not exist**, which is a stronger position than protecting it well.

It also removes a class of consistency problem. A mapping table can disagree with reality, can be partially migrated, can have orphans and can be the thing that breaks during a failover. A derivation cannot.

## And Then You Cannot List Your Own Users

The cost, and it should be chosen deliberately because it will otherwise be discovered at the worst moment.

A one-way derivation is one-way in both directions of usefulness. **You can find a user's vault given the user. You cannot produce the list of users.** And several ordinary operations need that list:

| Operation | Needs enumeration |
|---|---|
| Retention sweep, deleting data past its period | Yes |
| Bulk erasure for users inactive beyond a threshold | Yes |
| Migration to a new format or region | Yes |
| Incident scoping: who was affected | Yes |
| Usage and billing reconciliation | Yes |
| Answering how many customers there are | Yes |

So a registry is an **operational necessity**, not an optional convenience, and the useful move is to recognise that now and design it as a deliberate artefact rather than reconstructing one under pressure.

Designed deliberately, it can be made much safer than the mapping table it replaces:

**Append-only, and write-only from the hot path.** Ingest appends a derived identifier the first time it sees one, using a channel it cannot read back. That is the pattern this corpus has adopted five times, and here it means the component nearest the outside world cannot enumerate anybody.

**Derived identifiers only.** The registry holds hashes, not phone numbers. Compromising it yields a count and a set of opaque identifiers, not a customer list. That is a materially smaller loss than the table it replaces, and it is the whole point of storing it this way.

**Read by operations, never by request handling.** Nothing on the message path needs to read the registry, so nothing on the message path should be able to.

The honest note is that the registry is still the most sensitive artefact in the system, because it discloses the size and shape of the customer base. It is simply much less bad than an index of phone numbers.

## Separate Control Data From Conversation Data

The reason is specific and it follows from the most valuable product property in the design.

The previous brief identified that a user can be handed the **read key to their own conversation history**, which is genuinely unusual and defensible. That property dies the moment the same vault also contains their plan, their quota, their consent record and any internal flag an operator has set on the account.

So they are separate vaults, and the split is the same least-privilege argument the corpus made about the payments architecture on 6 August, where billing was separated so that the most exposed component held a credential to the smallest and least sensitive vault.

| | Conversation vault | Control vault |
|---|---|---|
| Contents | Turns, media, summary | Plan, entitlements, quota, consent, flags |
| Read by | Generation | Control and billing functions |
| Written by | Ingest, generation | Administration, billing |
| Shareable with the user | **Yes, that is the point** | No |
| On the message hot path | Yes | Only for a quota check |

One consequence worth stating: **a data subject request covers both.** Personal data is not only the messages, so export means the conversation vault plus the relevant contents of the control vault, and the control vault is the one that will need a rendering rather than a handover. Better to know that when designing it than when answering the first request.

## Shared Vaults Are A Caching Case, Not A Contention Case

Prompts, model configuration, limits and a blocklist are read on nearly every request and written rarely. That is the opposite of the problem the corpus worried about in the payments work.

The sgit identifier scheme already carries the answer, since the mutability segment tells the server how to cache. A configuration vault written only by an administrative process is **single-writer**, which earns short-lived server caching, and that matters far more here than at low volume because it is read constantly.

The rule that follows: **anything written per request must not live in a shared vault.** Counters, quota decrements and usage totals are exactly the contended-object problem, and the answer is the one settled on 6 August: append usage events under unique keys and derive the total, rather than mutating a shared value. Applied here, a per-user quota decrement belongs in the user's own control vault as an appended event, not as a field in a shared table.

## The Capability Matrix

The whole point of separating them is that different functions hold different keys, so the matrix is the design rather than documentation of it.

| Function | Conversation | Control | Service config | Registry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ingest** | Write only | none | Read | **Write only** |
| **Generate** | Read, write | none | Read | none |
| **Quota or billing** | none | Read, write | Read | none |
| **Administration** | none | Read, write | Read, write | Read |
| **Operations** | none | none | Read | Read |

Two properties fall out that are worth naming.

**No function holds everything.** There is no key that opens a conversation and the registry and the control record, so no single compromise yields both who your users are and what they said.

**And the outermost function is the weakest.** Ingest, the component reachable from the outside world, can append to two places and read one shared configuration. It cannot read a message, cannot enumerate, and cannot see a plan.

## What Leaks Anyway

Stated because the corpus recorded it on 4 August and it applies here with more force.

Deterministic naming is what makes a vault reachable in one request without a lookup. It also means that **a party with read access at the storage layer can count objects and observe their timing without decrypting anything.**

Concretely, that discloses the number of customers, roughly when each became active, and how often each is used. Not who they are, not what they said, but the shape of the business.

For most purposes that is acceptable. It should be an **accepted risk with a named owner and a stated level**, in the corpus's own terms, rather than an unexamined property, and it is worth writing down before somebody discovers it and assumes it was missed. Partial mitigations exist, including spreading vaults across prefixes and padding activity, and none of them is free.

## Sequencing

Ordered so that each step is useful alone and the hardest decision comes with evidence.

1. **Service configuration vault first.** Prompts and limits. No hot path, no personal data, immediate benefit, and it proves the client works inside a function.
2. **Conversation vaults**, alongside the existing store, with read latency compared before anything switches.
3. **The registry**, from the moment conversation vaults exist. It must be populated from the first write, because a registry started late cannot recover the users it missed. **This is the step most likely to be deferred and the one that cannot be.**
4. **Control vaults**, once there is anything to put in them beyond a retention preference.
5. **The capability split**, once ingest and generation are separate functions.

Step three deserves the emphasis. Everything else can be added later; a registry that starts in month three has a permanent hole where months one and two should be, and the derivation that makes the design good is exactly what prevents backfilling it.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not one vault per user.** Four kinds, differing in access pattern and sensitivity.
- **Not a mapping table.** Derivation removes the customer index as an asset entirely.
- **Not free of enumeration cost.** You gain a missing attack surface and lose the ability to list your users.
- **Not a shared counter.** Per-request writes append in the user's own vault and are derived.
- **Not opaque at the storage layer.** Customer count and activity timing are disclosed by construction.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Derivation against enumeration | Removing the mapping is the strongest property here and it removes an operational capability you will need |
| The registry | It is necessary and it recreates a smaller version of the artefact derivation was meant to eliminate |
| Four vault kinds | Each separation buys a real property and multiplies the key management and the number of things that can be misconfigured |
| Sharing the conversation read key | It is the best product property available and it constrains forever what may be stored in that vault |
| Deterministic naming | One-request reads depend on it and it discloses the shape of the customer base |
| Separate control vault | Least privilege is right and it means a quota check touches a second vault on the hot path |

## Open Questions

| Question | Notes |
|----------|-------|
| What exactly is in the registry? | Derived identifier and first-seen at minimum, and every additional field makes it more valuable to steal |
| Where does the service secret live, and how is it rotated? | Rotating it changes every derived identifier, which is a migration rather than a key change |
| Is a quota check on the hot path? | It costs a second vault read per message, and caching it undermines the point of checking |
| How is control data exported for a subject request? | It needs rendering rather than a read key handover |
| Is the counting leak accepted? | It should have a stated level and an owner rather than being unexamined |
| What happens when a user appears on a second identifier? | The same person, two derived vaults, and no way to link them without a mapping |
| Who can create a control vault flag? | Writing to a user's control record is a privileged act and needs its own audit |
