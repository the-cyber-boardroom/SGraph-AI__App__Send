# Mandate, Capability, Control, Authorization, Action: A Semantic Ontology, And Why Most Authorization Is Hope-Based

**from** Human (project lead)

## What This Is

A precise semantic ontology for five terms that are usually conflated, and the consequence of separating them: **a mandate is the bundle of authority conferred on an agent; a capability is what the agent can actually reach or do, which exists once conferred whether or not it is exercised; authorization is the conferral of that capability, so the moment of authorization is the grant, not the action; a control is a later, often defeasible decision about whether to exercise a capability that already exists; and an action is the exercise of a capability, so if an action is possible, the capability was conferred. Visualised, the common authorization flow, where an agent receiving an action gets a decision from a control, is hope-based authorization, because the system's security hopes the control makes the right decision every time. The real-world failure is that authorization decisions are spread across many layers of business logic with blind spots, usually from complexity and hard-to-test scenarios, and that is what lets an action occur that every control should have stopped. The axiom underneath is that if an action is possible, the capability was conferred somewhere, so someone authorised it, and a control choosing not to act is a choice not to exercise a held capability, not the absence of authorization.** It extends the moment-of-authorisation principle into a working vocabulary (cross-ref: the v0.33.31 moment-of-authorisation brief, the v0.33.30 ontology brief, the v0.33.40 risk-acceptance brief, and the hope-driven-development concept). New contributions: **the five definitions, the capability-versus-control distinction, and the hope-based-authorization framing.**

## The Framing

Why precision matters here: **conflating these terms is where the reasoning goes wrong.**

Mandate, capability, control, authorization, and action are used loosely and often interchangeably, and that looseness hides the most important distinction in the whole model: between the capability that was conferred and the control that decides whether to use it. Defining the five precisely makes that distinction visible.

## The Five Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Mandate | The bundle of authority conferred on an agent at the grant |
| Capability | What the agent can actually reach or do; it exists once conferred, whether or not it is exercised |
| Authorization | The conferral of capability; the moment of authorization is the grant, not the action |
| Control | A decision layer that governs whether a capability is exercised; applied after the grant, and often defeasible |
| Action | The exercise of a capability; if an action is possible, the capability was conferred |

The load-bearing pair is capability and control. A capability is a matter of fact: the agent either can reach the thing or it cannot. A control is a matter of decision: given that the agent can, something chooses whether it does. Authorization belongs to the capability, the grant, not to the control.

## The Flow

How the terms connect:

```
  Mandate --confers--> Capability --makes possible--> Action
                            ^                              ^
                            |                              |
                      Authorization                     Control
                   (the grant; the moment        (a later, defeasible decision
                    of authorization)             on whether to exercise the
                                                   capability that already exists)
```

Read plainly: a mandate confers a capability, which is the act of authorization; the capability makes an action possible; and a control, sitting in front of the action, decides whether the capability is exercised. The control does not create or remove the capability; it only chooses whether to use it.

## Hope-Based Authorization

The flow as it is usually built, and why it is fragile: **the agent gets a decision from a control, and the system hopes it is the right one.**

```
  An action is requested
        |
        v
  A control decides         (business logic, a guardrail, an upstream judgment)
        |
        +-- refuse           the system HOPES this happens when it should
        |
        +-- allow            the capability to act was always present
```

When the thing standing between an agent and an action is a control's decision, the security of the system rests on that control deciding correctly every time. That is hope-based authorization: the capability is present, and the system hopes the control will refuse when it should. It is the authorization analogue of hope-driven development. Real authorization is different in kind: the capability is not there, so the action is impossible, and nothing has to decide correctly (cross-ref: the hope-driven-development concept).

## The Axiom: If It Is Possible, It Was Authorized

The accountability ground truth: **possibility implies a conferred capability.**

Objectively and empirically, if an action is possible, then the capability to perform it was conferred somewhere along the line, which means someone authorised it. The fact that some flows do not allow the action to happen, that a piece of code or a workflow decides not to execute it, is business logic applied on top of a capability that is present: whoever made the decision not to execute was able to execute, and chose not to. So a control refusing is a choice not to exercise a held capability, not the absence of authorization. This axiom is what bars the escape hatch of saying nothing was granted: if the action can occur, a grant exists, and the only open questions are whether the grantor understood what they were conferring and its side-effects, and whether they were authorised to confer it, recursively up to the ultimate accountable entity (cross-ref: the risk-acceptance and moment-of-authorisation briefs).

## The Real-World Failure: Layered Business Logic With Blind Spots

Where hope-based authorization breaks: **many control layers, each with blind spots.**

In real systems, authorization decisions are not made in one place; they are spread across many layers of business logic, and those layers have blind spots, usually from complexity and from scenarios that are hard to test. A concrete example: in a chain of agents, upstream layers process and refuse a request, and yet a downstream agent performs the action anyway, because the accumulated context of the chain makes the action look authorised. Throughout, the downstream agent's capability is unchanged, the same tools, credentials, and access in the configuration where it refuses and the one where it acts. The only thing that changed was the conversational context the controls relied on. The action occurs even though the controls should have stopped it. This is hope-based authorization failing at a blind spot: the capability, the real authorization, was present the whole time, and only the controls were trusted to prevent the action, and they were defeasible. It is not authorization appearing from nothing; it is a control failing to refuse what the agent was always capable of doing.

## Why The Distinction Matters

The practical consequence: **never map a control as an authorization boundary.**

If a map of what an agent can do treats a control's refusal as a boundary, it will show an empty or narrow authorised universe for an agent that can in fact perform the action, and the map will be wrong precisely when it matters. The capability must be mapped as reachable, and the controls in front of it must be marked as defeasible decisions, not as limits. Relying on a control is hope; scoping the capability so the action is impossible is security. The map's job is to tell the difference, so the risk that a control might not refuse is a risk a stakeholder can see and accept (cross-ref: the assessment-template and side-effects briefs).

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a claim that controls are useless.** Controls are valuable; they are simply not authorization.
- **Not a single layer.** Real authorization decisions are spread across many.
- **Not about what the agent did.** It is about what the capability allows.
- **Not a rejection of business logic.** Business logic is a control choosing not to exercise a held capability.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the axiom can look tautological.** Saying if it is possible it was authorized seems circular. Mitigation: it is an accountability-forcing axiom by design; its purpose is to bar the no-one-granted-it escape and send you up the stakeholder graph.

**Tension 2: defeasible controls are still useful.** Treating controls as fragile may seem to dismiss them. Mitigation: they are useful and necessary; they just cannot be relied on as authorization.

**Tension 3: blind spots are inevitable.** Complex layered logic will always have untested cases. Mitigation: that inevitability is the argument for scoping the capability over trusting the control.
