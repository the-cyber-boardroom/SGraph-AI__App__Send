# Mandate Is Delegation: The Right To Delegate, And Handing Over The Keys

**version** v0.33.31
**date** 21 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Architect, Security, Strategy, @Dev
**type** Arch brief (in the series)

---

## What This Is

The delegation dimension of the mandate: **granting an agent a mandate is delegating permissions you hold to it, which raises whether the delegator even has the right to delegate, separate from holding the permission, the way cloud platforms separate using a role from being allowed to pass it, and whether all the stakeholders, not just the company, authorised the delegation, recursively to the ultimate accountable entity; it draws the distinction between the historical norm of direct, limited-scope human action, where doing one thing does not spawn others, and agentic flows, which are like handing over the keys to your house, car, or asset, carrying a blast radius of follow-on actions; and it notes that everyday grants, like sending someone an email or a document, confer permission to read, not permission to feed it to an agent that can do far more, which is a delegation the original grant never authorised.** It is, as the project lead says, a document worth exploring on its own (cross-ref: the v0.33.31 moment-of-authorisation and authorization-ontology briefs, the v0.33.40 risk-acceptance recursion, and the v0.33.30 third-party-impact brief). New contributions: **mandate-is-delegation, the right-to-delegate, and the direct-versus-agentic-action distinction.**

## The Framing

Where this starts: **the grant happens at allocation, and the grantor may not be entitled to make it.**

The project lead: **"on the topic of authorisation, the mandate is given at the moment the permission is allocated. There is an interesting set of mappings to do here: does the entity giving the authorisation actually have the right to do that?"** So beyond whether a grant happened, there is the question of whether the grantor was entitled to grant it.

## Mandate Is Delegation

The reframe: **granting an agent is delegating your own mandate.**

The project lead: **"maybe we should break this in two, because a mandate is delegation: you are delegating permissions, delegating mandates that you have, to somebody else."** So giving an agent a mandate is not creating new authority; it is passing on authority the grantor already holds, which means the grantor's own entitlement is what is being extended (cross-ref: the authorization-ontology brief).

## Direct Action Versus Delegated Agentic Action

The historical shift: **from limited direct actions to handing over the keys.**

The project lead: **"up until now, most actions in a business are direct actions from a person. We give permissions to a person, the person has access to specific things, the person does X, and there is a very limited scope of X, X does not then create Y and Z. It is almost the blast radius, but more like the blast actions, what happens after action ABC. The agentic flows are different: it is more like giving the keys to your house, or your car, or your asset to somebody else. You are delegating that mandate."** So the norm we built our permission models around is direct, bounded human action, and agentic delegation breaks it: the agent acts with the grantor's keys and can set off a chain of follow-on actions, a blast radius of consequence rather than a single bounded act (cross-ref: the side-effects brief).

## The Right To Delegate

The first question: **holding a permission is not the same as being allowed to pass it on.**

The project lead: **"does that person have the privilege to delegate? It is interesting, because in AWS security there is the concept of PassRole, a separate permission: you can have permission to something, and a separate permission to pass that role to somebody else. So the question is, does the person who gave the mandate have the privilege to give it away?"** So the right to delegate is a distinct entitlement from the permission itself, and the cloud-platform PassRole pattern, where using a role and passing a role are governed separately, is the precedent: a person may hold access and still have no right to hand it to an agent.

## Stakeholder Authorisation Of Delegation

The second question: **not just the company, but all the stakeholders.**

The project lead: **"the second is whether the stakeholders, not just the company, authorised it. Multiple stakeholders may be managed by multiple people in the business."** So the delegation has to be authorised by everyone with a stake in the assets it reaches, not only the delegator's employer, and that authorisation is recursive, up to the ultimate accountable entity, exactly the recursion the risk-acceptance model describes (cross-ref: the risk-acceptance and moment-of-authorisation briefs).

## The Email And Document Example

The everyday case that makes it concrete: **sending grants reading, not agent-handling.**

The project lead: **"if I send an email or a document to somebody, I gave them permission to read it, by design, because I sent it. But am I really giving them permission to share it with an agent that can do all sorts of other things with it? A lot of the time, no. Do you have the permission to hand over all the keys you have to an agent, which is fundamentally giving it to somebody else?"** So an ordinary grant carries a narrow, implied scope, the recipient may read it, and feeding that same content to an agent is a further delegation the original grant never authorised. The recipient delegating to an agent routinely exceeds what the sender conferred (cross-ref: the third-party-impact brief).

## Why This Matters

The point of mapping delegation: **legitimacy, not just occurrence.**

Mapping delegation, the right to delegate, and stakeholder authorisation is how you tell whether a mandate was legitimately granted, not merely whether it was granted. The moment-of-authorisation principle says a grant exists if the action is possible; this brief asks the next question, whether the grantor was entitled to make that grant, which is what determines who is accountable and whether the delegation should stand.

## What This Asks For

1. **Treat granting an agent as delegation** of the grantor's own mandate.
2. **Distinguish direct action from delegated agentic action** and its blast actions.
3. **Check the right to delegate** as a separate entitlement, on the PassRole pattern.
4. **Check stakeholder authorisation** of the delegation, recursively.
5. **Surface the implied scope of everyday grants** (read, not agent-handling).
6. **Map legitimacy, not just occurrence**, of each delegation.

Estimated effort: moderate; the concepts are clear; the work is modelling delegation and the right to delegate in the ontology and the assessment.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not the same as holding a permission.** Delegation is a separate right.
- **Not a single-stakeholder check.** All relevant stakeholders authorise.
- **Not the old direct-action model.** Agentic delegation hands over the keys.
- **Not implied by an everyday grant.** Sending a document is not agent-handling consent.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the right to delegate is rarely modelled.** Most systems do not separate it. Mitigation: the PassRole pattern shows it can be, and the ontology should add it.

**Tension 2: recursive stakeholder authorisation is heavy.** Tracing it up is work. Mitigation: it follows the same recursion as risk acceptance; reuse that.

**Tension 3: everyday grants are informal.** Implied scope is hard to pin down. Mitigation: state the default narrowly, read, and treat agent-handling as a new delegation.
