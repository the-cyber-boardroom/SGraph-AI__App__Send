# What Sending Something Authorizes: Ten Delegation Scenarios, Expectation Versus Reality

**version** v0.33.31
**date** 21 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Security, Legal, Product, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (scenarios)

## What This Is

An exploration of what sending something to someone actually authorizes, through ten scenarios from simple to complex, each mapping the gap between the sender's expectation, what is actually possible once the recipient connects an agent, and the legal or conventional question of who is responsible: **sending an email is expected to grant reading, but what becomes possible is forwarding, agent-handling, and more; with an NDA, confidentiality, contractual handling, or regulated data in place, feeding the content to a third-party agent may be a disclosure the sender never authorized; encrypted vaults are the contrast, where the grant is scoped, revocable, and visible; WhatsApp shows the accuracy problem, because end-to-end encryption now depends on how many agents are linked to the recipient's account; and it compounds across entities and across agents calling agents, where the authorization flows down layers no one mapped.** It follows the mandate-is-delegation brief and works its idea through concrete cases (cross-ref: the v0.33.31 mandate-is-delegation and authorization-ontology briefs, the v0.33.30 third-party-impact and WhatsApp-case-study briefs, and the v0.33.28 compliance brief). New contributions: **the ten scenarios, the expectation-versus-reality mapping, and the legal-question framing.** This is not legal advice.

## The Framing

What was asked: **define ten scenarios and explore the expectation and the legality.**

The project lead: **"I want to explore the idea that the person giving permission to an agent is delegating permissions it may not have to give. Can we define ten scenarios, from simple to more complicated, including contractual requirements, NDAs, and encrypted data, which is one of the reasons more people should use SG/Send and vaults, and explore how expected each is, and the legality?"** So the work is to make the delegation idea concrete across a range of cases.

## The Past Versus Now

Why this is newly real: **the old narrow scope is gone.**

The project lead: **"in the past there was an unwritten inefficiency. If I send an email to somebody, even when that person was compromised or you got phished from their data leak, the sense was, it is annoying, but it is not like the data you sent was really weaponised. What we are saying now is that when you give an agent access to the files and documents sent to a recipient, you are authorising that agent to do all of it."** So the implied narrow scope of sending, read it, keep it, has been broken by agents that can act on what they read (cross-ref: the why-now inefficiency brief).

## The Ten Scenarios

From simple to complex. For each: what is sent, the sender's expectation, the reality once an agent is connected, and the question it raises.

| # | Sent | Sender's expectation | Reality once an agent is connected | The question |
|---|------|----------------------|-------------------------------------|--------------|
| 1 | A plain email | The recipient reads it | The recipient's agent can read, summarise, forward, and act on it | Was agent-handling ever in scope |
| 2 | A document or attachment | Read, with limited use | Extracted, summarised, shared, and acted on by an agent | Does sharing a file authorise agent processing |
| 3 | Content shared in confidence (implied) | Kept private | An agent's access breaks the implied confidence | Is implied confidentiality binding on the recipient's agents |
| 4 | Content under an NDA | Bound by the NDA; no third-party disclosure | Feeding it to a third-party agent may be disclosure to a third party | Is the agent or its provider a third party under the NDA, and who breaches |
| 5 | Data under a contractual handling requirement | Handled per the contract | Agent processing may breach the handling terms | Does agent handling violate the contract |
| 6 | Personal or regulated data | Handled per data-protection rules | A third-party agent or model service becomes an unapproved processor | Is this an unauthorised processor or transfer |
| 7 | Content sent through an encrypted vault | Scoped, revocable, logged access | Delegating to an agent is itself a visible, governable grant | The contrast: expectation and reality align |
| 8 | An end-to-end encrypted message (WhatsApp) | Only the recipient reads it | Any agent linked to the recipient's account can read it | Is the encryption guarantee still accurate |
| 9 | Content shared two or three entities away | The first recipient handles it | Each hop re-delegates to agents without the sender's knowledge | Who authorised the delegations down the chain |
| 10 | Content reaching an agent that calls other agents | One recipient, one use | Authorization flows down layers of agents no one mapped | Where does responsibility sit across the layers |

## The Accuracy Problem, With WhatsApp

The sharpest case: **the encryption promise is no longer accurate.**

The project lead: **"WhatsApp encryption now depends on how many agents have access to the recipient's account, which depends on how many devices it is connected to. When I send a message, I think, this is encrypted end to end, this is great, but now any agent that person connected has access to it. The challenge is whether that permission is accurate."** So the comforting statement, only the recipient can read this, is no longer true the moment the recipient links an agent, and the gap between the promise and the reality is the issue (cross-ref: the WhatsApp case study).

## The Compounding

Why it grows: **entities away, and agents calling agents.**

The project lead: **"this compounds, because you could be two or three entities away. And what happens when you have an agent calling an agent? How do these multiple agents delegating down the authorization flows connect, and where do the layers of responsibility sit?"** So each hop re-delegates the content further from the sender's original grant, and an agent calling another agent passes the authorization down layers that no one mapped, which is where responsibility becomes impossible to trace (cross-ref: the supply-chain and third-party-impact briefs).

## The Vault Contrast

The case where it works: **a scoped, revocable, visible grant.**

Scenario seven is the contrast: when content is sent through an encrypted vault, the access is scoped, revocable, and logged, so delegating to an agent is itself a visible and governable grant rather than an invisible one. This is the reason, already noted in the corpus, that sharing sensitive content through SG/Send and vaults is safer than sending it loose (cross-ref: the encrypted-spaces and why-share work).

## The Legal And Convention Question

Stated as questions, not answers: **who is responsible, and are people willing.**

The project lead: **"what is worth exploring, from a convention and a legal point of view, is who is breaking the law. If you have an NDA or a confidentiality agreement in place, and you give a third party, an agent, access to that, would that be a problem? And even where there is no strong legal requirement, are people willing to do that, especially with agentic flows where that is literally what is happening?"** So the questions are whether feeding shared content to a third-party agent is an unauthorised disclosure, whether the agent or its provider becomes a new third party or processor, and who bears responsibility, and even where the law is unclear, whether people would accept it if they saw it stated plainly. These are questions to map, not legal determinations, and qualified counsel is needed for any actual case.

## What This Asks For

1. **Map the ten scenarios** against expectation, reality, and the question.
2. **Show the past-versus-now shift** in implied scope.
3. **Surface the accuracy problem** where a promise no longer holds.
4. **Trace the compounding** across entities and agent-to-agent chains.
5. **Use the vault as the contrast** of a scoped, governable grant.
6. **Frame the legal questions** factually, as questions to ask.

Estimated effort: low to map; the value is the clarity, and the scenarios feed the assessment and the case studies.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not legal advice.** It frames questions; counsel decides cases.
- **Not a claim of universal illegality.** It asks who is responsible.
- **Not only the simple case.** It runs from an email to agent-to-agent chains.
- **Not against sharing.** It is for sharing with a scoped, visible grant.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the legal answers vary.** Jurisdiction and contract terms differ. Mitigation: frame as questions, defer to counsel, and focus on expectation and willingness.

**Tension 2: implied scope is informal.** What an email authorises is not written down. Mitigation: state the default narrowly, read, and treat agent-handling as a further delegation.

**Tension 3: the vault contrast can read as a pitch.** Scenario seven favours the vault. Mitigation: keep it factual, as the case where the grant is scoped and visible.
