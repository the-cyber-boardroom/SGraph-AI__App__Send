# Risk Acceptance: Underwriting The Blast Radius, Flowing Upward

**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Architect, Security, Strategy, Risk, @Dev
**type** Arch brief (in the series)

## What This Is

Risk acceptance, the pillar that makes the whole solution work: **you are not predicting the risk of something happening, you are asking owners to underwrite it, insurance-style, because the executive is ultimately accountable, so accepting is underwriting; the risk already exists the moment the permission is provisioned, so the only variable is how long you accept it, until it is re-accepted, eliminated, or the permission is narrowed; saying you are not comfortable changes nothing, you either accept it for a realistic fix-window or remove the privilege now, because every action costs time and money; people only truly engage when they click accept, because that is when they become accountable and stay accountable if something happens later, which creates positive pressure; acceptance is multi-level and flows upward, user to boss to exec to board, each underwriting, consolidated and reported; cross-domain risk ownership is central and uncomfortable; players can object but the top exec has veto both ways, disagree-and-commit; you experiment freely in Explorer but crossing into production is a barrier; and it is a gigantic graph wired to the organisation's ontology and taxonomy, where the hook is risk registers connected to real-world changes and narrowing an agent's permissions improves the risk score.** It is the deep version of the risk-acceptance primitive from the first brief (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 agent-authorisation-blast-radius, terms-and-conditions, and graphs-of-graphs briefs, and the v0.32.3 nhi-2.0 briefs). New contributions: **underwriting-not-predicting, the-risk-already-exists, the click-creates-accountability insight, and the upward-flowing cross-domain model.**

## The Series Framing

What this is: **the pillar that gives the whole solution its stick.**

The project lead: **"this is a big one, risk acceptance, worth its own memo. The whole thing we do only works, only has stick, if you add a risk-acceptance element. It is a massive graph and gets complicated, because risk is complicated, with multiple parties and stakeholders, and we are capturing reality."** So without risk acceptance, the mapping is just information; with it, the mapping drives decisions and accountability.

## Underwriting, Not Predicting

The key reframe: **you are underwriting the risk, like insurance.**

The project lead: **"because what we describe is reality, we are not describing the risk of something happening, we are asking them to accept it, to underwrite it. Maybe the analogy is insurance: you are underwriting the damage, the same way an underwriter underwrites the cost. The business executive is ultimately accountable for everything, so whatever they accept, they are underwriting the risk."** So acceptance is not a forecast; it is an act of underwriting, where the accepter takes on the exposure, and risk flows upward to the ultimate stakeholder, the legal counsel, the board, the owners, or the government.

## The Risk Already Exists

The crucial point: **the risk exists the moment the permission is provisioned.**

The project lead: **"because we are assigning the agent the privilege, the person with the risk has to accept it, because the risk already exists. It exists the moment the permission is provisioned. The only variable to discuss is how long you want the risk to be there, until you have to accept it again, or it is eliminated. Because the permissions can be exchanged: you can go from accept the risk that we can completely destroy the application's data, to some minor data, maximum ten records can be deleted. That is a very big difference."** So the conversation is never whether the risk exists, it is how long it is accepted and how narrow the permission can be made (cross-ref: the nhi-2.0 moment-of-authorisation).

## Saying No Changes Nothing; Accept For A Window Or Remove Now

The hard logic: **discomfort is not a response; removal or a timed acceptance is.**

The project lead: **"say somebody uses Open Claw, or an LLM on their machine with their credentials, and here are the risks, the SLA, the terms and conditions. If they say I am not happy and want it fixed immediately, then they should say, I accept for a minute and I am shutting down my laptop and removing those privileges now. Because if they do not, they have to accept the risk until the realistic time it will be addressed: an hour, a day, a week. Every time an action has to happen, it costs money, somebody's time. So there is no point saying I am not comfortable. The point is the risk exists."** So the only real moves are to remove the privilege now or to accept the risk for a realistic fix-window; expressing discomfort without acting leaves the risk in place (cross-ref: the terms-and-conditions brief).

## The Click Creates Accountability

The accountability insight: **people engage only when they accept, because that is when they own it.**

The project lead: **"from a psychological and accountability point of view, it is only when you get somebody to click, or put an emoji, or accept the terms, that they really engage, because that is the moment they become accountable. And if you accept the risk today and something major happens a week or month later, you are accountable, you should have done something. This creates positive pressures."** So the act of accepting is what makes the risk real to the owner and creates the pressure to narrow or remove it.

## Multi-Level, Flowing Upward

How acceptance propagates: **from the user up to the board, each underwriting.**

The project lead: **"risk acceptance is done in multiple levels and flows upward. A department uses a third-party agent: the person using it accepts the risk, then it goes to their boss to underwrite, then to the exec to underwrite, and eventually to the board to underwrite."**

```
  User accepts
     |
     v
  Boss underwrites
     |
     v
  Exec underwrites
     |
     v
  Board underwrites  (the ultimate stakeholder)
```

## Consolidation And Reporting

What the upward flow enables: **consolidated, reportable risk.**

The project lead: **"you can consolidate: at the moment there are thirty places in your organisation where agents have access to large chunks of the database, or twenty-five agents that could destroy the database, compromise user data, cause harm, or cause the company to go bust. Or, at the moment, zero agents have that capability. You have to report this."** So the accepted risks roll up into a reportable picture, including the reassuring zero (cross-ref: the agent-blast-radius-service brief).

## Cross-Domain Risk Ownership

The uncomfortable, important part: **one team's risk impacts another.**

The project lead: **"it gets interesting with cross-domain ownership. Multiple teams depend on a database, and the financial team might not be comfortable with the risk the marketing team is taking, or the exec responsible for branding or availability says I am not comfortable with another team's risk. But the risk exists, so they have to accept it, a question of how long. This cross-dependency, everybody impacted by the risk, gets more interconnected the further up you go. Like the developer with a production credential on their laptop who gives an agent access: now the agent has production access."** So the model surfaces the discomfort of being accountable for risk you do not control, and the way permissions combine across domains into a larger blast radius (cross-ref: the agent-authorisation-blast-radius cross-linked-risk point and the hope-driven-development concept).

## Veto Both Ways, And Disagree-And-Commit

How disagreement resolves: **the top exec can override, both no and yes.**

The project lead: **"some players might say no, the security team or another team, I am not comfortable with another team's risk. But risk flows upward, so it can be overridden. The top exec has veto both ways, no and yes, and can override. That is okay. It is the disagree-and-commit principle: you might not agree, but if the business decided, you go that way. And execs pushing hard for identity solutions, which is correct, also need to own the risks they are asking the business to take, the potential risks and potential consequences of the permissions people accept."** So disagreement is allowed and recorded, but the upward flow and the top-level veto resolve it, with accountability sitting where the decision is made.

## Explorer Versus Production

Where to be strict: **experiment freely in Explorer, but production is a barrier.**

The project lead: **"it is okay to experiment and take more risks with Explorer technology not connected to real databases. But when you go to production, you cross a barrier. That is when the examples happen, the agent that gave permissions or accepted a transaction it should never have. And the question is, why did the agent have access to do that in the first place. So you contain all this."** So risk acceptance is graduated by Wardley stage: permissive in Explorer, strict and contained at the production barrier (cross-ref: the Wardley explorer-to-town-planner concept and the infrastructure-not-built-for-agents brief).

## The Gigantic Graph And The Hook

What it is, and why it is commercial: **a graph wired to the org, with risk registers connected to reality.**

The project lead: **"risk acceptance is one of the most important pillars, and a gigantic graph that needs to connect with the ontology and taxonomy of the organisation, the semantic graphs of the departments. From a hook point of view, those risk decisions become important to maintain, connect, and wire, so you have risk registers actually connected to real-world changes, and when you change the permissions an agent has, that reflects positively in the overall risk score of the organisation."** So the risk-acceptance graph is wired to the organisation's ontology, and the live, maintained risk register, where narrowing permissions visibly improves the risk score, is both the value and the commercial hook (cross-ref: the graphs-of-graphs and commercial-model briefs).

## What This Asks For

1. **Frame acceptance as underwriting** (not prediction; insurance-style).
2. **Treat the risk as already existing** (the only variable is how long).
3. **Offer remove-now or accept-for-a-window** (discomfort alone is not a response).
4. **Capture explicit acceptance** (the click that creates accountability).
5. **Flow acceptance upward** (user to boss to exec to board, each underwriting).
6. **Consolidate and report** (the rolled-up picture, including zero).
7. **Surface cross-domain risk** (accountable for what you do not control).
8. **Allow veto both ways** (disagree-and-commit; the top decides).
9. **Be strict at the production barrier** (permissive in Explorer).
10. **Wire it to the org graph and risk register** (permission changes move the score).

Estimated effort: high; this is the pillar; the build is the multi-level acceptance workflow, the upward flow and consolidation, and the risk register wired to the org ontology and the blast-radius graph.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a prediction.** It is underwriting an existing risk.
- **Not optional discomfort.** Remove the privilege or accept for a window.
- **Not flat.** Acceptance is multi-level and flows upward.
- **Not unanimous.** Veto both ways; the top decides.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the risk-already-exists reframe is uncomfortable.** People want to debate whether the risk is real. Mitigation: the risk exists at provisioning; the debate is duration and scope.

**Tension 2: cross-domain acceptance creates friction.** Being accountable for risk you do not control is hard. Mitigation: surfacing it is the value; the upward flow and veto resolve it.

**Tension 3: veto-both-ways concentrates power.** The top can accept risk others reject. Mitigation: accountability sits with the decision; it is recorded.

**Tension 4: the upward flow is operationally heavy.** Many acceptances and consolidations. Mitigation: the graph automates the flow, consolidation, and reporting.

**Tension 5: the click must be real accountability.** A click can be theatre. Mitigation: tie it to the named owner, the duration, and the risk register.
