# Mapping The Side Effects: Speaking The Recipient's Language, And Being Fact-Driven

**version** v0.33.40
**date** 18 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Security, Architect, Publication, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (thesis, in the series)

## What This Is

The side-effects primitive in the series: **when you connect the blast radius to risks and to the asset and risk owners, you have to put it in the recipient's context and speak their language, their role, culture, and domain (supply chain, finance, HR, strategy, regulation), because they are accountable for assets that third parties can impact, and you have to map the second, third, and fourth-order side effects, since that is when people truly understand and start generating even more scenarios themselves; the graph must carry the org chart, the roles of the agents and the humans and the interests, and the meta-play of commercial and compliance requirements; and everything is fact-driven, facts backed by evidence, then hypotheses and opinions each with a level of certainty, where some things are certain and detectability shifts the certainty, and the past is evidence too, so you need good data sets.** It delivers the side-effects memo flagged earlier and deepens the evidence model (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 agent-authorisation-blast-radius risk-acceptance primitive, the v0.33.38 confidence-through-evidence thesis, and the v0.33.40 graphs-of-graphs brief). New contributions: **the recipient-language requirement, the side-effects mapping, the graph attributes, and the facts-hypotheses-certainty model.**

## The Series Framing

What this covers: **connecting the blast radius to the risk and asset owners, in context.**

The project lead: **"when we map the blast radius, the permissions, and the orders of magnitude of what is happening, it is very important to connect to risks and to the business owner, or the asset owner, and to put these things into context, the context of the recipient."** So mapping the blast radius is only half the job; the other half is landing it with the people accountable for the assets.

## Put It In The Recipient's Context

Why it has to be in their language: **to get a risk accepted, speak the recipient's language.**

The project lead: **"because we want the risks to get accepted, we have to speak the language of the recipient. We have to explain to them, in a language, culture, and role they understand, what the implications are, what the risks are, who depends on the actions being made, how much control they have of those actions, and how much control they do not have. Those recipients are ultimately accountable for the assets, but the assets can be impacted by third parties, which may or may not be part of the same company."** So acceptance requires translation into the recipient's world, including the uncomfortable cases where they are accountable for what others control (cross-ref: the agent-authorisation-blast-radius risk-acceptance primitive).

## Map Second, Third, And Fourth-Order Side Effects

The critical part: **the side effects two or three degrees out are where understanding happens.**

The project lead: **"it is critical to map the second, third, and fourth-order side effects, and to correctly map what they mean from a business, a consumer, and a regulation point of view. Those two or three degrees of separation become super critical, because that is when people really understand."** So the value is not the first-order finding but the chain of consequences it sets off, mapped into what they mean for the recipient.

## Not Dumbing Down

An important clarification: **this is not about execs not knowing cyber.**

The project lead: **"this is not about dumbing down, or saying the execs are not knowledgeable or should know cybersecurity. Their domain is big enough. We need to explain it from the point of view of supply chain, finance, HR, strategic direction, risk, operations, whatever area the business person focuses on."** So speaking their language respects their expertise; it translates the risk into their domain rather than demanding they learn yours.

## That Is What The Graph Is For

What the graph must carry: **the org chart, the roles, the interests, and the meta-play.**

The project lead: **"that is what the graph is. The attributes we need are the org chart, the roles and definitions, not just of the agents but of the humans and the interests involved, and the meta-play, the commercial requirements, the compliance requirements. Many companies live with tens of business requirements, and we need to take that into account when we connect the dots."**

| Graph Attribute | Why |
|-----------------|-----|
| Org chart | Who is accountable for what |
| Roles of agents and humans | Both act; both must be in the graph |
| Interests | Who wants what; the meta-play |
| Commercial and compliance requirements | The tens of requirements companies live under |

(Cross-ref: the graphs-of-graphs and nhi-2.0 briefs.)

## Side Effects Are Where It Gets Interesting

A telling experience: **show business people what is possible, and they generate worse scenarios than you.**

The project lead: **"every time I got the business people to understand what was possible from a security point of view, they came up with even crazier scenarios, because they know the side effects. They know, I trust this piece of data, I trust that, and if it is not correct, then you have this problem and that problem. And from a regulation point of view, no, we cannot do this, we would lose this licence, and if we lose this licence, what happens. So you walk backwards from it."** So the recipients are the experts in their own side effects, and the map's job is to give them the starting point from which they reason forward to consequences only they can see.

## Fact-Driven: Facts, Hypotheses, And Opinions

The primitive underneath it all: **be fact-driven, with a level of certainty.**

The project lead: **"everything here is fact-driven. We should have facts, hypotheses, and opinions. Facts are super important and should be supported by evidence. The less evidence, the more it is a hypothesis or an opinion, an estimate, where you say we think this could happen and this is our level of certainty. Some things you can have a hundred percent certainty: this S3 bucket is connected to the outside world, so it is certain the data can be exfiltrated, and there is no control to stop it. How well it will be discovered depends: if it is something public, a hundred percent, and once there is evidence it has been found on the dark web, the certainty moves. So certainty is a matter of actions and reactions. And it is all graphs of graphs, the chain of evidence follows through."**

| Claim Type | Backing | Certainty |
|------------|---------|-----------|
| Fact | Evidence | Can be high, up to certain |
| Hypothesis | Partial evidence | An estimate, with a stated level |
| Opinion | Little evidence | The weakest, still labelled |

The detectability point (how likely abuse is to be discovered) is the toxicity-of-access theme flagged in the naming brief: the same exposure can be loud or quiet, and that shifts the certainty (cross-ref: the naming brief's detectability topic).

## The Past Is Evidence

A final, important addition: **the past provides evidence, so you need good data sets.**

The project lead: **"it is important to have a concept of facts and the past, because the past also provides evidence. There is no point saying things will happen if no such event has happened, say, a year ago. Which is why we need good data sets, because without them we do not know what is happening within the industry."** So evidence is not only the present configuration; it is the historical record, and the quality of the data sets determines how well the past can inform the risk (cross-ref: the confidence-through-evidence thesis).

## What This Asks For

1. **Translate into the recipient's language** (their role, culture, domain).
2. **Map the second, third, and fourth-order side effects** (business, consumer, regulation).
3. **Carry the graph attributes** (org chart, roles of agents and humans, interests, the meta-play).
4. **Let business people reason forward** (give them the starting point).
5. **Be fact-driven** (facts on evidence, then hypotheses and opinions, each with a certainty level).
6. **Include the past** (the historical record as evidence; good data sets).

Estimated effort: this is a primitive and a method; the build is the recipient-language translation, the side-effect mapping, and the fact-and-certainty model, on the existing graph.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not dumbing down.** It translates into the recipient's domain.
- **Not first-order only.** The side effects two or three degrees out matter most.
- **Not assertion without evidence.** Facts are backed; estimates are labelled.
- **Not present-only.** The past is evidence too.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: speaking every recipient's language is work.** Each role needs its own translation. Mitigation: the graph plus LLMs generate role-specific views (cross-ref: the agent-blast-radius-service brief).

**Tension 2: side-effect mapping can be unbounded.** Consequences ripple far. Mitigation: map the side effects that materially change the decision; map the gaps (cross-ref: the confidence-through-evidence thesis).

**Tension 3: certainty estimates can mislead.** A confident-sounding estimate is still an estimate. Mitigation: always show the level of certainty and its evidence.

**Tension 4: good data sets are scarce.** The past is only as good as the records. Mitigation: invest in data sets; treat their absence as a gap, since lack of evidence is evidence.
