# The Terms And Conditions You Already Accepted: Framing The Blast Radius As The SLA

## What This Is

The terms-and-conditions framing for the blast radius: **position what an agent is authorised to do as the SLA and the terms and conditions you accept when you give it access, the ones everybody just clicks accept on without reading, made explicit and deliberately alarming as a massive list of what you are authorising (corrupt the database, delete it, compromise customers, destroy the company, break reputation, lose PCI compliance), which both predicts the future, since agentic companies will need explicit terms of this kind, and gives a powerful way to position and explain the service by visualising the contract you are about to sign; a vivid, current example is the YOLO culture of running agents on the command line, a highly radioactive action dressed up as fun, which normalizes the very danger the framing exposes.** It picks up the SLA idea flagged earlier and connects to risk acceptance next (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 rising-tide brief, the agent-authorisation-blast-radius brief, and the second-third-order-side-effects brief). New contributions: **the terms-and-conditions positioning, the deliberately-alarming list, and the YOLO-CLI example.**

## The Series Framing

What this picks up: **the SLA and terms-and-conditions idea, as its own memo.**

The project lead: **"I want to pick up the idea of connecting with the terms and conditions, those things everybody just clicks continue on, playing to the fact that nobody reads them and nobody pays attention to what they are signing, which today are a kind of legal contract."** So this turns the throwaway click-through into the centre of the positioning.

## The Terms And Conditions Nobody Reads

The familiar behaviour to exploit: **everybody clicks accept without reading.**

The everyday terms and conditions are a legal contract that almost nobody reads; people click continue and move on. The framing leans on exactly that habit: when you give an agent a token or access, you are clicking accept on something far larger than a normal terms-and-conditions box, and the value is in making that visible.

## Making The Implicit Explicit, And Alarming

The core move: **show the SLA you are accepting, and make it scary.**

The project lead: **"we say, this is the SLA, these are the terms and conditions you are accepting right now, and we make them explicit. It should be alarming. Imagine a massive list: you allow a corrupted database, deleting the database, corrupting information, compromising your customers, destroying the company, destroying customer value, breaking reputation, losing PCI compliance. Map all those possible things, because this is the stuff we already have on the other side that we are connecting."** So the framing renders the authorised blast radius as an explicit, deliberately confronting list of consequences, drawn from the mapping the service already produces (cross-ref: the second-third-order-side-effects brief).

## Predicting The Future

Why this is where things are heading: **agentic companies will need explicit terms like this.**

The project lead: **"this is predicting the future, because a lot of agentic companies will need to provide these explicit terms: you are giving us permission to do this, so do not complain if our agent blows up your database, because you gave us that permission. Do not complain if something gets destroyed, or it does not work, or the agent hallucinates, because you gave it the permission."** So the framing is not just positioning; it anticipates a real shift in how agentic services will have to set expectations and allocate responsibility.

## A Way To Position And Explain The Service

Why it is useful beyond marketing: **it is how you explain what we do.**

The project lead: **"this is a great way to position it, and even to explain to people what we do, because you are showing, this is what you are already agreeing to, this is the SLA, this is the contract you have signed or are about to sign. How it gets implemented, even visually, matters."** So the terms-and-conditions device doubles as the clearest explanation of the service: the product is making the contract you are signing legible (cross-ref: the agent-authorisation-blast-radius brief).

## The YOLO Command-Line Example

A vivid, current illustration of the danger being dressed up as fun: **running agents in YOLO mode on the command line.**

A sharp example of an unread, enormous set of terms is the culture of running agents on the command line in YOLO mode, auto-approving everything and skipping the permission prompts, so the agent executes commands over your machine and environment without confirmation. By the framing of this series, that is a highly radioactive action: it hands an agent the full authorised blast radius with no checkpoint. Yet culturally it is dressed up as fun and bold, with YOLO often celebrated as the good, fast, confident thing to do. That cultural packaging is precisely the problem the terms-and-conditions framing exposes: the fun normalizes the danger and hides the implicit SLA, which in plain terms reads as you accept that the agent may run anything, change anything, and destroy anything, with no confirmation. Making that contract explicit is the point (cross-ref: the why-now-inefficiency-of-exploitation brief, on benign agents and over-privilege).

## Connects To Risk Acceptance

Where it leads next: **once the SLA is explicit, someone has to accept it.**

The project lead flagged that this connects to the next memo, risk acceptance: once the terms and conditions are made explicit and alarming, the question becomes who accepts them, which is the risk-acceptance primitive from the first brief (cross-ref: the agent-authorisation-blast-radius risk-acceptance primitive).

## What This Asks For

1. **Frame the blast radius as the SLA** (the terms and conditions you accept).
2. **Make the implicit explicit** (the contract behind the click).
3. **Make the list deliberately alarming** (the real consequences, evidence-backed).
4. **Use it to position and explain the service** (visualise the contract).
5. **Use the YOLO-CLI example** (radioactive action dressed up as fun).
6. **Lead into risk acceptance** (who accepts these terms).

Estimated effort: this is a positioning and presentation device; the substance is the existing blast-radius mapping, rendered as terms and conditions.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not the boilerplate nobody reads.** It makes the contract legible.
- **Not fear for its own sake.** The alarming list is evidence-backed.
- **Not legal advice.** It is a positioning device; the contracts are real, but this is not legal counsel.
- **Not an endorsement of YOLO mode.** It uses it as a cautionary example.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: alarming must stay accurate.** A scary list that overstates loses credibility. Mitigation: every item is backed by the mapped evidence (cross-ref: the second-third-order-side-effects brief).

**Tension 2: positioning versus legal advice.** This frames real legal contracts. Mitigation: present it as positioning and awareness, not legal counsel.

**Tension 3: YOLO culture is entrenched.** Calling it radioactive may read as scolding. Mitigation: use it as awareness, showing the hidden terms, not lecturing.

**Tension 4: scary versus credible.** Too alarming and people dismiss it. Mitigation: explicit, evidenced, and specific beats vague dread.
