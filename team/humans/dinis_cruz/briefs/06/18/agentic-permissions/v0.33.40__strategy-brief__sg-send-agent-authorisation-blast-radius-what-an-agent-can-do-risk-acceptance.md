## What This Is

The first of a series on a Vault-technology implementation, value proposition, and eventual business, focused on the blast radius: **instead of telling a company or person what their agent did, show them what their agent is authorised to do, the entire universe of what could happen with the permission set it has been given, which is the blast radius of the agent's authority, built on two primitives, a graph-based, hyperlinked, evidence-focused map of what the agent can do (mapping reality), and risk acceptance (getting the business and the asset owners to accept the mapped risk, including the uncomfortable case where someone is responsible for risks another party controls), on the premise that the moment of authorisation is the moment you grant the permission, not the moment the agent acts.** It applies yesterday's evidence thesis to agent authorisation and builds on the identity and risk work (cross-ref: the v0.33.38 confidence-through-evidence thesis, the v0.32.3 nhi-2.0 briefs, and the v0.33.27 agentic-compromise-cleanup brief). It is the complement to SG/Hermes, which contains the blast radius by design while this maps and accepts it (cross-ref: the v0.33.38 hermes-fully-ephemeral-vault-native-agent brief). New contributions: **the can-do-versus-did reframe, the authorisation-blast-radius map, and the risk-acceptance primitive.**

The project lead: **"this is the first of a number of memos about an implementation of the Vault technology around a very specific use case, value proposition, and eventually a business. The logic is to focus on the blast radius we talked about."** So this brief frames the value proposition; further memos will follow (including one on how this problem has always existed in software, and more on the permissions).

## The Shift: From What An Agent Did To What It Can Do

The core reframe: **show what the agent is authorised to do, not what it did.**

The project lead: **"instead of telling a company here is what your agent did, the logic is to say here is what your agent is authorised to do, here is what the agent can do. It is almost like simulating the blast radius to say here is the universe of everything that could happen with the current permission sets you have given the agent."** This is the headline: most tools report what happened, after the fact; this maps what is possible, in advance, which is the true risk surface regardless of whether the agent ever exercises it.

## Why Agents Make It Urgent

The timing: **the problem always existed in software, but agents pile a new layer onto unprepared infrastructure.**

The project lead: **"although this problem has always existed in software, and I will do a memo specific on this, the reason it is problematic now is that agents introduce a whole layer on top of an infrastructure that is completely not prepared for it."** So over-permissioning is an old problem, but agents act autonomously and at scale on top of systems that were never designed for them, which makes the authorised blast radius suddenly critical.

## The Access-Pass Analogy

How to picture it: **the agent's access pass is a backstage pass that does far more than you think.**

The project lead: **"the analogy is an access pass, like a building's access control. Or a concert backstage pass: it does not just let you see the concert, it lets you go backstage, pretend to be the artist, replace the staff, access the accounts, access everything related to the event, even destroy the event or cause damage to everyone involved. If you add up all the permissions agents can do, it is insane."** The point of the analogy is the gap between what a permission appears to grant and the full universe it actually unlocks when combined with everything else.

## The Moment Of Authorisation

The premise underneath it: **authorisation happens when you grant the permission, not when the agent acts.**

The project lead: **"the moment of authorisation is not when you allow the agent to do something, it is the moment you give them the permissions. That is the moment of authorisation."** So the decision that matters, and the one this service surfaces, is the grant, because from that moment the entire authorised universe is live whether or not it is ever used (cross-ref: the nhi-2.0 access-not-security brief and the hope-driven-development concept).

## The Two Primitives

The service rests on two pillars: **a map of what the agent can do, and acceptance of that risk.**

### Primitive One: The Graph-Based Evidence Map Of What The Agent Can Do

The project lead: **"the first primitive is a graph-based, hyperlinked, evidence-focused map, the same as the memos on understanding through connectivity and confidence through evidence. What we are showing is the universe of what is possible right now with this particular agent and this particular permission set. Just mapping that is massive, technically, but also from a business, regulation, compliance, risk, side-effects, and human point of view. It is about mapping reality."** So the first primitive is the evidence graph from yesterday's thesis, applied to authorisation: the connected, followable map of everything the permission set makes possible (cross-ref: the confidence-through-evidence thesis).

### Primitive Two: Risk Acceptance

The project lead: **"the second part is mapping risk acceptance. You connect to the map, then get the business and the owners of those assets to accept the risk. This gets interesting because sometimes you cross-link risks, where somebody is responsible for risks that somebody else actually controls, and they have no control over the execution of those risks, which is always uncomfortable."** So the second primitive turns the map into a decision: named owners accept the mapped risk, and the process surfaces the uncomfortable but important cases where responsibility and control are split across parties.

## The Value Proposition

The service, plainly: **give visibility into what an agent can do with the permissions it has been given.**

The project lead: **"the value is giving visibility into what an agent can do with the permissions it has been given. It is like saying, you already gave your agent permission to delete the database, regardless of whether it does it or not. That is valuable today for the companies developing agents, the projects doing agents, and the clients of those agents, who need to know how many permissions they actually gave it and what the agents they use are actually capable of. This changes when customers start to demand different things."**

## The Relationship To SG/Hermes

Worth stating, because they are complements: **SG/Hermes contains the blast radius; this maps and accepts it.**

SG/Hermes reduces the blast radius by design (ephemeral compute, NHI 2.0 least privilege, PKI, no persistent attack surface). This service addresses agents as they already exist, often badly over-permissioned, by mapping their authorised blast radius and getting owners to accept it. One prevents, the other reveals and governs, and together they cover both the agents you build well and the agents already in the wild (cross-ref: the hermes-fully-ephemeral-vault-native-agent brief).

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not after-the-fact reporting.** It shows what can happen, not what did.
- **Not a prediction of behaviour.** It maps the authorised universe, not the likely actions.
- **Not containment.** That is SG/Hermes; this maps and accepts.
- **Not the whole series.** This is the first framing memo.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: can-do versus will-do.** The map shows what is possible, not what is probable, and some will say the probable matters more. Mitigation: the authorised universe is the true risk surface precisely because a single compromise or error can exercise any of it; possibility is the right unit for risk.

**Tension 2: mapping the full authorised universe is hard.** Permissions compose, so the real blast radius is larger than any single grant suggests. Mitigation: the graph-based, hyperlinked map is built to capture composition; map the gaps too (cross-ref: the confidence-through-evidence brief).

**Tension 3: cross-linked risk is uncomfortable.** Owners may resist accepting risks they do not control. Mitigation: surfacing this is the value, not a flaw; it is exactly what needs to be made explicit.

**Tension 4: risk acceptance needs the right owners.** Acceptance is meaningless without authority. Mitigation: map the asset owners as part of the graph.
