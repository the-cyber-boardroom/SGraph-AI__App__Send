# The Rising Tide: The Better Everyone Gets, The Better We Get

**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Publication, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (in the series)

## What This Is

The rising-tide, or judo, strategy: **build the whole stack, which is why open source is critical, so that the better everything else becomes, the models, the agents, the platforms, and even the competitors, the better this platform becomes; the biggest challenge is not competition but that customers are not yet asking the right question, the visibility on the blast radius, what am I authorising and what am I signing when I give an agent credentials; the need will not go away as agents proliferate, the same way the need for identity will not go away, because every AI app must map this out; so you ride the rising tide as the market matures, for example a permissions bill of materials like an SBOM, growing from a small pond to a bigger one; open source is the mechanism, because competitors can copy the code but not the users and adoption, while the hyperscalers, whom you want to embrace it, open the market; and the core principle is to build a model where the better your competitors are, the better you become.** It extends the open-source and go-to-market work (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 open-source-strategy and go-to-market briefs, the v0.33.40 agent-authorisation-blast-radius brief, and the v0.32.3 nhi-2.0 briefs). New contributions: **the judo principle, the challenge-is-the-question point, the need-will-not-go-away argument, and two flagged memos.**

## The Series Framing

What this covers: **a principle for how the whole ecosystem should work.**

The project lead: **"a very important principle for the workflows this startup should have is that the whole solution, the whole ecosystem, which is why open source is critical, should be built so that the better everything else becomes, the better the platform becomes."** So this is the competitive-posture pillar of the series.

## The Judo Move

The core principle: **the better the models, agents, platforms, and competitors get, the better this platform gets.**

The project lead: **"the tech stack is built around the workflow so that the better the models become, the better the agentic solutions and platforms become, and the better the direct competitors become, when they exist or when existing companies pivot in, the better this platform should be."** So the posture is judo: you turn the strength and progress of the whole field into your own advantage rather than fighting it.

## The Challenge Is The Question, Not Competition

The real obstacle: **customers are not yet asking the right question.**

The project lead: **"the biggest challenge is not that there is competition or strong competitors. It is that the potential customers are not asking the right question, not looking to buy the key solution we provide: visibility on the blast radius, on what am I authorising right now, and what is the pre-authorisation that already happens when you deliver an authorisation to an agent, when you give it credentials. What am I signing?"** So competitors who educate the market are allies, because the bottleneck is awareness, not rivalry (cross-ref: the go-to-market brief).

## The SLA And Terms-And-Conditions Framing

A positioning lens, flagged for its own memo: **what you sign when you give an agent access is a frightening SLA.**

The project lead: **"what we are really talking about is the service level agreement, the terms and conditions people accept. That is a great way to position it: the terms and conditions and the SLA, the things you accept when you give a token or access, are way bigger, more complicated, and more dangerous than people realise. They should be scary to read. That is good to visualise, and I will do a separate memo on it."** So one way to make the question vivid is to render the implicit SLA of granting an agent access, and show how alarming it really is (a flagged future memo; cross-ref: the naming-the-agent-authorisation-bundle brief).

## The Need Will Not Go Away

Why the tailwind is durable: **the need persists like the need for identity.**

The project lead: **"this problem will not go away, because there is an explosion of agents, systems, and every app that calls itself AI needs to map this out. Even as the models get better and things get more secure, this need will not go away, the same way the need for identity will not go away."** So improvement does not remove the need; proliferation deepens it, which is what makes the rising tide reliable (cross-ref: the why-now-inefficiency and nhi-2.0 briefs).

## The Rising Tide

How growth compounds: **let the market grow, and grow with it.**

The project lead: **"you want to do a judo move: the better they become, the better the core workflow. Once there is the equivalent of an SBOM, or a permissions bill of materials, and that matures, they grow the market, and we grow with it. Let the tide rise. We are currently playing in a very small pond, so we want more water in the pond, and the better they become, the better the whole thing works."** So the strategy is to be positioned to benefit from market maturation no matter who drives it, expanding the pond rather than fighting for the current one.

## The Permissions Bill Of Materials

A maturing-market artifact, flagged for its own topic: **a permissions bill of materials, like an SBOM.**

The project lead flagged the permissions bill of materials as a separate topic: as the software bill of materials matured into a standard artifact, a permissions bill of materials could do the same for agent authorisation, and its emergence would grow the market this platform serves (cross-ref: the skills-as-code units-of-execution and key-gated supply-chain work).

## Open Source Is The Mechanism

How the judo works in practice: **competitors copy the code, but not the users and adoption.**

The project lead: **"open source is how you do this. Anyone competing closed-source can copy and re-implement and leverage the LLM capabilities, but they will not have the users and the adoption. The hyperscalers we leverage, and you want the hyperscalers to adopt and embrace these technologies, because they open up the market. So we want them involved."** So open source converts the field's progress into your advantage: code is copyable, adoption is not, and the hyperscalers' embrace expands the market rather than threatening it (cross-ref: the open-source-strategy and commercial-model briefs).

## The Core Principle

In one line: **build a model where the better your competitors are, the better you become.**

The project lead: **"you want to build a model where the better your competitors are, the better you become, and open source is the key element of that strategy."** So the whole posture reduces to anti-fragility to competition, with open source as the mechanism that makes it real.

## What This Asks For

1. **Build for the rising tide** (the better everything gets, the better the platform).
2. **Treat awareness as the obstacle** (the question, not the competition).
3. **Render the implicit SLA** (the scary terms of granting access; a flagged memo).
4. **Bet on the durable need** (it persists like identity).
5. **Welcome market-maturing artifacts** (the permissions bill of materials; a flagged topic).
6. **Use open source as the mechanism** (copyable code, uncopyable adoption).
7. **Want the hyperscalers in** (they open the market).

Estimated effort: this is a posture and a principle; it shapes how every other part of the strategy is built.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not anti-competition.** Better competitors make this better.
- **Not dependent on secrecy.** Open source is the mechanism.
- **Not threatened by hyperscalers.** Their embrace opens the market.
- **Not a fight for the current pond.** It grows the pond.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the tide must actually rise.** Riding it assumes the need becomes demand. Mitigation: the awareness work and the SLA framing turn the latent need into a question customers ask (cross-ref: the go-to-market brief).

**Tension 2: hyperscalers could commoditise it.** Their embrace opens the market but could also absorb it. Mitigation: adoption and being the creator and maintainer of the core is the edge, not the code (cross-ref: the open-source-strategy brief).

**Tension 3: small pond now.** The current market is small. Mitigation: the strategy is explicitly to grow the pond, and the need is durable.

**Tension 4: open source is copyable.** Competitors can re-implement. Mitigation: they cannot copy the users and adoption, which is the moat.
