# The Open Source Strategy: Everything Open, The Line At The Customer

**version** v0.33.40
**date** 18 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Publication, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (in the series)

## What This Is

The open source strategy for the project, and for the company: **everything the company does, code, logic, functionality, and schemas, is open source, with no proprietary core and no bait model where the best features are locked away, and the only line is at the customer boundary, where customer customisations, customer data, customer schemas, and privacy or commercial-agreement requirements may be closed because that is the customer's data and decision, while the commercial hook is maintaining the customer's custom stack, hosting deployments more cheaply than self-hosting, and brokering data sets; the reasons are that it simplifies everything, improves security, prevents the company from the temptation of not putting the customer first since technology is no longer the moat, and forces competitors to compete open; you compete on features and real value, never rent extraction, and the value is in adoption, in being the creator and maintainer of the core technology, ideas, and standards.** It delivers the open-source-strategy memo flagged earlier and extends the sovereignty work (cross-ref: the v0.33.26 sovereignty thesis, the open-source-success-paradox research, and the v0.33.38 guidance-gen-ai value-not-rent brief). New contributions: **the precise open-source line, the customer-boundary commercial hook, and the four reasons.**

## The Series Framing

What this covers: **the open source strategy for the blast-radius project, the third tracked memo.**

The project lead: **"I want to talk about the open source strategy for this project of mapping the blast radius and the permissions. In addition to what I said before about open source being needed for sovereignty and the business models it enables, I am yet to see evidence that open source is a deterrent to the business. All the evidence is that it is only a problem when companies shoot themselves in the foot."** So the premise is empirical: open source has not been shown to hurt the business, only self-inflicted moves have (cross-ref: the open-source-success-paradox research).

## The Line: Everything Is Open Source

The core position: **everything the company does is open, with no proprietary core.**

The project lead: **"my line is that everything the company does, the code, the logic, the functionality, and the schemas, is open source. There should be nothing proprietary. You never have the temptation of the bait model, where some of the best features are locked behind proprietary stuff, because the community always sees through it: the open source looks powerful but is not really delivering the value."** So the line is drawn at everything: no withheld features, no proprietary core, nothing the community has to see through.

## Where The Line Is Drawn: The Customer

The one exception, and the commercial hook: **closed begins at the customer boundary.**

The project lead: **"the moment you have closed repos is the moment you do customisations for the customer, the moment you hit customer data. Ideally it would run on the customer's repos so we never host it, but one model is that we host customisations and mass-customised deployments, because it is cheaper for the customer to pay us than to run it themselves. That is the key logic."** So the only place closed code appears is customer-specific work, and even that is ideally on the customer's infrastructure.

## The Customer Line Is The Natural Place For Proprietary

Why that boundary is the right one: **it is the customer's data and the customer's strategy.**

The project lead: **"that becomes the natural place for proprietary, non-public information, because at that point it is the customer's data and their strategy. If the customer has an open-source strategy, great; if not, great, it is their decision. The technology becomes highly customised, so the customer runs a custom stack, maybe ten percent of the codebase, but the ten percent they care about, which needs to be maintained, which is part of our business model."** So the closed part is not the company's secret; it is the customer's, and maintaining it is the revenue (cross-ref: the vault-powered-websites brief on customer deployments).

## No Lock-In; The Hook Is Value

The premise that makes it honest: **the natural line is a business line, and the hook is value, not lock-in.**

The project lead: **"we do not have that lock-in on technology, and that is fundamental. The natural line is a business line, and that is okay: that is when you charge to maintain it. It is the hook, but a hook based on value, based on the customer having made decisions that we now maintain and enforce. The customer's schemas can be closed, a lot of their data is closed, but our schemas are open."** So the company earns by maintaining the customer's choices, not by trapping them (cross-ref: the value-not-rent guidance brief).

## Data Sets: A Different Commercial Model

A further model: **broker data sets, some public, some closed.**

The project lead: **"some interesting data sets can exist, and this is a different commercial model. Some data sets, not all, can be public, and we become a broker for data sets we create and customers create. Some can be public, some closed, like feeds, and some have privacy issues. The line is privacy requirements and commercial agreements where the customer decides to close; everything else is open."** So beyond maintenance, the company can broker data sets, with the same line: privacy and customer choice decide what is closed.

## The Reasons Why

Why open everything:

| # | Reason |
|---|--------|
| 1 | It simplifies everything: operations, business discussions, workflows, the tech stack, and deployment; no secrets to deploy, no phone-home, no licence and restriction overhead |
| 2 | It improves security in many ways, not just many eyes, but a simpler deployment with fewer secrets |
| 3 | It prevents the temptation of not putting the customer first, because technology is no longer the moat (it can be replicated easily), so what matters is execution and customer focus |
| 4 | It forces competitors to compete open, because if they compete closed they carry all the disadvantages of closed |

The project lead on the third reason: **"technology is not the moat anymore, it can be replicated easily. What matters is execution: are you focused on the customer's needs and delivering what adds value. Open source puts the customer first, because the customer gains more with an open tech stack than a proprietary one: more freedom, independence, control of their own destiny, no lock-in. As long as the company executes well and stays customer-focused, it has the advantage."**

## Compete On Features And Real Value

Where competition belongs: **features, market fit, and value, never rent extraction.**

The project lead: **"you should compete on features, on market fit, on whether you are delivering the value the customer wants. Some customers care about sovereignty, and we care about them, but the way to compete is on the actual service. As soon as you move into rent extraction, you lose sight of it: you are playing the game of making it less expensive to keep you than to leave, and then they leave with pain. I do not want us to fall into that."** So open source keeps the company honest, competing on value rather than on the cost of escape.

## The Value Is In Adoption

Where the upside really is: **adoption, and being the core maintainer.**

The project lead: **"even with zero contributions, being open dramatically simplifies the tech stack, and my own solutions run everywhere because of it. Then a whole set of commercial models come into play, but the value becomes the adoption of the technology, how many people use it. If you are the creator and maintainer of the core technology, the core ideas, the core standards, there is crazy value, and that is where we want to play."** So the strategic prize is adoption and stewardship of the core, which open source maximises (cross-ref: the open-source-success-paradox research).

## The Core

The position, simply: **everything is open source, so you do not even have the conversation.**

The project lead: **"the line is very simple: everything is open source, so you do not even have that conversation. It is a key pillar. It is not for everybody, some investors will not like it, but you find the ones who do, and it is a good match. You can always commercial-license or relicense everything for a customer who wants it, but the core is all open source. No part of the company or the ecosystem is not open source. And I am yet to see evidence that this has a negative commercial or competitive impact."** So the strategy is absolute and simple, with commercial licensing available as a pressure valve, and the burden of proof placed on anyone claiming open source hurts.

## What This Asks For

1. **Open everything the company does** (code, logic, functionality, schemas).
2. **Draw the only line at the customer** (customisations, data, schemas, privacy).
3. **Make maintenance the commercial hook** (the customer's custom stack).
4. **Offer hosting and deployment** (cheaper than self-hosting).
5. **Broker data sets** (some public, some closed, on the privacy line).
6. **Compete on features and value** (never rent extraction).
7. **Play for adoption and stewardship** (the creator and maintainer of the core).

Estimated effort: this is a strategy and a discipline; the cost is foregoing a proprietary moat, and the return is simplicity, security, customer trust, and adoption.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a bait model.** No best features locked behind proprietary walls.
- **Not lock-in.** The hook is value and maintenance, not escape cost.
- **Not closed by default.** Only the customer's own data and choices are closed.
- **Not for every investor.** The right match self-selects.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: some investors and customers want a proprietary moat.** Open everything will put some off. Mitigation: find the matches; offer commercial licensing as a pressure valve.

**Tension 2: the data-set privacy line needs care.** Brokering data sets touches privacy. Mitigation: privacy and commercial agreements define exactly what is closed.

**Tension 3: maintenance and hosting are the revenue.** They must be genuinely cheaper than self-hosting. Mitigation: deliver real cost-effectiveness, not a disguised rent.

**Tension 4: no technology moat.** The technology can be copied. Mitigation: that is the point; compete on execution, customer focus, and adoption.
