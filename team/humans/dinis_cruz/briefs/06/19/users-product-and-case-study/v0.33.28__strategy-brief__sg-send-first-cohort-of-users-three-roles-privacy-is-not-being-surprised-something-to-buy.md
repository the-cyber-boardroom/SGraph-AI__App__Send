# The First Cohort: Three Roles, And Something Each Can Buy

**version** v0.33.28
**date** 19 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Go-To-Market, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (in the series)

## What This Is

The first cohort of users, mapped by role and by what each can buy: **there are three roles, often the same person playing different parts, and you walk backwards from each one's need; the first is the power user running agents and granting permissions, who just wants to use it safely and not over-privilege, captured by the definition that privacy is when you are not surprised; the second is the user adopting an AI-powered third-party service, who does not control the LLM and wants to make a good acquisition decision, asking what terms and conditions am I actually accepting; the third is the companies providing LLM services, a massive market, who want to sell more and keep customers and avoid trust and security incidents, and whose execs and devs need a good way to represent reality, first to fix things internally and later to compete on requiring fewer permissions and having guardrails; and the commercial test across all three is whether each has something to buy, free to start with a paying element that leads to the billable units, and an easy way to connect with us.** It answers the beachhead question in Explorer-appropriate form (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 go-to-market and commercial-model briefs, the v0.33.28 need-to-focus brief, and the terms-and-conditions brief). New contributions: **the three concrete roles, the privacy-is-not-being-surprised definition, and the something-to-buy test.**

## The Framing

What this maps: **the first cohort, their needs, and what we do for each.**

The project lead: **"let's map our first cohort of users, the target audience, their questions, concerns, and challenges, and what we can do for them. There are several levels: the overall need, which you walk backwards from. The same individual might play different roles."** So the cohort is defined by roles and needs, not by demographics.

## Role One: The User Running Agents And Granting Permissions

The heavy adopter: **wants to use it safely and not over-privilege.**

The project lead: **"the first is the user who is using an agent today and giving permissions: connecting a lot of MCP servers and connectors, using lots of agents with a lot of privileges. That user just wants to use it safely. I like the definition that privacy is when you are not surprised. So they want the agents, the LLMs, the workflows, not to do things that surprise them, deleting a database, corrupting data, things they were not aware of. They want to use it safely, not over-privilege, and make good decisions."** So the first role is the privilege-granting power user, and the promise to them is no surprises: visibility and good decisions before anything goes wrong.

## Role Two: The User Adopting An AI-Powered Service

The buyer of someone else's AI: **wants a good acquisition decision.**

The project lead: **"the second is the same user when they use an AI-powered service. It is different because in the first case the user has some control over the LLM and what is privileged; here they are using a third-party service that uses an LLM, that has the data and requires access and asks for certain permissions. At that moment they still want to use it safely, but they also want to make a good decision on that acquisition: what exactly are the terms and conditions I am actually accepting here?"** So the same person, in a different mode, needs to evaluate a service they do not control, and the promise is a clear answer to what they are signing up for (cross-ref: the terms-and-conditions brief).

## Role Three: The Companies Providing LLM Services

The vendors, a massive market: **want to sell more and keep customers.**

The project lead: **"the third is the companies providing LLM services, which I think is a massive market. They want to sell more, have happy customers, and not lose customers to trust issues or security incidents, and to gain customers through trust and confidence in the platform. Their execs and even their devs need a good way to represent reality: here is what is going on today, here is the situation, so they can do something about it. A lot of them will not initially share that yes, I can blow up your entire database, but they will use the same information internally to make changes, and eventually you want companies competing on being more secure than their competitors: we do not require all these permissions, we only require this, we have these guardrails."** So the vendor's need is to see and act on their own reality, first privately, then as a competitive advantage, which is the rising-tide dynamic in microcosm (cross-ref: the rising-tide brief).

## The Commercial Test

What makes this a cohort and not a list: **each role has something to buy.**

The project lead: **"the important thing is, when you look at the website, the first set of services, the first things we can charge for, do we have something each of these can buy? They can start using the service for free, but there is a paying element they go back to, and that takes us to the billable units. And do we have an easy way for them to connect with us? The more they use it, the more this grows, and then it is about learning the tooling, the workflows, the touchpoints."** So each role must have a free entry and a paid path that maps to the billable units, with a frictionless way to connect (cross-ref: the commercial-model and go-to-market briefs).

| Role | Core need | Promise | Something to buy |
|------|-----------|---------|------------------|
| Power user granting permissions | Use it safely, no surprises | Visibility and good decisions before harm | Free assessment, paid deeper analysis and monitoring |
| Adopter of an AI service | A good acquisition decision | A clear read on what they are accepting | Free check, paid per-service or ongoing assessment |
| LLM-service vendor | Sell more, keep customers | Represent reality, then compete on it | Internal analysis, paid posture and proof tooling |

## What This Asks For

1. **Define the cohort by role and need** (walk backwards from the need).
2. **Serve the power user** (safety, no surprises, no over-privilege).
3. **Serve the service adopter** (a good acquisition decision).
4. **Serve the vendor** (represent reality, internal then competitive).
5. **Give each something to buy** (free entry, paid path to billable units).
6. **Make connecting easy** (low-friction onboarding).

Estimated effort: this is targeting and packaging on top of the existing service; the work is matching each role to a free entry and a paid path.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not demographic targeting.** It is role and need based.
- **Not three separate products.** One service, three entry points.
- **Not adversarial to vendors.** They are a buyer, internal first.
- **Not free forever.** Free entry leads to the billable units.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the roles overlap.** The same person plays several. Mitigation: that is a feature; one service serves them as they switch roles.

**Tension 2: vendors will not admit the risk publicly.** Internal use comes first. Mitigation: sell the internal-reality value now; the competitive claim follows.

**Tension 3: free-to-paid conversion.** Free entry must lead somewhere. Mitigation: tie the paid path to the billable units from the start.

**Tension 4: relationship to the three audiences.** The go-to-market brief named users, buyers, and sellers. Mitigation: these three roles are a sharper cut of the same idea, with two user modes and the vendor.
