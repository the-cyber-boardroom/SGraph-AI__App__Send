## What This Is

The compliance and standards intersection, as a variation of the SG/Sentinel compliance-as-living-graph: **map how the agent-authorisation blast radius intersects the major standards, NIST, GDPR, ISO 27001, HIPAA and healthcare, privacy and governance, and the fast-moving EU AI Act, NIS2, and DORA, by building a relevant subset graph of just the controls that apply, almost a small standard of its own, then read it from several angles: the binary one, where if you cannot answer the blast-radius questions you already cannot demonstrate compliance, with hard breaches and softer ones; the boxes you can check once you know, because compliance is partly about understanding; the new breaches that appear once you know the side effects, because knowing and not acting is itself a failing; and the controls you recover as you reduce privileges; it extends to third-party governance and due diligence, where items get worse or better once known, and it stresses that much of this is breached before the agent does anything, at the moment of authorisation, and that the blast radius can breach other parties' compliance, especially across supply chains; risk drives it, since this agent access loses you that control, backed by evidence that exists because the access was granted.** It delivers the long-queued standards-and-regulations brief (cross-ref: the frozen `sg-sentinel/v0.27.58__arch-brief__sg-sentinel-compliance-as-living-graph.md`, and the v0.33.28 PBOM-adjacent-standards research). Sources are paraphrased and listed. New contributions: **the relevant-subset-graph, the four compliance angles, and the breached-before-the-agent-acts argument.**

## The Series Framing

What this builds on: **the SG/Sentinel mappings, applied to agent permissions.**

The project lead: **"go back to SG/Sentinel, where we mapped multiple standards. There are a lot of great mappings there, and this is a variation of that. Do a research on how what we do impacts standards like NIST, GDPR, ISO 27000, healthcare standards, privacy, and governance standards, and how it maps."** So this is the compliance lens on the agent-authorisation work, reusing the living-graph approach.

## The Relevant Subset Graph

The core method: **extract only the controls that apply, and connect them.**

The project lead: **"create a subset, a graph, of those standards that has just the bits, the controls, and the elements relevant here. When we talk about GDPR, which controls and parts are relevant? When we talk about NIST, which controls? How does everything connect? You almost recreate a small standard based on this."** So rather than reproduce whole frameworks, the work is a focused graph of the access, least-privilege, data-minimisation, supply-chain, and accountability controls that the blast radius touches, linked to the agent permissions they depend on (cross-ref: the graphs-of-graphs brief).

## The Standards That Map

The relevant controls, by standard (an engineering and governance mapping, not legal advice):

| Standard | Relevant controls (the subset) |
|----------|-------------------------------|
| GDPR | Data minimisation (Art 5), data protection by design and default (Art 25), records of processing (Art 30), security of processing (Art 32), DPIA (Art 35), processor duties (Art 28) |
| NIST | CSF 2.0 Govern, Identify, Protect; 800-53 access control family, least privilege (AC-6); AI RMF and the AI Agent Standards Initiative |
| ISO 27001:2022 | Annex A access control, privileged access management, information access restriction, least privilege |
| HIPAA | Security Rule access control, the minimum-necessary standard, audit controls, business-associate duties |
| EU AI Act | Deployer obligations for AI systems; high-risk duties on documentation, human oversight, and traceability (high-risk now deferred to late 2027 and 2028); concurrent with GDPR |
| NIS2 | Access control, encryption, supply-chain security, incident reporting, board-level accountability |
| DORA | ICT risk management, oversight of critical third-party providers, operational resilience, incident reporting |
| Agentic layer | OWASP Agentic Top 10 (identity and privilege abuse), OWASP NHI Top 10, MITRE ATLAS, CISA and Five Eyes guidance |

The throughline across all of them is the same small set of ideas: least privilege, data minimisation, access control, supply-chain and third-party risk, traceable evidence, and accountability, which is exactly what the blast radius makes visible.

## Angle One: The Binary Breach

The sharpest angle: **if you cannot answer the blast-radius questions, you cannot demonstrate compliance.**

The project lead: **"the first angle is almost binary: if you cannot answer these questions, you are already in breach. You have the hard ones, and then the soft ones where in principle yes."** The defensible form of this is about demonstrability: you cannot evidence least privilege, data minimisation, or access control if you do not know what an agent can reach. Some failings are hard (you cannot show who can access regulated data), and some are softer (you cannot fully evidence proportionality), but the inability to answer is itself the gap the standards care about.

## Angle Two: The Boxes You Can Check Once You Know

The positive mirror: **knowing the blast radius lets you evidence the controls.**

The project lead: **"the second one is, once you know, which boxes do you check, because a lot of compliance is also about understanding."** So the same map that exposes the gap also produces the evidence: once you can show the blast radius and the access, you can demonstrate the access-control, minimisation, and oversight controls you previously could only assert.

## Angle Three: The New Breaches Once You Know The Side Effects

The uncomfortable angle: **knowing and not acting is its own failing.**

The project lead: **"once you know the extent of the blast radius and the side effects, there is a whole number of other items you are now in breach of, because now you know."** So discovery shifts the picture: items that were merely unknown become actionable, and leaving a known excessive access in place is a different and worse posture than not having known, which connects to the risk-acceptance accountability that the click creates (cross-ref: the risk-acceptance and side-effects briefs).

## Angle Four: Compliance Recovered As You Reduce Privileges

The remediation angle: **narrowing access turns breaches back into compliance.**

The project lead: **"as you reduce the privileges, which ones do you now become compliant with, or which ones are no longer an issue?"** So the map is dynamic: each privilege removed or narrowed moves specific controls from failing to satisfied, and that improvement is exactly what should raise the organisation's risk and compliance score (cross-ref: the risk-acceptance hook).

## Third-Party Governance And Due Diligence

The same logic, outward: **which due-diligence items get worse or better once known.**

The project lead: **"this is also important for third-party governance. In due diligence reports, which items get worse or better once you know what is happening? You map the situations, then fix and remove the privileges."** So the compliance graph applies to vendor due diligence and third-party oversight, which NIS2 and DORA make mandatory, and crucially the blast radius can breach other parties' compliance: **"you could be breaking a lot of stuff on their side that they are not prepared to accept, or their own compliance requirements, especially on supply chains."** An agent's access can put a supplier or customer out of compliance with obligations they never agreed to take on.

## Breached Before The Agent Acts

The principle that ties it together: **the failing exists at the grant, not at the incident.**

The project lead: **"remember that a lot of this is before the agent did anything. There are a lot of standards and controls that are broken just by having this access."** So the compliance position degrades the moment an over-broad permission is provisioned, consistent with the series' moment-of-authorisation idea, and the standards that demand least privilege and minimisation are failed by the grant itself, not only by a later event (cross-ref: the agent-authorisation-blast-radius brief).

## Risk Drives Compliance

The framing for the product: **say which compliance an access costs you, with the evidence.**

The project lead: **"risk should be driving this. If you say, with this particular agent access you are going to lose this compliance, that has tonnes of implications, and you need the evidence, and the evidence came up because you gave an agent access to this."** So the compliance mode is risk-driven and evidence-backed: it names the specific control an access endangers and shows why, from the blast-radius graph.

## Relationship To SG/Sentinel

This is the compliance-as-living-graph applied to agent permissions: the same idea that compliance is a live graph wired to reality, now driven by the blast radius and the risk-acceptance flow, so the compliance posture updates as permissions change (cross-ref: the frozen SG/Sentinel anchor).

## Honest Notes

- **Not legal advice.** This is an engineering and governance mapping; breach determinations are legal judgements for qualified counsel.
- **The binary-breach claim needs care.** Frame it as inability to demonstrate a control, not as an automatic legal verdict.
- **The regulatory layer moves fast.** The EU AI Act high-risk deadlines were deferred in 2026; NIS2 and DORA are in force with evolving technical standards.
- **The mapping is interpretive.** The relevant subset is a judgement to validate with compliance teams.
