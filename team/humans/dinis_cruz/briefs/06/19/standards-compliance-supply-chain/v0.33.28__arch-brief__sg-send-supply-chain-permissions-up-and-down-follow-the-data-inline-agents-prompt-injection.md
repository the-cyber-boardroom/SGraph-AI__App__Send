# Supply Chain: Agent Permissions Up And Down The Chain, And The Inline Contamination Problem

**version** v0.33.28
**date** 19 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Architect, Security, Compliance, Strategy, @Dev
**type** Arch brief (in the series)

## What This Is

The supply-chain implications of the agent-authorisation work: **the agent and permission information must flow up and down the supply chain, because companies already run agentic workflows they are unaware of, provided by a partner, so every company that adds an AI agent must make its customers, their customers, its regulators, and its environment aware and get them to accept it as part of the product's terms and conditions, the way you catalogue the software you use you now catalogue agent access; this needs a hyperlinked graph that follows the data the way a DPIA does and then asks, for each piece, whether it is accessed, modified, or decided on by an agent; it is the whole chain, not one unit, and it forces a much better understanding of the supply chain, which is positive; the risks must be distinguished, inline versus out-of-bounds, how much access, and what transformation, because inline agents bring the prompt-injection problem, and in a shared-data chain a payload placed in one piece can impact a completely different one; and the vaults help by propagating and distributing public and private keys to every member, so permissions are tracked and risks accepted at every level, with a vault carrying the risk-factorisation-matrix decisions.** It connects the series to supply-chain governance (cross-ref: the v0.33.28 compliance-intersection brief, the v0.33.40 PBOM and risk-acceptance briefs, and the key-gated supply-chain and PKI work). New contributions: **the up-and-down propagation, the follow-the-data-then-check-for-agents graph, the inline-versus-out-of-bounds risk axis, and the vault key distribution.**

## The Series Framing

What this is: **the information has to move along the chain.**

The project lead: **"the next one is supply-chain management, something we talked about in vaults. It is the idea that this information needs to go up and down the supply chain."** So the blast-radius and permission picture is not a single-company artefact; it has to travel.

## Companies Already Run Agents Unaware

The urgent reality: **partner-provided agents are already in workflows.**

The project lead: **"you now have situations where a lot of companies are already using agents and already have agentic workflows in their normal workflows, and they are just not aware of it, because it is provided by a partner. Every company that adds an AI agent to their product, and everybody is doing that, needs to make sure their customers are aware."** So organisations may already be exposed to agent access they never evaluated, because it arrived inside a supplier's product.

## Make The Whole Chain Aware And Accepting

The obligation: **inform and get acceptance, up and down.**

The project lead: **"wherever they are in the supply chain, make sure their customers, their customers' customers, their regulators, and their environment are all aware that this is happening, and accept it, as part of the terms and conditions of the product and service. It is the same way you catalogue the software you use; now you need to catalogue this."** So agent access becomes a declared, accepted part of the product, catalogued the way software is, and the awareness and acceptance propagate to every affected party (cross-ref: the terms-and-conditions and risk-acceptance briefs, and the PBOM as the catalogue).

## Follow The Data, Then Check For Agents

The method: **a DPIA-style data flow, extended to agents.**

The project lead: **"we need a hyperlinked graph that connects the use of the data. It is like what we did with the DPIA, where you follow the data: where is it, where is it stored, how does it flow? Now do the same, but then see whether any of that data is impacted by an agent, accessed by an agent, can be modified by an agent, or whether decisions are made by agents on top of it."** So the supply-chain graph starts from the familiar data-flow map and adds an agent layer at every node: accessed, modified, or decided upon (cross-ref: the graphs-of-graphs brief).

## Not One Unit, The Whole Chain

The scope: **every provider is a link in the chain.**

The project lead: **"it is not just one unit. The company, the company that provides services to it, and the wrapping around it, everybody up and down the supply chain needs to understand what is happening with that data and accept it. A lot of the pieces of the puzzle come together in supply chain, because ultimately every SaaS provider is a member of a supply chain. This will force a much better understanding of how everything fits together, which is positive."** So the work both spans the chain and improves it, turning an opaque supply chain into a mapped one.

## Inline Versus Out-Of-Bounds

The key risk distinction: **where the agent sits changes everything.**

The project lead: **"it is important to distinguish the risks: is the agent inline or out-of-bounds? How much access does it have? What is the actual transformation it is making? The inline agents are very dangerous."** So the assessment must classify each agent by position (inline in the data path, or out-of-bounds observing it), by access, and by the transformation it performs, because an inline agent that alters data in flight is a categorically larger risk (cross-ref: the integrity-over-confidentiality thesis).

## The Prompt-Injection Contamination Problem

Why inline is so dangerous: **a payload in one link reaches another.**

The project lead: **"with inline agents you have the prompt-injection problem. How much of your supply chain is now susceptible to prompt injection? Can you put a payload in one piece of the supply chain that impacts other parts? Especially when multiple people access or contribute to the same data, you can literally have a payload in one piece of the puzzle that impacts a completely different one. That is crazy dangerous."** So shared data in a supply chain becomes a transmission medium for prompt injection: a malicious payload introduced at one node can be read and acted on by an inline agent at another, crossing organisational boundaries (cross-ref: the why-now brief, on prompt injection turning benign agents).

## How The Vaults Help

The mechanism, distinct from data management: **distribute keys, track permissions, carry the decisions.**

The project lead: **"the vaults could help manage the data, but that is a separate project. What the vaults can do here is help propagate and distribute, and give each member of the supply chain public and private keys, so the permissions are tracked and distributed at every level and the risks are accepted at every level. A vault can carry the risk-factorisation-matrix decisions, which is quite important."** So the vault role here is propagation and trust: a key for each member, permissions tracked and risks accepted at every level, and the risk-factorisation-matrix decisions travelling with the vault up and down the chain (cross-ref: the PKI and key-gated supply-chain work, and vaults-to-vaults).

## What This Asks For

1. **Propagate agent and permission information up and down the chain.**
2. **Surface partner-provided agents** organisations do not know they run.
3. **Make the chain aware and accepting** (terms and conditions, catalogued).
4. **Build the follow-the-data-then-check-for-agents graph** (the DPIA extension).
5. **Classify inline versus out-of-bounds** (position, access, transformation).
6. **Assess prompt-injection contamination** across shared data.
7. **Use vaults to distribute keys and carry the risk decisions** at every level.

Estimated effort: high; this spans organisations; the graph and the vault key-distribution are the core build, on top of the assessment template.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not single-company.** It spans the whole chain.
- **Not the data-management project.** Vaults here propagate trust, not manage the data.
- **Not behaviour after the fact.** It maps access and the inline risk in advance.
- **Not optional disclosure.** Awareness and acceptance are part of the product.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: up-and-down propagation is a coordination problem.** Many parties must participate. Mitigation: PKI keys per member and vaults are the propagation mechanism.

**Tension 2: catalogue fatigue.** Cataloguing every agent can overwhelm, as SBOM adoption showed. Mitigation: the graph and the inline-versus-out-of-bounds filter prioritise what matters.

**Tension 3: inline contamination is hard to bound.** Shared data makes blast radius cross-organisational. Mitigation: classify inline agents first; they carry the prompt-injection risk.

**Tension 4: scope creep into data management.** This is not the data project. Mitigation: keep the vault role to keys, tracking, and the risk decisions.
