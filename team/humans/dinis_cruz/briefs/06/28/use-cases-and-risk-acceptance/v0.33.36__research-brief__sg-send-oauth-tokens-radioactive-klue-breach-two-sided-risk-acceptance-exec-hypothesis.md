# OAuth Tokens Are Radioactive Material: The Klue Breach as a Risk-Acceptance Use Case

**version** v0.33.36
**date** 28 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Strategy, Architect, @Dev
**type** Research brief (use case)

## What This Is

The first specific use case, OAuth tokens treated as radioactive material, grounded in the June 2026 Klue supply-chain breach: **a threat actor harvested a supplier's database of active customer OAuth tokens and used them to reach the Salesforce data of many large companies, authenticating directly and bypassing the usual controls; the use case maps the incident and then the two-sided risk acceptance, the supplier that hosts the tokens on one side and the customer companies that granted them on the other, each propagating to its executives; and it states the central hypothesis, that most executives on both sides have no real grasp of the risk, treating the tokens like a bit of toxic material in a cupboard when they are in fact radioactive, assets that must be handled, protected, and monitored as among the most important the company holds.** It is the first shipped example under the use-case strategy (cross-ref: the v0.33.36 use-case-driven-shipping brief, the v0.33.35 risk-register brief, and the v0.33.34 Calendly RiskMandate review). New contributions: **the radioactive-material framing, the two-sided acceptance, and the exec hypothesis.**

## The Incident

In mid-June 2026 a threat actor group known as Icarus compromised Klue, a competitive-intelligence platform whose Battlecards application integrates with Salesforce and other systems through OAuth. The attackers gained initial access through a long-disused but still active legacy credential, originally created to prototype an integration that was later abandoned, then deployed code inside Klue's infrastructure to harvest the OAuth tokens Klue held for its customers. With those tokens they authenticated directly into customers' Salesforce environments and exfiltrated CRM data before access was cut off. Salesforce disabled the Klue integration on 17 June 2026, and Klue rotated the compromised tokens and shut down its other integrations. Reported affected organisations include Huntress, Recorded Future, LastPass, Jamf, Tanium, Gong, Sprout Social, and ReliaQuest, among others, and the exposed data was commercial CRM information such as contacts, quotes, and sales messages rather than passwords or payment data. Reporting notes this is the third major Salesforce-ecosystem compromise this year to turn on connected-application OAuth token abuse, following the 2025 Salesloft Drift incident and the Vercel and Context.ai breach earlier in 2026. See Sources.

## Why OAuth Tokens Are Radioactive

OAuth tokens are secrets that grant access to systems without a password, and a stolen token authenticates on its own, so the usual second-factor protection on the account does not stand in the way. The project lead: **"you do not have 2FA on it, so if you steal them, that is it."** Agentic workflows make granting this access frictionless. The project lead: **"the agentic workflow makes it super easy, off you go, and suddenly it has full access to everything."** And concentrating these tokens is uniquely dangerous. The project lead: **"when you capture OAuth tokens you create one of the most dangerous databases there is, because it is literally a database of your customers' access."** Hence the framing. The project lead: **"OAuth tokens are radioactive material, they have to be handled carefully, protected, monitored, and treated as some of the most important assets the company has, and I do not think that is what happens in most cases."**

## The Two-Sided Risk Acceptance

What makes this a rich use case is that the risk has to be accepted on both sides of the relationship.

**Side A, the company hosting the tokens.** A supplier like Klue holds a store of active customer OAuth tokens. The risk that this store is breached, and becomes the single point from which many customers are compromised, has to be accepted and owned, and propagated up to that supplier's executives.

**Side B, the company that granted the tokens.** Each customer that connected the supplier to its Salesforce, by granting OAuth access, handed out a key to its own data. The project lead: **"I want the risk acceptance of both the company that hosts the OAuth tokens and the company that gave the tokens out."** The executives of the customer authorised, directly or by omission, the granting of that access, so the risk has to be accepted and propagated up their chain too.

Each side runs the same workflow: the risk is presented, the stakeholder asks what it means, how to think about it, what they are deciding, and where the evidence is, and it is accepted for an interval and propagated. And each side faces the same control question, revocation: who is responsible for revoking and rotating the tokens, and how fast can they do it, since revocation is the act that ends the exposure.

## The Hypothesis

The use case rests on a claim the project lead wants stated as a hypothesis to test, not a determination. The project lead: **"my biggest hypothesis is that most execs of both these companies have no idea of the risk these carry."** The intuition behind it is an analogy. The project lead: **"it is the difference between having a bit of toxic material in a cupboard versus having radioactive material."** Executives may feel the tokens need protecting in a general way, while not treating them as radioactive, monitored, and ring-fenced as among the most critical assets they hold. Whether that is true of any given company is exactly what the use case is designed to surface.

## Mapping It Onto the Model

The scenario maps cleanly onto the semantic graph and the twins. The token store is an asset held by a system, the supplier and each customer are twins, and the OAuth token is a high-value asset node. The attack is token harvest followed by direct authentication that bypasses the second factor, performed by the threat agent. The impact runs through confidentiality, the exfiltration of CRM data, and on to regulatory exposure where personal data is involved. Two acceptance and propagation chains run in parallel, one for the supplier and one for each customer, and revocation is the control whose owner and speed are themselves recordable facts. As a shipped use case, every acceptance it produces counts toward the recurring-risk-acceptance metric.

## Why This Is a Good First Use Case

It is current, real, and agentic, the tokens are exactly the kind of access an agent is handed and an attacker now harvests at scale. It has the two-sided structure that shows propagation on both the supplier and the customer side. And the radioactive-material framing makes the stakes visceral in a way a control checklist never does, which is precisely the kind of example people only understand once they see it.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a determination.** The exec ignorance is a hypothesis to test, not a verdict on any company.
- **Not legal advice.** Regulatory exposure is noted, not adjudicated.
- **Not the final incident record.** Details are from public reporting and are still evolving.
- **Not blame.** The point is the risk to be owned, not fault to be assigned.

## Honest Notes

- **Reporting evolves.** The affected-company list and the root cause may be refined as investigations continue.
- **The analogy simplifies.** Radioactive material is a framing device, not a technical equivalence.
- **Two sides, different evidence.** The supplier and the customer hold different facts and evidence about the same risk.
