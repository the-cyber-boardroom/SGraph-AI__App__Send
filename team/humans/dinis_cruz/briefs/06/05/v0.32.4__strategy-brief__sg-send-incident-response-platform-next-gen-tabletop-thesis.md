# An Incident Response Platform On SGraph: Next-Generation Tabletop Exercises, Anchored On Real Compromises

## What This Is

The founding thesis for an incident response platform built on SGraph, put together as a whole the way the NHI 2.0 thesis was (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-agent-identity-startup-thesis brief): **the next generation of tabletop exercises, where LLM-driven and human players role-play real-world compromise scenarios on as-real-as-possible data, with full provenance, replay, and time travel, and the vault as the incident store.** It pulls together several existing threads (the digital twin, simulation, time travel, multi-agent coordination, and provenance) and enriches them with ideas and workflows from earlier incident and tabletop work. This is a set of ideas for how it could be done, especially with partners, and the founding brief for a series.

## The Core Idea: Next-Generation Tabletop Exercises

The central concept: **the next generation of tabletop exercises, made possible by primitives that did not exist before LLMs.**

The project lead: **"the core idea is to do the next generation of tabletop exercises, where a bunch of primitives now go into play, the stuff we can now do with LLMs that we could never do in the past. This is where it gets very interesting to pull it off."** So the platform reinvents the tabletop exercise (the structured walkthrough of an incident scenario) using LLM-era primitives: LLM-driven players, semantic graphs, provenance, replay, and time travel. The vault is the underpinning architecture, the incident store and the replay engine (below). This sits directly on a side business already identified (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 sellable-deliverables brief, which named incident simulation and tabletop exercises) and is now developed into its own platform thesis.

## Simulations On Real Data, All The Time

The first premise, and an important one: **the platform runs simulations on as-real-as-possible data all the time, moving away from theoretical exercises toward educating users with real material.**

The project lead: **"one of the fundamental premises is that we are running simulations on top of as-real-data-as-possible all the time, moving away from theoretical things, and leveraging the ability to really educate the user and show real stuff."** So the platform is not a set of abstract what-ifs; it runs on real data and real scenarios, which both educates the user more effectively and lends credibility. This is the digital-twin and simulation thinking (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-visualisation and risk-management briefs) applied to incident response: simulate on the real picture, not a theoretical one.

## Gamification And Role-Play

The format: **a gamification element where individuals role-play how they would react and respond to a set of scenarios and events.**

The project lead: **"there is a bit of a gamification element to this. The typical exercise is to allow individuals to role-play, based on a set of scenarios, how they would react and respond to a particular set of events. There is an element of education, and an element of what happens next."** So the exercise is a game: participants take roles and play out how they would respond as events unfold, learning by doing rather than reading. The what-happens-next element (the choose-your-own-adventure below) is what makes it engaging and educational at once.

## Human And LLM Players, On The Graph

The mechanism: **a scenario has multiple players, some played by the humans learning and some by LLMs operating inline, all on semantic graphs and workflows with maximum provenance and the ability to go back in time.**

The project lead: **"there are a number of personas and players. Some players are done by the humans, the people learning, and some are done by an LLM operating inline. This should all be based on semantic graphs, workflows, and decision-making, with maximum provenance all the time, even including the ability to go back in time, navigate the graph, and try different scenarios."**

| Player Type | Role |
|-------------|------|
| **Human players** | The people learning, role-playing their part |
| **LLM players** | Operating inline, playing other parts |
| **The substrate** | Semantic graphs, workflows, decision-making |
| **The guarantees** | Maximum provenance; go back in time; navigate the graph; try different scenarios |

So a scenario is populated by a mix of human and LLM players (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 three-agent-comms-demo brief's multiple agents and the FAQ's agent roster), all acting on the semantic graph (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-semantic-knowledge-graphs brief), with every move provenanced and the whole thing rewindable (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-time brief's time travel). The graph, the provenance, and the time travel are what make it more than a conversation: it is a navigable, replayable, branchable simulation.

## Anchored On Real-World Compromises

A key credibility move: **anchor every scenario on real-world compromises, researched from the last tens or hundreds of disclosed incidents, so nothing is theoretical and the platform stays fresh.**

The project lead: **"the whole thing should be anchored on real-world scenarios. I would research the last ten, fifty, a hundred real-world compromises, stuff that actually happened, publicly disclosed, reported, written up as case studies. So when people look at a scenario, it is not theoretical. Especially now, in the age of LLMs that can invent a hundred scenarios, the credibility to say, no, this just happened, even the date, this is recent, brings a level of freshness to the platform."**

| Invented Scenario | Real-World-Anchored Scenario |
|-------------------|------------------------------|
| One of a hundred LLM inventions | This actually happened, on this date |
| No credibility | Credible: disclosed, reported, case-studied |
| Stale | Fresh: recent, current incidents |

So scenarios are drawn from real, disclosed compromises (researched from the last 10, 50, 100 incidents), which matters precisely because LLMs can invent endless plausible scenarios: the credibility comes from this really happened, recently. This also keeps the platform fresh, new real incidents continually refresh the scenario library. (This is a research workflow the platform runs continuously: ingest and structure recent disclosed incidents.)

## Runs Everywhere, Isolated

A deployment requirement, tied to trust: **it must run everywhere, in a completely isolated environment, because people will not put their data in unless it does.**

The project lead: **"this needs to run everywhere, because people are not going to put their data in here unless it runs in an environment that is completely isolated."** So the platform must deploy into the customer's own isolated environment (the sovereignty and no-inline principles; cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-access-not-security and the v0.31.9 blast-radius briefs), because incident scenarios run on the customer's real, sensitive data, and that only happens if the environment is isolated and under their control. The vault's zero-knowledge, run-anywhere model is what makes simulating on real data acceptable.

## The Players: Adversary, Regulator, And The Whole Cast

The cast, and a sharp inclusion: **map the players a scenario needs, including the adversarial player and the regulator player, across the executive, security, and ops roles, and one person can play several.**

The project lead: **"for each scenario we map out two, three, five, ten players, call them players. One individual can even do multiple players. You should have the adversarial player, the regulator player, whatever players make sense, from the CEO to the executive to the security team to the ops team. The vision is multiple agents talking to each other: say four players, the executive, the security professional, the attacker, and the regulator."**

| Player | Perspective |
|--------|-------------|
| **Executive / CEO** | The business decisions and pressures |
| **Security professional / team** | The technical response |
| **Ops team** | Operations under incident |
| **The attacker (adversarial player)** | The threat actor's moves |
| **The regulator** | The compliance and notification dimension |

So a scenario is a multi-party simulation including not just the defenders but the attacker and the regulator, which is what makes it realistic (an incident is shaped by what the attacker does and what the regulator demands, not only by the defenders). One person can play several players, and the players talk to each other (cross-ref: the three-agent-comms-demo and Email-FS-lite briefs' multi-agent coordination). Including the adversarial and regulator players is the move that turns a defender walkthrough into a real-world simulation.

## Multi-Act, Choose-Your-Own-Adventure

The structure: **scenarios run as multiple acts or phases, replayable, choose-your-own-adventure, where what you choose determines what happens next.**

The project lead: **"it is like multiple acts. You should be able to replay this: here are four or five phases. SG, and I think Sentinel had some ideas on this, describes something like this. You have multiple phases, multiple acts, and then it is choose-your-own-adventure: depending on what you choose, what happens next."** So a scenario is staged in acts, and at each branch the players' choices determine the next act, a choose-your-own-adventure structure, and the whole thing is replayable. This connects to SG/Sentinel's earlier thinking on phased scenarios (cross-ref: the v0.27.58 SG-Sentinel compliance-as-living-graph brief) and to the replay and branching the graph and time travel enable.

## Tweakable Variables

A powerful realism feature: **anchor on a real scenario, then tweak the variables, the attacker's sophistication, the security team's maturity, the executives' literacy, the regulator's aggressiveness, scaling to dozens of players.**

The project lead: **"if you take a real scenario, there are a number of paths it could take, and you can tweak things. I want a more aggressive attacker, or a less sophisticated attacker. I want a mature security team, an immature one, an outsourced one. I want executives who understand cybersecurity, or who do not. I want a regulator that cares, or one that is not aggressive. You can make this as big as you want, eventually dozens of people, twenty, which is more like a real-world scenario."**

| Variable | Range |
|----------|-------|
| **Attacker** | More aggressive, or less sophisticated |
| **Security team** | Mature, immature, or outsourced |
| **Executives** | Cyber-literate, or not |
| **Regulator** | Caring and aggressive, or lax |
| **Scale** | A handful of players up to dozens, like the real world |

So from one real-world anchor, you generate many variations by tuning the players' characters, which both teaches (how does the same incident go with a mature versus immature team?) and stress-tests (how robust is our response across attacker and regulator behaviours?). Scaling to dozens of players approaches the messiness of a real incident.

## The Vault As Incident Store And Replay Engine

The architectural payoff: **the vault is the incident storage, holding all the data and all the LLM calls with provenance, so the whole exercise can be replayed, scored, compared with the real world, and saved.**

The project lead: **"with the vault, the vault becomes the incident storage, the place where all the data gets stored and where you can replay. You have all the LLM calls with provenance, so it becomes a great way to visualise how everything fits together, connect the dots, save past executions, and map across them. Then you play the game, and you see what happens, how you score, what happened in the real world, what different behaviours occurred, and you can tweak the variables."**

| The Vault Provides | For The Exercise |
|--------------------|------------------|
| **Incident storage** | All the data and state in one place |
| **All LLM calls with provenance** | Every player move recorded and attributable (cross-ref: the foundations brief) |
| **Replay** | Re-run the exercise exactly |
| **Scoring and comparison** | How you scored; what happened in the real world; what behaviours differed |
| **Saved executions** | Past runs saved and mapped across |

So the vault is the underpinning architecture (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-multiple-identities-chain and foundations briefs' provenance, and the v0.32.3 visualisation brief): it stores the incident, records every LLM call with provenance, and enables replay, scoring, comparison against the real outcome, and saving runs for later analysis. This is what turns a one-off exercise into a durable, analysable asset, and it is the natural home for big-team, collaborative tabletop exercises.

## Enriching Ideas And Workflows From Past Memos

Drawing on earlier incident and tabletop work to add depth (these predate the current version line but apply directly):

| Prior Idea | How It Enriches The Platform |
|------------|------------------------------|
| **Incidents are the best time to fix things (the 5x multiplier)** | Frame exercises around what gets fixed (observability, runbooks, knowledge, tests); an exercise should surface the fixes a real incident would |
| **Simulation before action (first, do no harm)** | Use the world model and digital twin to predict the consequences of each response action before taking it; a core player skill to teach |
| **Multi-audience stakeholder communication** | One incident produces many briefings (executive, technical, regulator, customer); generate them per player and score the communications |
| **Escalation mapping (the 2am question)** | Build the escalation graph into scenarios: who gets woken, when, how triggered, with what acknowledgement |
| **Framework integration (MITRE ATT&CK, NIST, ISO)** | Map each scenario to the frameworks one incident at a time; the exercise expands the organisation's coverage |
| **Third-story systemic weaknesses** | Beyond what happened and why, surface the systemic weakness that recurs across incidents; track it across exercises |
| **The football-team preparation spectrum** | Track each scenario along unprepared, practised, operationalised, automated; practice makes real incidents look like exercises |
| **Every role is a security sensor** | Each player watches for the how-come-this-was-possible signals from their domain |
| **The slow-tennis-game replay model** | The earlier tabletop ran every action, question, and decision as a node with relationships in a replayable store; the vault is now that store, with provenance |
| **Realistic timing, layered in later** | Start with answers at any speed; later add realistic constraints (that is a two-hour investigation, not five seconds) |

These workflows give the platform its depth: the exercise is not only a role-play but a structured way to surface fixes, teach simulation-before-action, generate and score multi-audience communications, build escalation graphs, expand framework coverage, and find systemic weaknesses, all replayable in the vault with provenance.

## What This Asks For

1. **Build the next-generation tabletop platform on the vault** (LLM and human players; the vault as incident store and replay engine).
2. **Simulate on real data, all the time** (the digital twin; not theoretical; educate with real material).
3. **Use gamification and role-play** (players act out how they respond; what-happens-next).
4. **Mix human and LLM players on the graph** (semantic graphs, workflows, maximum provenance, time travel, branching).
5. **Anchor on real-world compromises** (research the last tens or hundreds of disclosed incidents; credibility and freshness; a continuous research workflow).
6. **Run everywhere, isolated** (the customer's environment; sovereignty; so they will use real data).
7. **Cast the full set of players** (including the adversary and the regulator; one person can play several; scale to dozens).
8. **Structure as multi-act, choose-your-own-adventure** (phases, branches, replay; the SG/Sentinel phased thinking).
9. **Tweak the variables** (attacker sophistication, team maturity, executive literacy, regulator aggressiveness).
10. **Enrich with the prior workflows** (the 5x fix multiplier, simulation-before-action, multi-audience comms, escalation graphs, framework integration, third-story weaknesses, the preparation spectrum, every-role-a-sensor, replayable provenance, realistic timing later).
11. **Pursue partners** (this is a partner-friendly platform, like the NHI 2.0 thesis).

Estimated effort: substantial but well-supported, the digital twin, simulation, provenance, time travel, multi-agent coordination, and the vault all exist across the corpus; the contribution is the incident-response framing and the real-world-anchored, replayable, tweakable tabletop. The MVP is one real, disclosed compromise turned into a multi-act scenario with four players (executive, security, attacker, regulator), human and LLM, run and replayed in an isolated vault with full provenance and scoring.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not theoretical.** Simulations on real data, anchored on real compromises.
- **Not invented scenarios.** Real, disclosed incidents; credibility and freshness.
- **Not defender-only.** The adversary and the regulator are players.
- **Not a one-off.** Replayable, saveable, comparable, in the vault.
- **Not centralised or inline.** Runs everywhere, isolated, in the customer's environment.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Real customer data in scenarios is sensitive.** Simulating on real data concentrates sensitive information. Mitigation: runs everywhere, completely isolated, in the customer's environment (cross-ref: the access-not-security and blast-radius briefs); zero-knowledge.

**Risk 2: Real-world incident research must be accurate and current.** Anchoring on real compromises requires good, fresh research. Mitigation: a continuous research workflow over disclosed incidents and case studies; cite sources; keep it fresh.

**Risk 3: LLM players must behave plausibly.** Unrealistic LLM players undermine the exercise. Mitigation: ground them in the semantic graph and the real scenario; the adversary and regulator players modelled on real behaviour; provenance to review.

**Risk 4: Scoring and realism need care.** Scoring responses and timing is hard. Mitigation: start simple (any speed), layer in realistic timing later (the prior workflow); compare against the real-world outcome.

**Risk 5: Scope can balloon (dozens of players, many variables).** The platform can grow large. Mitigation: start with one scenario and four players; the preparation spectrum and incremental framework mapping keep it bounded.
