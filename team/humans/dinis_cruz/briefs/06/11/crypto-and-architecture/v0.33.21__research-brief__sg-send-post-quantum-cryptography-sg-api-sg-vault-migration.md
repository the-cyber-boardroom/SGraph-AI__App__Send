## What This Is

Research on post-quantum cryptography and how to add it to SG/API and SG/Vault: **the symmetric layer (AES-256, hashing) is already largely quantum-appropriate, so the work is the asymmetric layer, the elliptic-curve key exchange and signatures, which a cryptographically-relevant quantum computer would break, and the right path is hybrid post-quantum algorithms plus crypto-agility, prioritised by the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat to long-lived confidential vaults.** It builds on the TLS research and the PKI and key-management work (cross-ref: the v0.33.2 tls-ssl-cybersecurity-skills-landscape, v0.32.4 pki-public-key-registry, v0.33.16 recipient-key-management-options, and v0.33.21 biscuit-comparison briefs). The primary source is the Red Sift guide by Ivan Ristic (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 expert-brand-vaults-ivan-ristic-tls brief), with the SG facts from the project's own tooling. New contributions: **the SG crypto inventory mapped to the quantum threat, the harvest-now-decrypt-later priority, the hybrid migration for SG/API and SG/Vault, and crypto-agility as the architecture.**

## The Threat

The core of it: **a cryptographically-relevant quantum computer would break today's public-key cryptography outright, while only weakly affecting symmetric cryptography and hashing.**

Shor's algorithm, run on such a computer, breaks the popular public-key primitives: RSA, elliptic-curve encryption, and Diffie-Hellman key exchange. Grover's algorithm affects symmetric encryption and hashing, but on a much smaller scale, so it is far less dangerous. The practical consequence is that the asymmetric parts of any system are the exposure, and the symmetric parts are largely safe at adequate key sizes.

The urgent variant is harvest now, decrypt later: an adversary captures encrypted data today, stores it, and decrypts it once a quantum computer is available. The Red Sift guide is explicit that encrypted backups and centralized cryptographic architectures, where breaking a central key unlocks a deep hierarchy of keys, are obvious targets, and that data with a confidentiality lifetime beyond ten years is in the urgent category.

## The Standards

The landscape, as of this research: **NIST standardised the first general-purpose post-quantum algorithms in August 2024, and hybrid classical-plus-post-quantum designs are the recommended transition.**

| Standard | Purpose | Replaces |
|----------|---------|----------|
| ML-KEM (FIPS 203, formerly Kyber) | Key encapsulation and exchange | ECDH, X25519 |
| ML-DSA (FIPS 204, formerly Dilithium) | Digital signatures | Ed25519, ECDSA, RSA |
| SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, formerly SPHINCS+) | Hash-based signatures, a conservative backup | A backup for ML-DSA |
| FN-DSA (formerly Falcon), HQC | Compact signatures, a KEM backup | Forthcoming |
| Classic McEliece, FrodoKEM (ISO) | Key encapsulation, more conservative | The EU and conservative path |

Two practical points from the guide. First, hybrid designs combine a classical and a post-quantum primitive so that a failure of a brand-new post-quantum algorithm does not drop security below today's level; in TLS 1.3 the hybrid key exchange X25519MLKEM768 is already protecting significant internet traffic and is being adopted by all major browsers. Second, leading messaging platforms (Apple iMessage and Signal) have already updated their protocols, and the recommended technical actions are to phase out RSA, EC, DH, and ECDH, use 256-bit symmetric encryption such as AES-256, and move to 384-bit hash functions.

## SG/Vault's Crypto, Mapped To The Threat

The good news first: **most of SG/Vault's cryptography is symmetric and already quantum-appropriate, and the work is concentrated in the asymmetric layer.**

| SG Crypto | Type | Quantum Exposure | Action |
|-----------|------|------------------|--------|
| AES-256-GCM (vault data, SGit) | Symmetric | Grover only halves effective strength; 256-bit is the recommended quantum-safe choice | Keep; already at the recommended size |
| PBKDF2 (passphrase to keys) | Key derivation | Weakly affected; high iteration count helps | Keep; consider raising iterations or a memory-hard KDF |
| SHA-256 (content-addressable object IDs) | Hash | Grover halves effective strength; the guidance prefers 384-bit | Consider a 384-bit hash for new content (with care for the content-addressable scheme) |
| X25519 (NaCl sealed messages, key exchange, vault-to-vault) | Asymmetric (ECDH) | Shor breaks it | Augment with ML-KEM (hybrid) |
| Ed25519 (signed updates, vault key pairs, the PKI registry) | Asymmetric (signature) | Shor breaks it | Augment with ML-DSA (hybrid) |

So the symmetric core (AES-256, PBKDF2) already meets the guidance, and the asymmetric layer (the NaCl X25519 sealed messages and the Ed25519 signed updates and registry keys; cross-ref: the vault-to-vault-comms guide and the pki-public-key-registry and shipping-vault-signed-message briefs) is the part a quantum computer would break and therefore the focus of the migration.

## Why SG/Vault Is Closer To The Urgent Category

A sober assessment: **SG/Vault has several of the exact characteristics the guide flags as urgent.**

| Characteristic | Why It Raises Priority |
|----------------|------------------------|
| Long-lived confidential data | The healthcare and sensitive vaults have a confidentiality lifetime well beyond ten years, the urgent threshold |
| Harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure | The data is AES-256 (safe), but the data keys are exchanged or wrapped with X25519 (breakable), so harvesting the wrapped key plus the ciphertext now allows decryption later |
| A central key hierarchy | The parent-to-child key model and vault-in-vault hierarchy are exactly the centralized architecture where breaking a central key unlocks a hierarchy (cross-ref: the central-key-management and vault-in-vault briefs) |
| Signed updates that must hold for years | An Ed25519-signed update could be forged in the future, undermining the authorization model (cross-ref: the shipping-vault-signed-message brief) |

So even though the bulk encryption is quantum-safe, the asymmetric wrapping and signing create real harvest-now-decrypt-later and future-forgery exposure, and the central key hierarchy concentrates it. The priority is the central and parent keys and the long-lived vaults.

## Adding Post-Quantum To SG/API

The transport layer, and much of it comes for free: **enable hybrid post-quantum TLS (X25519MLKEM768) on the API endpoints, which the libraries, CDNs, and cloud providers are already adopting.**

Because the hybrid key exchange is being rolled out across browsers, Cloudflare, and the cloud providers, SG/API can gain quantum-resistant transport largely by keeping its TLS stack current and enabling the hybrid suite (cross-ref: the tls-ssl-cybersecurity-skills-landscape brief and the integration-landscape briefs). This protects API traffic in transit against harvest-now-decrypt-later with minimal bespoke work, and the deploy-into-the-customer's-cloud model means the customer's own current TLS termination can provide it (cross-ref: the aws-deployment brief).

## Adding Post-Quantum To SG/Vault

The harder and more important part, and the priority: **augment the asymmetric layer with hybrid post-quantum algorithms, keep the symmetric core, and prioritise the central keys and long-lived vaults.**

| Layer | Migration |
|-------|-----------|
| Sealed messages and key exchange (X25519) | Hybrid X25519 plus ML-KEM; encapsulate the data or wrapping key with ML-KEM |
| Signed updates and registry keys (Ed25519) | Hybrid Ed25519 plus ML-DSA; sign with both |
| Vault data (AES-256-GCM) | Keep; already quantum-appropriate |
| Object hashing (SHA-256) | Consider a 384-bit hash for new content |
| Key wrapping in the hierarchy | Re-wrap key material under hybrid post-quantum, central and parent keys first |

So the migration concentrates on the asymmetric operations: the vault-to-vault sealed messages and any key wrapping move to a hybrid X25519-plus-ML-KEM encapsulation, and the signed updates and registry keys move to a hybrid Ed25519-plus-ML-DSA signature. The AES-256 data does not need re-encrypting, but the key material that wraps it should be re-wrapped under post-quantum to defeat harvest-now-decrypt-later, starting with the central and parent keys that sit atop the hierarchy.

## Crypto-Agility Is The Real Win

The architectural principle that matters most: **build crypto-agility so algorithms can be swapped without re-architecting, because this shift will not be the last.**

The guide stresses crypto-agility, the ability to switch algorithms rapidly without disrupting operations, as the durable lesson of the post-quantum shift. For SG/Vault this means versioning the cryptography, carrying algorithm identifiers in the vault format and the public-key registry, and negotiating algorithms rather than hard-coding them (cross-ref: the pki-public-key-registry and the access-not-security briefs). This aligns with the corpus's portability and no-lock-in ethos: a crypto-agile vault can adopt ML-KEM and ML-DSA now and the next algorithms later, and can run hybrid in the meantime.

## Implementation Considerations

Three practical constraints to plan for:

**Post-quantum keys and signatures are larger.** ML-KEM and ML-DSA public keys and signatures are kilobytes, against Ed25519's tens of bytes. This matters for any small-token goals (cross-ref: the biscuit-comparison brief, where tokens fit in a cookie), for the message size (the QA brief's 5 MB limit absorbs it easily; cross-ref: the vault-update QA brief), and for storage and bandwidth. It is manageable but real.

**The browser lacks native post-quantum support.** SGit matches the Web Crypto API exactly, but the Web Crypto API does not yet offer ML-KEM or ML-DSA, so application-layer post-quantum in the browser-based vault apps needs a WebAssembly library. Hybrid TLS already covers the browser's transport, but the vault's own sealed messages and signatures in the browser need WASM.

**Hybrid is the safe transition, with options.** Use hybrid (classical plus post-quantum) so a failure of a new primitive does not reduce security below today's; use the NIST algorithms (ML-KEM, ML-DSA), and consider the more conservative ISO options (Classic McEliece, FrodoKEM) where a conservative or European posture is wanted (cross-ref: the hyperscaler-outreach brief's European play).

## The Advantage: A Clean, Owned Crypto Inventory

A point in SG/Vault's favour: **the first step the guide recommends, inventorying cryptographic assets, is easy for SG/Vault because its crypto stack is small, owned, and known.**

Where most organisations face a sprawling, opaque crypto estate, SG/Vault's stack is a short, known list (AES-256-GCM, PBKDF2, SHA-256, X25519, Ed25519) in code the project controls. This makes the discovery and inventory step (cross-ref: the artefact-driven-security-assessments and blast-radius briefs) fast, and the migration far more tractable than a typical enterprise's, which is both an operational advantage and a positioning one.

## The Positioning Opportunity

Worth noting for the outreach work: **being hybrid post-quantum and crypto-agile is a differentiator, especially for long-lived sensitive data and for partner and hyperscaler conversations.**

Because SG/Vault targets exactly the long-lived, sensitive, harvest-now-decrypt-later data that is in the urgent category, post-quantum readiness is a selling point, not just hygiene (cross-ref: the partner-briefing and hyperscaler-outreach briefs). Crypto-agility is itself a feature to advertise. And the source for this research, Ivan Ristic of Red Sift, is already a TLS-expert target in the corpus (cross-ref: the expert-brand-vaults-ivan-ristic-tls brief), which connects this work to the partner and expert outreach.

## Recommendations

Mapped to the consensus timeline (start by 2026, high priority by 2030, complete by 2035):

1. **Now: inventory and triage** (the short, owned crypto list; identify the long-lived vaults and the central keys).
2. **Now: enable hybrid TLS on SG/API** (X25519MLKEM768; mostly via current libraries and the cloud).
3. **Now: design crypto-agility into the vault format and registry** (algorithm identifiers; negotiation; versioned crypto).
4. **Next: hybrid the asymmetric layer in SG/Vault** (X25519 plus ML-KEM for sealed messages and wrapping; Ed25519 plus ML-DSA for signed updates and registry keys).
5. **Next: re-wrap the central and parent keys first** (defeat harvest-now-decrypt-later on the hierarchy).
6. **Next: add a WebAssembly post-quantum library for the browser vault apps** (the Web Crypto gap).
7. **Later: consider 384-bit hashing for new content, and the conservative ISO options** (where warranted).
8. **Throughout: keep the symmetric core** (AES-256 already meets the guidance).
