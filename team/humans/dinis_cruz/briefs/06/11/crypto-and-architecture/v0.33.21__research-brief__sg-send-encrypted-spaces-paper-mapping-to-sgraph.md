## What This Is

A mapping of the core ideas of the Encrypted Collaboration Spaces whitepaper (Orru, Perrin, Trapp, and Zaverucha, dated 11 June 2026) onto what SGraph is building with SG/Send, SG/Vault, and SGit: **the paper and SGraph share the same north star, end-to-end-encrypted collaboration on an untrusted server that holds no keys, and SGraph is the shipping, pragmatic version of that vision while the paper is the rigorous, cryptographically-verifiable version, so the paper validates the SGraph thesis, several SGraph designs already match it independently, and its biggest gifts to SGraph are verifiable server behaviour and a formal retention system.** It connects to most of the SG/Vault corpus, especially the signed-update, registry, key-management, indexing, and post-quantum work (cross-ref: the v0.33.2 shipping-vault-signed-message, v0.32.4 pki-public-key-registry and deterministic-value-indexes, v0.32.7 central-key-management, v0.33.16 recipient-key-management, and v0.33.21 post-quantum-cryptography and biscuit-comparison briefs). The paper is paraphrased throughout; the source is listed at the end. New contributions: **the concept mapping, the independent-convergence findings, the verifiability opportunity, and the retention-system adoption path.**

## The Paper In Brief

Encrypted collaboration spaces are a storage architecture that layers secure-collaboration protocols on top of untrusted servers. To a developer a space looks like a sync engine with built-in group management; underneath, it encrypts the data, manages keys and users, and lets clients cryptographically verify that the server behaved correctly. The paper's claim is that no prior system satisfied all four of: a generic data store for arbitrary collaborative apps with rich membership and retention policies; encrypted and integrity-protected data; client-verifiable correctness of server behaviour; and key management hidden from both users and developers. Its threat model holds confidentiality and integrity even against the hosting server, tolerating server misbehaviour as long as it is detectable, and a defining property is that the server holds no cryptographic keys.

## The Shared North Star

The most important point first: **the paper and SGraph are after the same thing.** Both build end-to-end-encrypted collaboration on an untrusted server that holds no keys; both present to developers as a sync-engine-with-groups rather than a cryptography toolkit; both aim to hide key management from users and developers; and both target small collaborative groups (the paper cites up to around two hundred members). SG/Vault's zero-knowledge vaults, SGit's server-never-sees-plaintext model, the recipient-key-management goal of making keys disappear, and the vault-as-a-database ambition are the same commitments the paper formalises (cross-ref: the recipient-key-management-options and webify-spreadsheets briefs). The paper is, in effect, the peer-reviewed-calibre statement of the SGraph thesis.

## Where SGraph Already Matches The Paper

Several SGraph designs land on the paper's choices independently, which is the strongest signal that SGraph is on the right track:

**The transition system is the signed-update model.** The paper models a space as a transition system where an apply function maps an authenticated state and a signed change to a new state, run identically on the server, the prover, and the client. SG/Vault's signed updates are exactly this: a message signed with the parent admin key is the only way to advance a vault's state (cross-ref: the shipping-vault-signed-message brief). The paper formalises what SG/Vault ships.

**Plaintext structure, encrypted values is the deterministic value index.** The paper encrypts column values while keeping structural identifiers (channel and user IDs) plaintext so the server can route, filter, and order without learning content. SG/Vault's deterministic value indexes do the same thing, plaintext structural coordinates over encrypted values, and the content-transformation proxy keeps structural IDs plaintext for the same reason (cross-ref: the deterministic-value-indexes and content-transformation-proxy-vault-integration briefs).

**Hybrid X25519 plus ML-KEM is today's post-quantum direction.** The paper's prototype uses the X-Wing KEM (X25519 with ML-KEM-768) and quantum-sound hash-based proofs, and notes every primitive except the signature is quantum-secure. This is precisely the hybrid asymmetric path and quantum-appropriate symmetric core that today's post-quantum brief recommends for SG/Vault (cross-ref: the post-quantum-cryptography brief). Two independent designs converged on the same answer on the same day.

**Actions versus native apply is app-logic versus the kernel.** The paper distinguishes declarative actions (constrained, statically analysable, like stored procedures) from hand-written apply functions (writing kernel code, where the author becomes the trust root). This is SGraph's native-versus-app decision, with the vault-in-vault kernel model as the kernel tier (cross-ref: the vault-in-vault-kernel-model and the content-proxy and QA-vault native-versus-app notes).

**Server-has-no-keys to portable is the sovereignty thesis.** Because the server holds no keys, the paper observes the server state can move between devices, enabling peer-to-peer, federation, and local-first. SGraph makes the same argument for sovereignty and no lock-in, and the S3-backed distributed vault is a concrete instance (cross-ref: the access-not-security and content-proxy vault-integration briefs).

## The Biggest Opportunity: Verifiable Server Behaviour

The clearest thing SGraph can take from the paper: **cryptographic proof that the untrusted server applied changes correctly, not just that changes were signed.** SGraph today trusts the signed-update model, signatures authorise a change, and version control records history. The paper goes further with two proof types: tracer proofs (a sparse Merkle view of exactly the nodes an operation touched, which a client replays to confirm the state transition) and fast-forward proofs (a recursively-composable zkVM proof that a batch of changes was reconciled correctly, authored by valid members, permitted by the access rules in force at the time, and produced the claimed commitment). A client joining or returning from offline verifies one fast-forward proof to trust the latest state, rather than replaying the whole history. For SGraph this is the path from this-update-was-signed to this-server-applied-every-update-correctly-and-I-can-prove-it, a meaningful trust upgrade for the sensitive, long-lived vaults (cross-ref: the artefact-driven-security-assessments and shipping-vault briefs). The cost is real (the paper's prototype spends tens of milliseconds of GPU time per change and produces proofs of a few hundred kilobytes), so it is an aspiration to study, not a quick adoption.

## The Most Adoptable Machinery: The Retention System

The single most directly useful piece for SGraph's real problems: **a retention system that cryptographically deletes old data and gives new members selective access to history, aligned to the data structure.** The paper's two-tiered key tree (group keys linked by encryption edges at the top, per-data derivation chains below) supports three composing operations, rekey, derivation, and cryptographic deletion, without re-encrypting historical data, and it can be aligned to a filesystem (grant a directory, drop a subtree) in the Cryptree style. This answers two problems SGraph has named but not formalised: the data-lifetime and retention question for sensitive (for example healthcare) vaults, and the requirement that a newly-added recipient should not automatically inherit all prior history. SG/Vault's vault-in-vault hierarchy and central key management are the natural home for a retention tree (cross-ref: the central-key-management, recipient-key-management, and vault-in-vault briefs), and the alignment-to-data-structure idea fits the semantic-graph and folder models SGraph already uses.

| Client cache as verifier | Once a client has enough coverage, its cache verifies without per-change proofs (fits SGit clones) |

## Where SGraph Differs, And Stays Pragmatic

The differences are as instructive as the overlaps. SGraph is shipping and pragmatic: it authorises with signatures and records history with version control, where the paper is a research prototype that proves correctness with a zkVM (RISC Zero) at real cost. SGraph's append-and-commit model is simpler than the paper's changelog-plus-proof system, and its registry is a lighter key-transparency layer. The paper does not address SGraph's vault-as-app and webify experiences, the content-transformation use cases, or the agentic and outreach work; it is focused on the verifiable-database core. So the relationship is complementary: SGraph has the product, the UX, the apps, and the go-to-market, and the paper has the rigorous cryptographic machinery SGraph can adopt selectively where the trust requirements justify the cost.

## Validation And Outreach Signal

Worth flagging for the strategy and outreach work: **a team of well-known applied cryptographers, including the designer of the Signal protocol and a Microsoft Research cryptographer, independently converged on SGraph's architecture and published it the same day.** That is strong external validation of the SGraph thesis (untrusted server, no server keys, encrypted collaboration, a key hierarchy, hybrid post-quantum, a changelog), and the paper's citations and acknowledgements point at the same landscape SGraph is mapping (Proton Drive, Signal private groups, MLS, key transparency). The authors and the cited organisations are credible expert and partner-adjacent contacts (cross-ref: the expert-brand-vaults, partner-outreach, and company-intelligence briefs), and the convergence itself is a usable narrative: the academy is arriving where SGraph already is.

## Honest Caveats

**The paper is a prototype, not a product.** Its proofs are expensive today, and it acknowledges optimisation is needed; SGraph should not mistake the research for something shippable as-is.

**Adopting the verifiability core is a large lift.** A zkVM, tracer proofs, and fast-forward proofs are substantial engineering; the retention system is more tractable and probably the better first step.

**SGraph's pragmatism has real value.** Signatures plus version control ship today and serve the current users; the paper's machinery earns its place only where the trust requirement (a fully malicious server, with detectability) justifies the cost.

**The mapping is conceptual.** This is a reading of an external paper against SGraph's design; a deeper technical due-diligence would be needed before adopting any specific construction.

## Recommendations

1. **Treat the paper as validation and a roadmap input, not a competitor** (same north star; SGraph is the product).
2. **Adopt the retention system first** (cryptographic deletion and selective history, aligned to the vault-in-vault hierarchy; solves real data-lifetime and new-recipient problems).
3. **Study fast-forward and tracer proofs as the verifiability upgrade** (from signed-updates to provable correct server behaviour; a longer-term aspiration).
4. **Confirm the post-quantum direction with the paper's choices** (X-Wing, hybrid X25519 plus ML-KEM, quantum-sound hashing match today's brief).
5. **Consider key-transparency rigour for the registry** (consensus signers, fork detection).
6. **Use the convergence as an outreach narrative and expert-contact lead** (the authors and the cited organisations).
