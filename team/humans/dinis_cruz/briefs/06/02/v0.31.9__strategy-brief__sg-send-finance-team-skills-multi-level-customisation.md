## What This Is

A concrete use case that makes the skills thesis tangible: **skills for a finance team, expenses, invoicing, purchase orders, contract negotiations, budgets, contract sign-offs and approvals, NDAs, due-diligence requirements, each owned by the finance team that sets the standard.** The underlying principles (skills capture business knowledge, someone must own and maintain a delegated process, the economic model is paying the owner being cheaper than doing it yourself) are already documented (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 skills-as-business-knowledge-capture and ownership-of-delegated-processes briefs); this brief grounds them in a vivid example and adds two sharp points: **why the existing 20-to-200-page finance document does not work, and the multi-level customisation cascade** (the corporate skill, then the group version, the cybersecurity version, the ethics-response-team version).

## The Use Case: Finance Team Skills

The example: **each finance function needs its own skill, owned by the finance team because they set the standard.**

The project lead: **"creating a skill for the finance team on expenses, on invoicing, on purchase orders, on contract negotiations, budgets, contract sign-offs, approvals, even NDAs, due-diligence requirements. Each one of these needs a skill owned by the finance team, because they're the ones who set the standard."**

| Finance Function | The Skill Captures |
|------------------|--------------------|
| **Expenses** | How expenses are handled, approved, what is allowed |
| **Invoicing** | The invoicing process and rules |
| **Purchase orders** | How POs are raised and approved |
| **Contract negotiations** | How contracts are negotiated |
| **Budgets** | How budgets are set and managed |
| **Contract sign-offs / approvals** | The approval chains and thresholds |
| **NDAs** | The NDA process and standards |
| **Due-diligence requirements** | What due diligence requires |

Each is a discrete unit of finance-function knowledge, owned by the team that sets the standard. This is the knowledge-capture thesis (cross-ref: the knowledge-capture brief) made concrete: a skill captures how a finance function actually works, owned by the finance team. And the ownership is natural: the finance team sets the standard, so they own the skill, which is the right-owner principle (cross-ref: the ownership brief, where creators and the responsible team are the best owners).

## The Sharp Point: They Already Have This, As A 200-Page Document

The first genuinely new insight, and a sharp one: **finance teams already have this knowledge, but as a 20-to-200-page document that covers every corner case and does not apply to most teams.**

The project lead: **"usually finance teams already have this, not as a skill, but as a 20-to-200-page document that addresses everything to do with the company, every single corner case, everything, which doesn't apply to most teams."**

This is the static-document problem (cross-ref: the knowledge-capture brief's dead-playbook point) in its most recognisable form:

| The 200-Page Finance Document | The Skill |
|-------------------------------|-----------|
| Covers every corner case | The distilled, applicable knowledge |
| Applies to the whole company | Customised to the team that needs it |
| Static, consulted (if read at all) | Living, used, maintained |
| Most of it irrelevant to most teams | Only what is relevant to this team |

So the value of a skill over the existing document is not that the knowledge is new (finance has it), it is that the skill is the **distilled, applicable, customised, living** form of what is currently a giant, mostly-irrelevant-per-team, static document. This connects directly to the customisation and token-optimisation dimensions (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 skill-comparison brief's customisation dimensions): the skill is the document customised and optimised for a specific team's context, the relevant 5 pages, not the irrelevant 200. The 200-page document is the raw source; the skill is the usable, per-team derivative.

## The Multi-Level Customisation Cascade

The second new point, which makes the multi-level/fractal customisation idea concrete: **a single finance standard cascades into multiple customised versions, one per team or function.**

The project lead: **"even within the business function, you need the corporate skill in finance, then the group skill for technology, then the cybersecurity version of that skill, then maybe the ethics-response-team version. Multiple levels, and each skill needs to be customised, maintained, and kept in sync."**

| Level | The Version |
|-------|-------------|
| **Corporate finance** | The base finance skill (the standard) |
| **Group / technology** | The technology team's version of the finance skill |
| **Cybersecurity** | The cybersecurity team's version |
| **Ethics-response team** | The ethics-response team's version |

So one finance standard becomes a cascade of customised versions, each tailored to a team's context, each derived from the corporate base. This is the base-to-customised model (cross-ref: the base-vault and opinionated-expertise briefs) made concrete and multi-level: the corporate finance skill is the base; each team's version is a customisation; and crucially, they must all be **kept in sync** (when the corporate standard changes, every derived version must update). This is the fractal customisation idea (cross-ref: the vault-per-standard brief's intersection vaults and the base-vault brief): a base standard, with per-team customised derivatives, all kept in sync.

The keep-in-sync requirement is the hard part and the value: a static document cannot keep its per-team interpretations in sync (they drift, go stale, and diverge); a living skill cascade can, because the base is maintained and the derivations propagate (cross-ref: the ownership brief's amortise-maintenance-across-customers point and the knowledge-capture brief's living-not-static point).

## Why This Crystallises The Economic Model

The use case makes the economic model vivid, the model already stated in the ownership brief (cross-ref): **the maintenance is complicated, so you need a service and an owner, and the model works when paying the owner to maintain it is cheaper than doing it yourself.**

The project lead: **"the maintainability of this becomes complicated, and that's where you have a service and need an owner. People can do it themselves or be allowed to, but you touch it, you own it. So what's the economic model that makes paying the person to maintain it, ideally the subject-matter expert or the team, cheaper than doing it yourself? That's the fundamental model."**

Two things this sharpens:

- **"You touch it, you own it."** A crisp statement of the ownership principle (cross-ref: the ownership brief): if you customise a skill, you take on its maintenance. So a team can make their own version, but then they own keeping it in sync, which is real, ongoing work. This is why the service-and-owner model exists: most teams do not want to own the maintenance.
- **The fundamental model.** The economic model works when **paying the owner (the SME or the team that sets the standard) to maintain it is cheaper than doing it yourself.** For the finance cascade, this is clearly true: maintaining a 200-page document's per-team interpretations, kept in sync, across the whole company, is expensive and error-prone for each team to do; paying the finance team (or an SME, or a broker) to maintain the base and propagate the customised, in-sync versions is cheaper and better. This is the answer to "how much is it worth?" (cross-ref: the ownership brief): it is worth the cost a team would otherwise bear doing it themselves, badly.

So the finance use case is a clean proof of the economic model: multi-level, customised, must-be-kept-in-sync skills, where centralised, expert-owned maintenance is plainly cheaper and better than every team doing it themselves.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not new principles.** It grounds the existing knowledge-capture, ownership, and economic-model theses in a use case.
- **Not the 200-page document.** The skill is the distilled, applicable, per-team derivative.
- **Not a single flat skill.** A multi-level cascade of customised, in-sync versions.
- **Not finance-only.** Finance is the flagship; the pattern applies to every function.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Keep-in-sync is genuinely hard.** Propagating base changes to many customised versions is the hard part. Mitigation: version control and provenance (cross-ref: the base-vault and ownership briefs); the base-to-derivation propagation; the owner maintains it (the economic model).

**Risk 2: Finance knowledge is sensitive and high-stakes.** Errors in finance skills matter. Mitigation: the finance team owns and sets the standard; validation, evals, and provenance (cross-ref: the base-vault and marketplace briefs); the zero-knowledge vault; not financial advice, the team's own standard.

**Risk 3: Teams may resist the you-touch-it-you-own-it principle.** Owning maintenance is a real commitment. Mitigation: that is why the service-and-owner model exists, most teams will pay rather than own it; the economic model (cheaper than DIY) is the argument.

**Risk 4: Over-distilling could omit a needed corner case.** The per-team derivative might drop something that team needs. Mitigation: the base (the full standard) remains; the derivation is validated; the cascade keeps the relevant corner cases per team.
