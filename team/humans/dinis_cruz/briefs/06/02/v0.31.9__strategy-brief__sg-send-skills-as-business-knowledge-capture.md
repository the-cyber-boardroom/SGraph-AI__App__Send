## What This Is

A deeper articulation of why the skills work matters so much, the thesis underneath the base-vault, marketplace, and creator-economy briefs (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 skill-base-vaults, skills-marketplace, and skills-as-opinionated-expertise briefs). The argument: **skills are massively undersold, even by the companies building them, because their real power is not making an LLM more effective, it is capturing the knowledge of how a business actually works, the playbooks, the actions, the operational reality, in a way businesses have always struggled to do.** And the reason this is finally viable is that, unlike the static playbooks and runbooks of the past, skills can be living, maintained, and fed back, which is what makes capturing business knowledge economically sustainable for the first time.

This brief is the why; the base-vault, marketplace, and creator-economy briefs are the how. A companion brief covers the ownership and maintenance consequence (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 ownership-of-delegated-processes brief), which the project lead flagged as a key point worth its own document.

## The Thesis: Skills Capture Business Knowledge

The core point, and the reframe: **the real power of skills is capturing knowledge, not just assisting an LLM.**

The project lead: **"the power of skills is not just that they let an LLM be more effective, which in itself would be powerful. The real power is that you're capturing knowledge, capturing playbooks and actions and fundamentally how things work in a business, in a way that we always struggled with before."** So a skill is, at its most valuable, a captured unit of business knowledge: how a function works, what the playbook is, what actions to take, how things actually operate. That is a far bigger claim than "skills make LLMs better," and the project lead's view is that even the companies building skills are underselling them by framing them as LLM-assistance rather than knowledge-capture.

This connects to the opinionated-expertise framing (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 opinionated-expertise brief): a skill captures an expert's way of thinking. Here the point is generalised to the business: a skill captures how a business *function* works, its operational knowledge, not just an individual expert's perspective.

## Why Businesses Always Struggled To Capture This

The project lead set out, from experience, why capturing operational knowledge has always been hard, and it is the context that makes skills significant.

### Businesses Are Bad At Capturing How Functions Work

The project lead: **"every business I worked with was always very bad at capturing the knowledge of its functions, how they operate, how they interact, how they connect, the handovers, the schemas, the operationals."** This is a near-universal organisational failing: the real, operational knowledge of how a business runs, how functions interact, where the handovers are, how things actually connect, lives in people's heads and in practice, not in any durable, usable form. When people leave, when functions change, that knowledge is lost, and incidents follow.

The project lead: **"we had lots of incidents caused by businesses changing functions, things not happening, people leaving, not capturing information, companies not understanding what roles are doing, the handovers."** So the cost of not capturing operational knowledge is real and recurring: incidents, lost knowledge, broken handovers, roles nobody fully understands.

### Past Attempts Were Static And Dead

The project lead drew on the cybersecurity experience of playbooks and runbooks to explain why past attempts failed: **"in cybersecurity we used to have a lot of playbooks and runbooks. In a lot of cases those were static resources. Even if they got used, they were almost not the real thing, something to be consulted, and they didn't have feedback loops. It was a static version of what somebody thought is actually happening, and it was really bad at capturing all the details, the ephemeral little things, the inner knowledge."**

| Past Knowledge Capture (Playbooks, Runbooks) | The Problem |
|----------------------------------------------|-------------|
| **Static resources** | Written once, not living |
| **Consulted, not lived** | "Something to be consulted," not the real thing |
| **No feedback loops** | They did not learn or update from use |
| **A version of what someone *thought* was happening** | Not the actual operational reality |
| **Bad at the details** | Missed the ephemeral, inner, detailed knowledge that matters |

So the past artefacts were dead: a static, idealised, detail-poor snapshot of what someone imagined the process was, with no connection to how it actually ran and no mechanism to stay current. They were better than nothing, but they were not the living knowledge.

## The Economic-Viability Breakthrough

The crucial point about *why* businesses never sustained knowledge capture, and why it is different now: **capturing knowledge is not a one-off, and a static skill goes out of date fast, so it was never economically viable, until now.**

The project lead: **"the core reason businesses never captured skills is that this is not a one-off exercise, not a spectator sport, not something you write once and forget. That's why in the past it wasn't economically viable, because even if you wrote the skill, it would be out of date very quickly. It wasn't sustainable, which is why it didn't happen."**

This is the heart of the matter:

| Why It Failed Before | Why It Is Viable Now |
|----------------------|----------------------|
| Not a one-off; needs constant maintenance | Skills can be living, version-controlled, and fed back |
| A written skill went out of date fast | Feedback loops and maintenance keep it current |
| Not economically sustainable to maintain | The creator/broker model makes maintenance economically viable (cross-ref: the ownership brief) |
| So it did not happen | So it can now happen |

So the breakthrough is not that we can *write* a skill (you always could); it is that a skill can now be **living and maintained sustainably**, with feedback loops, version control, and an economic model for keeping it current (cross-ref: the base-vault and ownership briefs). That is what makes capturing how a business works finally achievable: not a dead snapshot, but a living, maintained, fed-back artefact. The vault is central to this (cross-ref: the base-vault brief): it provides the version control, the provenance, the feedback loops (cross-ref: the evidence and marketplace briefs), and the home for a living skill.

## The Example: The GDPR Erasure Workflow

The project lead gave a concrete illustration of the messy operational reality that real knowledge capture must handle: **rebuilding a GDPR erasure workflow, which was massive once automated, with much of it being loops to handle the operational reality that things get missed.**

The project lead: **"when we rebuilt an erasure workflow on GDPR, the workflow was massive because we automated most of it, and parts of it were just loops to deal with the fact that a request would be given to a team, then they would miss it, then something else would happen. It was just a mess."** The lesson: the real workflow is not the idealised one. The actual operational knowledge includes the failure modes, the handovers that get missed, the loops needed because a team will not action a request, the messy reality. A static playbook captures the idealised process; a living skill must capture the real one, including the loops that exist precisely because of how the organisation actually behaves. This is exactly the ephemeral, inner, detailed knowledge the static artefacts missed, and it is what makes a captured skill genuinely valuable: it encodes how things *really* work, failure modes and all.

## Why This Is Massive

Pulling it together, the significance of skills, properly understood:

- **They capture what businesses could never durably capture before**: how functions work, interact, connect, hand over, the operational reality.
- **They are living, not static**: feedback loops, version control, and maintenance keep them current, unlike dead playbooks.
- **They handle the messy reality**: the failure modes, the loops, the ephemeral inner knowledge, not just the idealised process.
- **They are finally economically viable**: the creator/broker maintenance model (cross-ref: the ownership and base-vault briefs) makes sustaining them sustainable.
- **The cost of not doing it is real**: incidents, lost knowledge, broken handovers, which businesses have always suffered.

This reframes the entire skills proposition (cross-ref: the base-vault, marketplace, and creator-economy briefs): the platform is not building a better way to assist LLMs; it is building the first economically viable way to capture, maintain, and operate the knowledge of how a business actually works. That is the massive, undersold opportunity, and it is why the skills work matters.

## What This Asks For

1. **Frame skills as business-knowledge capture** (how functions work, interact, connect, hand over), not just LLM assistance.
2. **Make skills living, not static** (feedback loops, version control, maintenance; the vault as the home).
3. **Capture the messy reality** (failure modes, loops, ephemeral inner knowledge, the real process not the idealised one).
4. **Lean on the economic-viability breakthrough** (sustainable maintenance via the creator/broker model; cross-ref: the ownership and base-vault briefs).
5. **Use the cost-of-not-doing-it argument** (incidents, lost knowledge, broken handovers) in the proposition.
6. **Position the platform accordingly** (the first economically viable way to capture and operate business knowledge).
7. **Connect to the companion ownership brief** (who maintains the living skill, and why that is the moment of value; cross-ref: the ownership brief).
8. **Produce the library page and example vault** (the page explains the knowledge-capture thesis; an example living skill capturing a real business function, like the GDPR erasure workflow, is the MVP).

Estimated effort: this is a strategic framing, not a new build; it reframes and deepens the existing skills work (the base vault, the marketplace, the creator economy). The example (a living skill capturing a real, messy business function) is the MVP, and it reuses the base-vault and feedback-loop machinery.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not just LLM assistance.** The real value is capturing business knowledge.
- **Not a static playbook.** Living, fed-back, maintained, unlike the dead artefacts of the past.
- **Not the idealised process.** It captures the real, messy operational reality, failure modes and all.
- **Not a one-off.** Sustained maintenance is the point (and the economic breakthrough).

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Capturing real operational knowledge is genuinely hard.** It is the thing businesses always failed at. Mitigation: the discovery and evidence loops (cross-ref: the GDPR-assessment and visualisation briefs) help extract it; the creator/broker maintains it; start with high-value functions.

**Risk 2: Maintenance is the make-or-break.** A skill that is not maintained becomes a dead playbook again. Mitigation: the economic-viability model (the creator/broker is paid to maintain; cross-ref: the ownership and base-vault briefs); version control and feedback loops; the vault as the living home.

**Risk 3: Sensitive operational knowledge.** How a business really works is sensitive. Mitigation: the zero-knowledge vault; private and customised versions (cross-ref: the base-vault and consulting briefs); controlled sharing.

**Risk 4: Over-claiming.** "Capture how a business works" is a big promise. Mitigation: be honest that it is hard and incremental; start with specific functions (the GDPR erasure example); the living-and-maintained model is the differentiator from past failures.
