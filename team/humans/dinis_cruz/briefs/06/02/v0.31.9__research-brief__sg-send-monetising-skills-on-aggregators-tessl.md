# Monetising Skills On Top Of Aggregators: The Tessl Case And The Menu Of Revenue Paths

**version** v0.31.9
**date** 2 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Strategy, Architect, @Dev
**type** Research brief

## What This Is

A research brief, prompted by a conversation with Tessl, exploring **how to monetise skills that live on a third-party aggregator, and the full menu of monetisation paths a vault layer could provide on top of such a platform.** Tessl is the worked example (the project lead is in conversation with them), but the pattern generalises to other skill registries and aggregators. It builds on the skills creator-economy, marketplace, ownership, and billing work (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 skill-base-vaults, skills-marketplace, and OpenRouter-broker briefs, and the v0.31.9 ownership-of-delegated-processes brief) and answers the project lead's explicit request to research the different ways monetisation could work.

The core question the project lead is asking: **can a vault layer take skills from an aggregator like Tessl, add value (a vault, customisation, maintenance, token handling), and create monetisation paths that reward the skill's creator?**

## What Tessl Is (The Aggregator)

The research picture, current as of this brief:

- Tessl (tessl.io) is a package manager and registry for agent skills, from the founder of Snyk, bringing package-manager discipline (install, version, update skills as dependencies) to agent skills, rather than being just a directory.
- Its registry indexes thousands of skills with automatic evaluations, quality scores, impact ratings, version histories, and author information; skills are installed via a CLI (tessl install) and can be published org-scoped or public, from the registry or any GitHub repo (pinned to exact commits).
- Through a partnership with Snyk, every public skill in the registry carries a Snyk security score, bringing package-manager trust signals to the skills ecosystem (the ToxicSkills research and an eight-category risk taxonomy underpin it).
- It is positioned as an Agent Enablement Platform: govern (install policies, ownership, access controls), measure (adoption, evals with before/after evidence), and connect/publish (works across Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot, Gemini; MCP-compatible).

So Tessl is an evaluated, secured, versioned registry and lifecycle manager for skills, with quality scores, security scores, and governance. It is an aggregator with a credible stamp of approval (the evals and the Snyk security score), which is exactly the property the project lead is interested in monetising on top of.

## The Question: Can You Sell Skills From An Aggregator?

The project lead's starting question, asked of Tessl directly: **can I sell skills that are on their platform?**

The project lead: **"the question I was asking them is, can I sell your skills? Can I sell skills they have on their platform? Can I go in, grab the data they publish there? Is there a licence restriction? A lot of them are public, so it's interesting to see if there's a licence restriction."** This is the first thing to establish, and it is a licensing question, not an assumption:

| To Check | Why |
|----------|-----|
| **The licence on each skill** | Many skills are public, but public does not mean freely resellable; each skill's licence governs what can be done |
| **The aggregator's terms** | Tessl's terms on grabbing and reselling registry data |
| **What is resellable vs reference-only** | Some may be reusable/sellable; others reference-only |

The honest position: this must be verified per skill and per platform, not assumed. The project lead flagged it as "interesting to see," which is the right stance, the opportunity depends on what the licences actually permit. The brief treats the licensing as a precondition to establish, not a settled fact. Where licences permit, the monetisation paths below apply; where they do not, the model still applies to skills the creator themselves brings or licenses.

## The Goal: Reward The Creator

The project lead was clear that the point is to reward the skill's creator: **"the logic is to think about how you reward the creator of the skill, how you create a financial model where the skills creator actually gets paid."** This is consistent with the creator-economy thesis (cross-ref: the base-vault and opinionated-expertise briefs): the creator is the one whose knowledge and expertise the skill captures, and they should be paid. The aggregator (Tessl) acts as a distribution channel or aggregator/collection point, and the vault layer adds value and creates the monetisation paths on top, with the creator rewarded. The research on agent-skill monetisation supports this framing: revenue splits on agent marketplaces typically run heavily in the creator's favour (commonly 70 to 85 percent to the creator), comparable to the app-store model.

## The Vault Layer On Top: Stamp Of Approval Plus Added Value

The model the project lead sketched: **take a skill from the aggregator, wrap it in a vault, add value, and let it be customised and sold, carrying the aggregator's stamp of approval.**

The project lead: **"we provide a vault with the skill, provide information, provide other things on top of that, and allow the vault to be customised by the vault owner or by a company or individual who then sells that skill. It comes with this stamp of approval on what happens there, because they maintain that. And this is where you think about the SLAs, the maintainability, who maintains what, how fast it can be maintained, the customisability, and making sure the final product actually works. That's what we deliver to the end customer."**

| Layer | Contribution |
|-------|--------------|
| **The aggregator (Tessl)** | The skill, the evals, the security score, the maintenance, the stamp of approval |
| **The vault** | Wraps the skill, adds information and other value (the base-vault contents; cross-ref: the base-vault brief) |
| **Customisation** | The vault owner, a company, or an individual customises it (cross-ref: the base-vault and opinionated-expertise briefs) |
| **The deliverable** | A working, customised, maintained product to the end customer |

The stamp of approval is the interesting part: because Tessl evaluates and security-scores skills (and maintains them), a vault built on a Tessl skill inherits that trust signal. This complements the vault's own provenance-grounded scoring (cross-ref: the marketplace brief): the aggregator's eval and security score, plus the vault's provenance and the creator's reputation, together make the deliverable trustworthy. And the SLA/maintainability/ownership questions the project lead raised (who maintains what, how fast) are exactly the ownership-of-delegated-processes point (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 ownership brief): the value is in the maintained, owned, working product, and the aggregator's maintenance plus the vault layer's customisation-and-maintenance combine to deliver it.

## The Menu Of Monetisation Paths

The project lead asked for research on the different ways to monetise on top of this. Drawing on the current agent-skill monetisation landscape and the platform's own capabilities, here is the menu:

| Path | How It Works | Source / Cross-ref |
|------|--------------|--------------------|
| **Customisation (and support)** | Sell a customised, supported version of the skill (the prime IP product) | The base-vault and opinionated-expertise briefs; the research's "buyers pay for convenience, updates, and support, not the markdown" |
| **Token monetisation** | The user uses a skill (on top of aggregator content) and pays for the tokens, or the tokens are covered/marked up | The project lead's explicit second path; the OpenRouter-broker billing substrate (cross-ref) |
| **Open-core / paid packs** | A baseline skill is open; charge for advanced packs (extended checklists, industry variants, scripts) | The research's open-core model; the base-vault's open-foundation-plus-commercial-versions |
| **Maintained ownership / SLA** | Sell the ongoing ownership, maintenance, and SLA of the delegated process | The v0.31.9 ownership brief; the research's "monetise the integration work and support" |
| **Install-and-wire engagements** | Package the skill into a delivery engagement (install, wire, train); day-rate or project revenue | The research's delivery-accelerator model; the Tavon channel (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 Tavon brief) |
| **Usage / per-outcome** | Charge per use or per measurable outcome of the skill | The research's usage-based and per-outcome models (e.g. per resolved task) |
| **Subscription / access** | Recurring access to a maintained skill or a set of skills | The research's subscription models; the maintained-relationship point (cross-ref: the opinionated-expertise brief) |
| **Marketplace distribution (revenue split)** | List on a marketplace; creator gets the majority split | The marketplace brief; the research's 70 to 85 percent creator splits |
| **Branded / certified versions** | Sell the branded or certified version of the skill | The opinionated-expertise brief |

The project lead named two explicitly, customisation and token monetisation, and asked for the fuller research, which the rest of the menu provides. The two named ones are worth drawing out:

- **Customisation**: the prime path (cross-ref: the base-vault and opinionated-expertise briefs), a customised, maintained, supported version is the IP product, and the research confirms buyers pay for convenience, updates, and support, not the raw skill.
- **Token monetisation**: the project lead: **"allow somebody to use the skill, and they pay for tokens, using a skill on top of something from Tessl. If I have a chat and want to use a particular skill, can the token cost be covered, or can you monetise the tokens on top of this?"** So a second path is to monetise the token usage of running the skill, either covering the tokens (bundled, cross-ref: the Vault App Store bundled-credits) or marking them up, brokered through the billing substrate (cross-ref: the OpenRouter-broker brief). This is a usage-based path that fits naturally because the billing substrate already handles keys, credit, and brokering.

## Aggregators Will Do Some Of This Themselves, And That Helps

A strategic note the project lead made: **the aggregators will do some of this monetisation themselves, and the more they do, the more the vault layer can leverage.** The project lead: **"others should be doing some of these themselves, and the more they do it, the more you can leverage that. There's a lot you can build now because they don't have it, but once you have this, it becomes a lot easier to do."** So there are two timing dynamics:

- **Now**: the aggregators do not yet have rich monetisation on top of their skills, so there is room to build the vault layer and the paths (a near-term opportunity).
- **Later**: as aggregators add their own monetisation (Tessl is already an enablement platform with governance and measurement), the vault layer can leverage that rather than compete, building on their evals, security scores, maintenance, and any monetisation they add.

So the relationship with aggregators is complementary, not competitive: the vault layer adds the customisation, the maintained ownership, the token handling, and the additional value, on top of the aggregator's evaluated, secured, maintained skills. The more the aggregator does (evals, security, maintenance, even monetisation), the stronger the foundation the vault layer builds on.

## What This Asks For

1. **Establish the licensing** (per skill and per platform; what is resellable; verify, do not assume) before monetising aggregator skills.
2. **Build the vault-layer-on-top model** (wrap an aggregator skill in a vault, add value, allow customisation, deliver a working maintained product, carrying the aggregator's stamp of approval).
3. **Reward the creator** (the financial model pays the skill's creator; the aggregator is the distribution/aggregation channel).
4. **Provide the menu of monetisation paths** (customisation, token monetisation, open-core, maintained ownership/SLA, install-and-wire, usage/per-outcome, subscription, marketplace split, branded/certified).
5. **Lead with customisation and token monetisation** (the two the project lead named; both already supported by the base-vault and billing work).
6. **Combine trust signals** (the aggregator's evals and security score plus the vault's provenance and the creator's reputation).
7. **Treat aggregators as complementary** (build what they lack now; leverage what they add later).
8. **Start with Tessl, then others** (the worked example; generalise the pattern).
9. **Produce the library page and example vault** (the page explains the monetisation paths; an example vault monetising an aggregator skill is the MVP).

Estimated effort: the monetisation paths reuse work already specified (the base-vault customisation, the OpenRouter-broker billing substrate, the marketplace, the ownership model); the new work is the licensing verification, the aggregator-to-vault wrapping, and the partner conversation with Tessl. The research provides the menu; the platform provides the machinery.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not an assumption that aggregator skills are freely resellable.** Licensing is verified per skill and platform.
- **Not a competitor to the aggregator.** Complementary: the vault layer adds value on top of the aggregator's evaluated, secured, maintained skills.
- **Not a single monetisation path.** A menu, led by customisation and token monetisation.
- **Not bypassing the creator.** The creator is rewarded; that is the point.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Licensing may not permit reselling aggregator skills.** Public does not mean resellable. Mitigation: verify per skill and platform (the project lead's own caution); where licences do not permit, apply the model to creator-brought or licensed skills; partner with the aggregator on terms.

**Risk 2: Dependence on a third-party aggregator.** Building on Tessl's registry and scores. Mitigation: the pattern generalises to other aggregators and to the platform's own marketplace; the vault layer is portable; treat aggregators as complementary channels.

**Risk 3: The creator must genuinely be rewarded.** A monetisation layer that does not pay creators undermines the ecosystem. Mitigation: the creator-reward model is the stated goal; the research's creator-favourable splits; transparency.

**Risk 4: Token monetisation must be transparent.** Marking up tokens needs care. Mitigation: the billing substrate handles it transparently (cross-ref: the OpenRouter-broker brief); cover or mark up with clarity; the budget discipline.
