# Someone Has To Own It: The Ownership And Maintenance Of Delegated LLM Processes

**version** v0.31.9
**date** 2 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Strategy, Architect, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief

---

## What This Is

A key point the project lead flagged as worth its own document: **every time a process is delegated, to an LLM or to anyone, someone has to own it, and that ownership and maintenance is the moment of value, the moment of handover, the reason people pay.** It is the companion to the knowledge-capture thesis (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 skills-as-business-knowledge-capture brief): if a skill captures how a business works, the question becomes who owns and maintains that captured, delegated process over time, and the answer (the creator or broker of the skill) is where the economic model lives.

This sharpens the customisation and creator-economy work (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 skill-base-vaults and skills-as-opinionated-expertise briefs) with a clear principle: customising a skill into a delegated LLM process is not the end, it is the start of an ownership-and-maintenance responsibility, and that responsibility is what is bought and sold.

## The Principle: Someone Has To Own A Delegated Process

The core principle: **every time you put an LLM to do something, or delegate a process at all, someone has to own it.**

The project lead: **"every time somebody puts an LLM to do something, every time there's a process that is not being delegated to somebody else, somebody has to own that."** Delegation does not remove the need for an owner; it relocates it. When a process is handed to an LLM (or to a team, or to a tool), responsibility for that process does not vanish, someone must own it: own its correctness, its upkeep, its behaviour, its consequences. This is true of any delegation, and it is especially true of LLM-delegated processes, which are powerful but not self-maintaining.

This is an easy thing to miss in the excitement of delegation: the assumption that once a skill captures the process and an LLM runs it, the work is done. The project lead's point is the opposite: that is the moment the ownership question begins.

## Customisation Is Not The End, It Is The Start Of Maintenance

The sharp consequence for the skills model: **customising a skill into a delegated LLM process raises a chain of ownership questions, and answering them is the real work.**

The project lead: **"let's say there's a skill that has all the information, and somebody says, I'll take that and customise it. But what does that customisation mean? If that customisation means passing it to an LLM, who's going to maintain it? What about the cost of that? What about the next versions when they come out? How does the maintainability work? How does the CI pipeline of all that work?"**

So a customised, LLM-delegated skill is not a finished artefact; it is an ongoing responsibility with a chain of questions:

| Question | Why It Matters |
|----------|----------------|
| **Who maintains it?** | A delegated process needs an owner; an unmaintained skill rots (cross-ref: the knowledge-capture brief's dead-playbook problem) |
| **What is the cost?** | Running an LLM process has ongoing cost (cross-ref: the budget discipline in the visualisation and GDPR briefs) |
| **What about next versions?** | When new model versions or skill updates come, the process must adapt |
| **How does maintainability work?** | The process must be sustainably maintainable, not a one-off |
| **How does the CI pipeline work?** | The delegated process needs testing, validation, and controlled release (cross-ref: the agent-controlled-websites CI pipeline) |

This directly extends the customisation dimensions (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 skill-comparison brief's personalisation/context/license/token dimensions and the opinionated-expertise brief's branded/certified/customised versions): customisation is not just tailoring the content; it is taking on the maintenance, cost, versioning, and CI of a living, delegated process. That is a much bigger commitment than "edit the skill," and it is where the real value and the real work sit.

## The Moment Of Handover Is Why They Pay

The commercial heart of the brief: **the point where someone takes on the ownership and maintenance of a delegated process is the moment of handover, and that is why they pay.**

The project lead: **"that's the moment of handover, that's why they pay. And then the question just becomes, how much is that worth?"** So the thing being bought is not the skill as a static artefact (that may be open; cross-ref: the opinionated-expertise brief's open base vault). The thing being bought is **the ownership and maintenance of the delegated process**: someone taking responsibility for keeping it correct, current, cost-managed, versioned, and validated over time. That is the valuable handover, and it is what commands a price.

This crystallises the creator-economy commercial model (cross-ref: the base-vault and opinionated-expertise briefs): the customised, maintained version is the IP-bearing, sold product precisely because it carries the ongoing ownership and maintenance. The price is for the assumed responsibility, not the bytes. And the question "how much is that worth?" is answerable in those terms: it is worth what it costs the buyer to own and maintain the process themselves (which they cannot do well), versus paying someone who can.

## The Best Owners Are The Creators And Brokers

The conclusion: **the best people to take on and maintain that responsibility are the creators or brokers of the skills.**

The project lead: **"the best person to maintain that, the best person to take that responsibility, are the creators or the brokers of those skills."** This follows directly: the creator (who captured the knowledge and built the skill) and the broker (who curates and distributes it) are best placed to own its maintenance, because they understand it deeply, they have the incentive (it is their product and their reputation), and they can amortise the maintenance across all their customers (maintain the base once, propagate to the customised versions; cross-ref: the base-vault brief's base-to-customised model).

| Owner | Why They Are Best Placed |
|-------|--------------------------|
| **The creator** | Captured the knowledge, built the skill, understands it deeply, has the reputation stake |
| **The broker** | Curates and distributes, can maintain across many customers, has the relationship |

This is the economic engine behind the knowledge-capture thesis (cross-ref: the knowledge-capture brief): the reason capturing business knowledge is now viable (where it was not before) is precisely that creators and brokers can own and maintain the living skills sustainably, as a paid responsibility, across many customers. The maintenance that made it uneconomical for a single business to do for itself becomes economical when a creator or broker does it for many. So the ownership principle is not just a caveat; it is the mechanism that makes the whole skills economy work.

## How This Fits The Ecosystem

This brief locates the value precisely within the ecosystem:

| Element | Role |
|---------|------|
| **The base vault** | The captured knowledge (open foundation; cross-ref: the base-vault brief) |
| **The customised version** | The delegated process, tailored, and the IP product (cross-ref: the opinionated-expertise brief) |
| **The ownership/maintenance** | The valuable handover; why they pay (this brief) |
| **The creator/broker** | The best owner; maintains across customers |
| **The marketplace** | Where the maintained, owned versions are sold and scored (cross-ref: the marketplace brief) |
| **The billing substrate** | The key/credit/billing service that prices and collects for it (cross-ref: the OpenRouter-broker brief) |
| **The CI pipeline** | How the delegated process is tested, validated, and released (cross-ref: the agent-controlled-websites brief) |

So the ownership principle ties the whole skills ecosystem together: it explains *what* is actually sold (assumed responsibility for a living, delegated process), *who* sells it best (creators and brokers), and *why* it is worth paying for (the buyer cannot own and maintain it well themselves). It is the answer to "what is the product, really?", the product is maintained ownership of a delegated process.

## What This Asks For

1. **Adopt the principle**: every delegated process (especially LLM-delegated) needs an owner; delegation relocates responsibility, it does not remove it.
2. **Treat customisation as the start of maintenance**: a customised, delegated skill carries maintenance, cost, versioning, and CI, not just tailored content.
3. **Frame the handover as the value**: the assumed ownership and maintenance is the moment of handover and why people pay.
4. **Make creators and brokers the owners**: they are best placed to maintain, with the incentive and the ability to amortise across customers.
5. **Price the responsibility, not the bytes**: "how much is it worth?" is answered by the cost of owning it oneself versus paying someone who can.
6. **Provide the maintenance machinery**: version control, CI pipelines, cost management, and validation for delegated processes (cross-ref: the agent-controlled-websites and base-vault briefs).
7. **Connect to the billing substrate**: the key/credit/billing service prices and collects for the maintained ownership (cross-ref: the OpenRouter-broker brief).
8. **Produce the library page and example vault** (the page explains the ownership principle; an example of a maintained, owned, delegated skill is the MVP).

Estimated effort: this is a strategic principle that sharpens the existing creator-economy and customisation work, not a new build. The machinery it relies on (version control, CI, billing, the base-to-customised model) is already specified across the briefs. The contribution is the clear principle and its commercial framing.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not delegation-removes-responsibility.** Delegation relocates ownership; someone must own it.
- **Not customisation-is-the-end.** Customisation into a delegated process is the start of maintenance.
- **Not selling static bytes.** The product is maintained ownership of a living process.
- **Not anyone can own it.** Creators and brokers are best placed; that is why they are the owners.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Buyers may not see why they are paying for maintenance.** The value (ongoing ownership) is less visible than a one-off artefact. Mitigation: frame the handover and the cost-of-owning-it-yourself clearly; the dead-playbook problem (cross-ref: the knowledge-capture brief) shows what happens without maintenance.

**Risk 2: Maintenance commitments must be honoured.** Selling ownership means committing to maintain. Mitigation: the creator/broker has the incentive (reputation, recurring revenue); the marketplace scoring reflects maintenance (cross-ref: the marketplace brief's update-cadence and reputation signals); version control proves it.

**Risk 3: Cost of maintaining LLM-delegated processes.** Ongoing LLM cost plus upkeep. Mitigation: the budget discipline and token optimisation (cross-ref: the visualisation and skill-comparison briefs); amortise across customers; the billing substrate prices it in (cross-ref: the OpenRouter-broker brief).

**Risk 4: Versioning churn.** New model versions can break delegated processes. Mitigation: the CI pipeline (test, validate, controlled release; cross-ref: the agent-controlled-websites brief); the creator/broker owns adapting to new versions; that is part of the paid responsibility.
