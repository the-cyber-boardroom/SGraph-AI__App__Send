## What This Is

A practical follow-up to the "webify/AI-fi your spreadsheets" use case (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 webify-spreadsheets brief), getting specific about **how the consultant actually works in practice: the setup they run, the steps they take with the customer, what they charge, and the safety rule that makes it work.** Where the previous brief was the concept and the tagline, this is the operational playbook, the concrete answer to "how do things physically work?" The scenario is a local consultant (the example given: someone in the Algarve, Portugal, already adding value to local companies) who wants to offer this as a new service.

The aim is to capture, concretely, the end-to-end delivery model, and the genuinely new contribution here is the **side-by-side rule**: a new solution must always run alongside the existing one, never as a full or continuous replacement, so the customer gains confidence and the work is de-risked.

## The Setup: The Consultant Runs Their Own Vault, With Dev And Prod

The starting point: **the consultant runs and manages their own vault (serverless or self-hosted), so they control production, and they keep a development environment separate from it.**

The project lead: **"you run your own vault servers, manage the vault yourself, either serverless or self-hosted, so you control the vault, and that's your production. You have a production vault, and you have development code."**

| Environment | Role |
|-------------|------|
| **Production vault** | The consultant runs and controls it (serverless or self-hosted); the live solution |
| **Development** | Where the solution is built and tried before it goes live |

This is the consultant operating the platform themselves (the self-managed delivery model; cross-ref: the OpenRouter-broker brief's managed-vs-self-managed and the open-evaluation deployment models), with a clean dev-to-prod separation (cross-ref: the agent-controlled-websites CI brief's dev-to-prod pipeline). The consultant owns their production environment, which matters for trust (they control the customer's solution) and for the side-by-side rule below (dev is where the customer tries it safely first).

## The Steps: How The Engagement Physically Runs

The project lead walked through the concrete sequence, which is the heart of this brief:

| Step | What Happens |
|------|--------------|
| **1. Ask for a spreadsheet** | The consultant goes to the customer: "give me a spreadsheet, or an example of one" |
| **2. The customer provides it** | The customer hands over the spreadsheet (or a representative example) |
| **3. Recreate value-adding workflows** | The consultant automates something, a better data-entry UI, a workflow that adds value |
| **4. Customer signs off the intent** | The customer confirms "this is what I want" before the build is finalised |
| **5. Wire up auth** | Figure out user authentication and authorization, for example Google OAuth (cross-ref: the authentication and connector-OAuth briefs) |
| **6. Try it on dev** | The users try the workflow on the development environment |
| **7. Users sign off** | The users sign off on the workflow, having tried it |
| **8. Go live** | The solution goes to production, running side by side (below) |

The project lead: **"you go to the customer and say, give me a spreadsheet. They give you a spreadsheet. You recreate workflows that add value, automate something, a better UI, a better way to enter data. You have the customer sign it off, this is what I want. You figure out user authentication and authorization, maybe Google OAuth. Then users sign off on the workflow, they try it on dev, then you try it live."**

Two sign-offs are built in, and that is deliberate: the customer signs off the *intent* (step 4, what they want), and the users sign off the *workflow* (step 7, having tried it on dev). This double sign-off is what makes the engagement safe and billable: nothing goes live that the customer has not asked for and the users have not tried. It is the confirm-not-assume discipline (cross-ref: the assessment briefs) applied to delivery.

## The Critical Rule: Side By Side, Never A Full Replacement

The genuinely new and most important contribution: **whatever is created must work well side by side with the existing solution, never as a full or continuous replacement.**

The project lead: **"a very important thing: in the beginning, whatever solution is created should work well side by side. It should never do a full replacement, always a side-by-side replacement, which is always the safest one, where you let the user gain confidence. Never do a continuous replacement. That's super critical."**

| Full / Continuous Replacement | Side-By-Side |
|-------------------------------|--------------|
| Rip out the old, switch to the new | Run the new alongside the old |
| The customer must trust it blind | The customer sees both, gains confidence |
| High risk if the new one is wrong | De-risked: the old one is still there |
| Threatening | Safe and reversible |

The side-by-side rule is the safety mechanism and the trust mechanism at once: the new solution runs next to the spreadsheet the business already relies on, so the customer can compare, verify, and gain confidence before depending on it, and nothing is lost if the new one needs fixing. This is consistent with the augment-not-replace philosophy (cross-ref: the webify-spreadsheets and consulting briefs): you are not ripping anything out, the existing thing keeps working while the better thing proves itself beside it. The project lead was emphatic that this is critical, and it is the operational expression of de-risking: **let the users gain confidence; never do a wholesale switch.**

## The Commercial Test: Is The Value Worth The Charge?

The project lead returned to the recurring commercial question (cross-ref: the ownership and webify-spreadsheets briefs), here in its simplest delivery form: **how much do you charge to justify the consultant's time, versus how much value there is for the customer, and if those are compatible, you have a business.**

The project lead: **"the only question then becomes, how much is it worth? How much do you charge that justifies the consultant's time, and how much value is there for the customer to pay for it? As long as those values are compatible, you have a business. Then you put a case study, and you do it better next time."**

| The Two Sides | Must Be Compatible |
|---------------|---------------------|
| **The consultant's time** | What they need to charge to make it worth doing |
| **The customer's value** | What the improvement is worth to the customer |

When the charge the consultant needs and the value the customer gets are compatible, there is a business. The model then compounds: **put a case study together, and do the next one better** (cross-ref: the industry-pages and library-as-shop-front briefs' case studies). Each engagement produces a case study that makes the next sale easier and the next delivery sharper. And the focus throughout is value, not technology: **"focus on how much value you can add to the customer."** How much AI is used, how it works under the hood, is secondary to the value delivered on the ground.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a full replacement.** Side by side, always; the existing thing keeps working.
- **Not a continuous switch-over.** No wholesale cut-over; the customer gains confidence first.
- **Not technology-led.** Value to the customer leads; the AI under the hood is secondary.
- **Not the concept brief.** This is the practical playbook; the webify-spreadsheets brief is the concept.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Side-by-side means running two things for a while.** Maintaining the old and the new together is overhead. Mitigation: it is the deliberate de-risking cost; the dev environment and the consultant-run vault support it; the side-by-side period ends when the customer has confidence.

**Risk 2: The consultant runs production (an operational responsibility).** Self-hosting or running serverless is a commitment. Mitigation: the managed-vs-self-managed flexibility (cross-ref: the OpenRouter-broker and deployment-models briefs); serverless lowers the burden; the consultant controls their environment, which is also the trust advantage.

**Risk 3: Auth and access to customer data.** Wiring OAuth and touching live customer data needs care. Mitigation: the connector-OAuth-data-broker model and user-browser auth (cross-ref: the connector and authentication briefs); least-privilege; the data stays governed.

**Risk 4: Pricing mismatch (value vs charge).** The two sides may not be compatible. Mitigation: the commercial test is explicit; scope to a genuinely painful, valuable spreadsheet; the case-study compounding improves the value story over time.
