## What This Is

A strategic document applying the skills creator-economy model to a real, named worked example: **Ivan Ristic, creator of ModSecurity and a world-recognised TLS/SSL expert, and how the vaults could carry his brand so his knowledge can be sold, customised, and maintained for the organisations that need it.** The general model (base vaults, customisation and maintenance as the product, token monetisation, the ownership principle) is already documented (cross-ref: the v0.31.7 skill-base-vaults and skills-as-opinionated-expertise briefs, and the v0.31.9 ownership-of-delegated-processes, monetising-on-aggregators, and finance-team briefs); this brief grounds it in a real expert and a real scenario, and adds three sharp, new contributions: **the concrete current painful path the model removes, the anonymisation path (the expert maintains without seeing the customer's confidential data), and the open-source-as-credibility argument.**

The framing here is the project lead's, drawn from a conversation with Ivan about how to monetise skills; the specifics of any arrangement are to be agreed with Ivan and are presented as the project lead's strategic proposal, not as commitments on Ivan's part.

## The Worked Example: Ivan Ristic And TLS

The example is real and well-chosen: **Ivan Ristic, creator of ModSecurity, a world-recognised TLS/SSL expert, whose deep, specialised knowledge organisations genuinely need.** The project lead: **"Ivan Ristic, the creator of ModSecurity, one of the world-recognised SSL and TLS experts. I'm talking to him about how to monetise skills, how to create a path between the person with the skills, in this case Ivan, and the person who needs those skills."** TLS customisation is a strong example because it is genuinely deep and specialised, few people have Ivan's depth, the stakes are high (security), and organisations regularly need it but cannot easily get it. It is exactly the kind of opinionated, hard-won expertise the skills model is meant to capture and reward (cross-ref: the opinionated-expertise and knowledge-capture briefs).

This also frames monetising skills as an open-source challenge: **how do you create a path between the person with the skills and the person who needs them**, when the knowledge is (and should be) open. That is the question the brief answers.

## The Sharp Point: The Current Painful Path

The first genuinely new contribution, and a vivid one: **the concrete, painful path an organisation needing TLS customisation has to take today.**

The project lead described it precisely: **"that organisation had to pay somebody to understand it, probably research, probably read Ivan's documents, then they had to apply it, figure it out, apply it to their own domain, and then distribute it internally to multiple users, even customers."**

| Step In The Current Painful Path | The Cost |
|----------------------------------|----------|
| **Pay somebody to understand it** | A person's time, who is not Ivan |
| **Research** | Time spent gathering the knowledge |
| **Read Ivan's documents** | The knowledge exists, but must be absorbed |
| **Figure it out** | Interpretation, with risk of getting it wrong |
| **Apply it to their own domain** | Customisation, done by a non-expert |
| **Distribute internally to many users/customers** | The same effort, multiplied across the org |

This is the cost the model removes, and stating it concretely is what makes the value tangible. Today, an organisation pays a non-expert to laboriously reconstruct (imperfectly) what Ivan already knows, then apply and distribute it themselves. The model replaces this with: buy Ivan's vault, get the customisation and the maintenance, done by (or backed by) the actual expert. This is the answer to "how much is it worth?" (cross-ref: the ownership brief) made concrete: it is worth the cost of the current painful path, the paid non-expert, the research, the imperfect interpretation, the per-team distribution, which is substantial and error-prone.

## The New Path: Anonymisation, So The Expert Maintains Without Seeing Confidential Data

The second genuinely new contribution, and a clever one: **the expert can maintain the customised skill without having to see the customer's confidential information, via anonymisation.**

The project lead: **"what is the pricing point to get Ivan to be the one that maintains that, including even anonymising stuff, the point where you don't have to share confidential information with Ivan? That could be one of the paths we can streamline."**

This solves a real obstacle to expert-maintained customisation: the customer's specific configuration, domains, and setup are often confidential, and they may not want to share them with an external expert, even Ivan. The anonymisation path resolves this:

| Without Anonymisation | With The Anonymisation Path |
|-----------------------|------------------------------|
| The customer must share confidential config with the expert | The customer's confidential information stays private |
| A barrier to expert-maintained customisation | The expert maintains the skill without seeing the sensitive specifics |
| Trust and confidentiality concerns | The vault's zero-knowledge model supports this |

So Ivan can maintain the customised TLS skill, and the customer keeps their confidential configuration private, the expertise flows without the confidential data flowing back. This fits the vault's zero-knowledge model naturally (cross-ref: the authentication and consulting briefs' private/zero-knowledge handling): the customer applies Ivan's maintained skill to their own (private) domain, and Ivan maintains the skill itself without needing the customer's secrets. This is a genuinely new path that makes expert-maintained customisation viable even for confidential, high-stakes domains like TLS, and the project lead flagged it as something the platform can streamline.

## The Open-Source-As-Credibility Argument

The third sharp contribution, and an important one for the whole model: **Ivan's materials should be open source, and the open-source nature is itself the credibility signal that distinguishes the real expert from the shallow imitator.**

The project lead gave three reasons Ivan's materials should be open source: **"Ivan's materials should be open source, because he wants to contribute, not just that, because he leverages other open source materials, he doesn't want to close the data, and also from a credibility point of view, how do you know the difference between Ivan and somebody who doesn't have that depth of knowledge? That comes from the open source nature of it."**

| Reason For Open Source | Why |
|------------------------|-----|
| **He wants to contribute** | The expert's intent (and reputation) is to contribute |
| **He leverages other open source** | He builds on open materials, so he does not want to close his own |
| **Credibility** | The open-source nature is how you tell the real expert from the imitator |

The credibility point is the sharp one, and it reframes the open-versus-commercial question (cross-ref: the opinionated-expertise brief's open-base-plus-commercial-versions): open source is not a giving-away of the value, it is the **credibility and provenance mechanism**. Anyone can claim TLS expertise; what distinguishes Ivan is that his materials are open, scrutinised, built-upon, and verifiably his, the depth is visible and provable precisely because it is open. So open-sourcing the base materials *builds* the brand and the credibility, rather than eroding the commercial value. This aligns with the platform's provenance-grounded model (cross-ref: the marketplace brief's provenance-over-popularity and the base-vault brief's reputation): the open base is the verifiable proof of expertise; the commercial value is in the customisation, the maintenance, and the brand built on that proven depth.

This also answers the "future of open source" question the project lead raised: the path between the expert and the people who need the knowledge is that the knowledge is open (for credibility and contribution), and the commercial model is the customisation, maintenance, and brand on top, exactly the open-base-plus-commercial-versions model (cross-ref: the opinionated-expertise brief), here validated by a real expert's own reasons for wanting to stay open.

## The Commercial Model For Ivan

Pulling it together, the commercial model for Ivan's expertise, applying the existing model to this real case:

| Element | For Ivan |
|---------|----------|
| **The open base** | Ivan's TLS materials, open source (credibility, contribution, provenance) |
| **The branded vault** | A vault carrying Ivan's brand, selling his knowledge (cross-ref: the opinionated-expertise brief's branded version) |
| **Customisation** | The customer buys the customisability of the vault (Ivan's knowledge applied to their domain) |
| **Maintenance** | The customer buys the maintenance (Ivan maintains it; cross-ref: the ownership brief) |
| **Anonymisation** | Ivan maintains it without seeing the customer's confidential data (the new path) |
| **Token monetisation** | Customers bring their own token or pay for tokens to apply and customise it in the environment, a revenue stream (cross-ref: the monetising-on-aggregators and OpenRouter-broker briefs) |

The project lead: **"let's map out how our vaults can be used with Ivan's brand, so he can sell Ivan's knowledge, people can pay for it, buy a vault, buy the customisability and the maintenance. And one way to monetise is when people in the environment bring their own token or pay for tokens to apply and customise it, that's a revenue stream."** So the model is the branded base vault (open for credibility), with revenue from the customisation, the maintenance (expert-maintained, with anonymisation), and the token usage. This is the creator-economy and monetisation model (cross-ref: the base-vault, opinionated-expertise, and monetising-on-aggregators briefs) applied to a real, named, world-class expert, which is the best possible test of whether it works.

## Why This Is The Right Test

Ivan is an ideal first real case for the model:

- **Genuine, scarce, deep expertise** (TLS/SSL, ModSecurity), the kind the model is meant to reward.
- **A real brand and credibility** (world-recognised), which the branded-vault model needs.
- **An open-source ethos** (he wants to stay open), which validates the open-base-plus-commercial-versions model and the credibility argument.
- **A real customer need** (organisations needing TLS customisation, on the painful current path), which proves the demand.
- **A confidentiality obstacle** (customers' configs are sensitive), which the anonymisation path solves.

So if the model works for Ivan, it works: it has a real expert, a real brand, a real need, a real obstacle (solved by anonymisation), and a real open-source rationale. Making this commercially work is the practical proof of the whole skills creator-economy thesis (cross-ref: the knowledge-capture, ownership, and base-vault briefs).

## What This Asks For

1. **Build the branded expert vault for Ivan** (his TLS knowledge, his brand, sold to organisations that need it).
2. **Keep the base materials open** (for credibility, contribution, and provenance; the open-source-as-credibility argument).
3. **Sell the customisation and the maintenance** (Ivan's knowledge applied and maintained for the customer's domain).
4. **Build the anonymisation path** (Ivan maintains without seeing the customer's confidential data; the zero-knowledge model).
5. **Add token monetisation** (customers bring or pay for tokens to apply and customise; a revenue stream).
6. **Replace the current painful path** (buy the vault instead of paying a non-expert to research, interpret, apply, and distribute).
7. **Use Ivan as the flagship real test** (a genuine expert, brand, need, obstacle, and open-source rationale).
8. **Produce the library page and example vault** (the page explains the expert-brand-vault model; Ivan's TLS vault is the MVP, subject to agreement with Ivan).

Estimated effort: low to moderate, mostly applying the existing model (base vault, branded version, customisation, maintenance, token monetisation) to a real case; the genuinely new build is the anonymisation path (expert maintains without seeing confidential data). The MVP is Ivan's branded, open-base, customisable, maintained TLS vault, agreed with Ivan.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not closing Ivan's knowledge.** The base is open (credibility, contribution); the commercial value is customisation, maintenance, and brand.
- **Not requiring the customer to share confidential data.** The anonymisation path keeps it private.
- **Not a generic skill.** It is a named, world-class expert's brand and depth.
- **Not a presumption of Ivan's commitment.** The arrangement is the project lead's proposal, to be agreed with Ivan.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: It depends on agreement with Ivan.** The model presumes Ivan participates. Mitigation: this is a proposal from a real conversation; the terms are to be agreed; the model is presented as the project lead's strategic framing.

**Risk 2: The anonymisation path is the new, harder build.** Maintaining a skill without seeing the customer's confidential data is non-trivial. Mitigation: the zero-knowledge vault supports it; the customer applies the maintained skill to their private domain; design and validate the anonymisation carefully.

**Risk 3: High-stakes domain (TLS security).** Errors in TLS guidance matter. Mitigation: the expert (Ivan) owns and maintains it; the open base is scrutinised; validation and provenance (cross-ref: the base-vault and marketplace briefs); not a substitute for the customer's own security responsibility.

**Risk 4: Proving open-source-does-not-erode-value.** The open base must be shown to build, not undercut, the commercial value. Mitigation: the credibility argument (open is the provenance); the commercial value is customisation, maintenance, and brand, not the raw materials; the research confirms buyers pay for convenience, updates, and support (cross-ref: the monetising-on-aggregators brief).
