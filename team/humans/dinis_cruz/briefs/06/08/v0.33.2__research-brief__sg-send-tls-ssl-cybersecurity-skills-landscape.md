# Research Brief: The TLS And SSL Cybersecurity Skills Landscape

**version** v0.33.2
**date** 8 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Research, Strategy, Security, @Dev
**type** Research brief

## What This Is

A brief scoping a focused piece of research: **catalogue and analyse every published skill on TLS, SSL, and HTTPS, capturing where they live, their licences, what is inside them (including executable code and its threat implications), how they are version controlled and provenanced, what should become a skill but has not, how they are customised, and what commercial models exist, then place it all on a Wardley map.** It narrows the broader skills work (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 skills series) to one specific, expert domain, and connects to the OWASP expert skills, the TLS expert-brand vault, and the skills-monetisation work (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 owasp-expert-skills, v0.31.9 expert-brand-vaults-ivan-ristic-tls, and v0.31.9 monetising-skills-tessl briefs). This brief defines the research; the research itself can be run as a follow-up.

## The Focus: TLS, SSL, And HTTPS Skills Specifically

The project lead is deliberately narrow: **this is not skills in general, it is specifically TLS, SSL, and HTTPS, the use of SSL encryption.**

The project lead: **"I want this to be very specific on TLS slash SSL, the whole HTTPS, the use of SSL encryption. We talked about skills before, but I want this very specific."** So the research is scoped tightly to one expert cybersecurity domain (TLS and SSL), which makes the catalogue tractable and the conclusions sharp, and which aligns with the existing TLS-expert direction (cross-ref: the expert-brand-vaults-ivan-ristic-tls brief). The narrow focus is the point: do this one domain thoroughly, as a model for others.

## Research Task 1: Catalogue Every Published TLS And SSL Skill

The first task: **find every skill published on TLS and SSL, including those inside skill packs, and establish the lay of the land.**

The project lead: **"first, catalogue, see if you can find every single skill published on this, including skills that are part of the skills packs. Anthropic and others have published security skills, so I am assuming there are some on TLS and SSL. Look at Tessl and others that have done similar things. Figure out the lay of the land and what exists."** So the catalogue spans skills published by Anthropic, Tessl (the skills platform from the monetisation work; cross-ref: the monetising-skills-tessl brief), OWASP (likely; below), and others, including skills bundled inside larger packs. The output is a map of what TLS and SSL skills exist today and where.

## Research Task 2: For Each, Capture Licence, Platform, And Workflow

For every skill found: **record its licence, platform, hosting, availability, and the workflow to use it.**

The project lead: **"for each, I would like to see the licence, the platform they are in, where they are hosted, how they are made available, the licence they have been published under, and the workflow to use them."**

| Dimension | What To Record |
|-----------|----------------|
| Licence | The publishing licence |
| Platform | Where it lives |
| Hosting | Where it is hosted |
| Availability | How it is made available |
| Usage workflow | How you actually use it |

## Research Task 3: Look Inside, And Assess The Threat

A crucial, security-minded task: **look inside the skills, because they are not just text files, some contain executable code, and assess how dangerous they could be.**

The project lead: **"look at what is inside them, because there is a misunderstanding that skills are just text files. First, they are code, because they tell the model what to do. But some skills even have executable code, so from a threat point of view, see how dangerous these skills could be."** So the research opens the skills and assesses them as a threat surface: a skill is already instructions that direct a model (code in that sense), and some carry actual executable code (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 skills-are-software-packages brief). The question is how dangerous a given skill could be, which matters because skills are adopted and run (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 skills-and-permissions and the accountability briefs).

## Research Task 4: Version Control And Per-Skill Provenance

Examine how they are versioned: **are they version controlled, and individually or only as part of a big pack, which obscures the provenance of each?**

The project lead: **"are they version controlled, and version controlled individually, or version controlled as part of a big pack, so you have to bring the big pack and it is hard to see the provenance of each of them?"** So the research records whether each skill has its own version history and provenance, or is only versioned inside a larger pack (which forces you to take the whole pack and makes per-skill provenance hard to trace; cross-ref: the v0.32.7 deterministic-value-indexes and PKI-registry briefs on provenance).

## Research Task 5: The Packs, And Author And Source Provenance

For the packs: **what else is included, are there usage instructions and customisation paths, and crucially, what is the provenance of the author and the source materials?**

The project lead: **"when you look at the packs, what else is included? Do you have instructions on how to use it? Ways to customise it? And more importantly, the provenance of the author and the sources of the materials, because it is important to track back and really understand why they say things."** So for each pack the research records the contents, the usage instructions, the customisation paths, and the author and source provenance, so a claim in a skill can be traced back to who wrote it and what it is based on. Provenance is the trust question for skills.

## Research Task 6: Resources That Should Become Skills

A valuable side output: **map the security resources that should be converted into skills but have not, such as OWASP cheat sheets and other open-licensed guidance, books, and materials.**

The project lead: **"a good side effect: map out what resources exist today that really should be converted into a skill, like the most obvious ones, the OWASP cheat sheets, and a lot of other security guidance, books, and materials, especially ones released under Creative Commons and open licences."** So alongside cataloguing existing skills, the research identifies high-value security knowledge not yet packaged as skills: OWASP cheat sheets (cross-ref: the owasp-expert-skills brief), and other open and Creative-Commons-licensed guidance, books, and materials. This is a pipeline of skills waiting to be created.

## Research Task 7: The Open-Source And Customisation Mapping

Map the open-source mechanics: **how are these skills used, versioned, updated, and, most importantly, how are they customised?**

The project lead: **"map it to the open source: how are they actually used, how do people version it, how do people update it, and more importantly, how are they customizable?"** So the research maps the open-source lifecycle of these skills (use, versioning, updating) with particular attention to customisation, because customisation is where adoption and ownership happen (below; cross-ref: the v0.32.3 skill-as-projection brief's forking and the open-source-not-free brief).

## Research Task 8: Commercial And Customisation Models

The commercial question, and the thesis behind it: **is anyone already offering customised services or commercial models around these skills, and what are their pricing and business models, given that adopting a skill costs money because you change it, touch it, and own it?**

The project lead: **"are there any customisation, security consulting, or commercial models connected to those skills? The idea is that it costs money to adopt a skill: you change it, you touch it, you own it. Is anybody out there already providing customised services? What is the pricing model, the business model?"** So the research looks for existing commercial activity around TLS and SSL skills (consulting, customisation services, pricing, business models), grounded in the thesis that adopting a skill is not free: customising and owning it has a cost, which is a business opportunity (cross-ref: the consulting-firms-skills-service-to-product, monetising-skills-tessl, and owasp-expert-skills briefs).

## Research Task 9: The Wardley Map

The synthesis: **place it all on a Wardley map, what is commodity and what is not, what is manual and what is automated, and how it all fits together.**

The project lead: **"finish by mapping what a Wardley map of skills would look like, the manual bits, the automated bits. It is a good target for a Wardley map: what is commodity, what is not, because even if we commoditize the skill, which is important, there is a lot of other stuff that stays manual, and it is important to map how everything fits together."** So the research concludes with a Wardley map of the TLS and SSL skills landscape (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 skill-lifecycle-explorer-to-town-planner and v0.32.7 tool-replacement-strategy briefs): which parts are commoditized (the skill itself) and which remain manual and custom (the expertise, the customisation, the consulting), and how they fit together. Commoditizing the skill is important, but the map shows where the manual, higher-value work still lives.

## What This Asks For (The Research Deliverable)

1. **Catalogue every TLS and SSL skill** (Anthropic, Tessl, OWASP, others; including those in packs; the lay of the land).
2. **Record licence, platform, hosting, availability, and usage workflow** for each.
3. **Look inside and assess the threat** (skills are not just text; some have executable code; how dangerous).
4. **Record version control and per-skill provenance** (individual versus pack-only).
5. **Examine the packs** (contents, instructions, customisation, author and source provenance).
6. **Map resources that should become skills** (OWASP cheat sheets; open and CC-licensed guidance, books, materials).
7. **Map the open-source and customisation mechanics** (use, versioning, updating, customisation).
8. **Find the commercial and customisation models** (consulting, pricing, business models; the cost-to-adopt thesis).
9. **Produce a Wardley map** (commodity versus not, manual versus automated, how it fits together).

Estimated effort: a research task; this brief defines the scope and questions, and the research can be executed as a follow-up (web research across skill platforms, repositories, and security publishers). The deliverable is a catalogue plus analysis across the dimensions above, ending in a Wardley map.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not general skills research.** Specifically TLS, SSL, and HTTPS.
- **Not surface-level.** Look inside the skills, including their code and threat surface.
- **Not pack-only provenance.** Trace per-skill version control, author, and sources.
- **Not just a catalogue.** It maps what should become skills, how they are customised, and the commercial models.
- **Not the executed research.** This brief scopes it; the research follows.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: The catalogue may be incomplete.** Skills are scattered across platforms and packs. Mitigation: search platforms, repositories, and publishers; record gaps; iterate.

**Risk 2: Some skills cannot be inspected.** Closed or pack-only skills may resist looking inside. Mitigation: record what is inspectable; note what is not; assess threat where possible.

**Risk 3: Provenance is often missing.** Many skills lack clear author and source provenance. Mitigation: that absence is itself a finding; record it; it supports the provenance and trust thesis.

**Risk 4: The threat assessment must be careful.** Skills with executable code are a real surface. Mitigation: assess, do not execute untrusted skills; treat as a threat-modelling exercise (cross-ref: the security briefs).

**Risk 5: Commercial-model data may be thin.** Pricing and business models are often opaque. Mitigation: record what is public; note the gap as an opportunity.
