## The Core Idea

A vault is an encrypted container for data. The defining property is **zero-knowledge**: data is encrypted on the client before it is uploaded, so SG/Send itself never sees the plaintext. The server stores only ciphertext. This is not a compliance veneer; it is a mathematical guarantee, and it is the thing to protect and reason about in all of this work.

## The Vault Technology, In Depth

**Zero-knowledge, client-side encryption.** The device that creates, edits, or reads the data is the only place the data is ever in the clear. The content is encrypted client-side (a passphrase or key-derived scheme), the ciphertext is uploaded, the key or passphrase travels out of band, and the recipient's browser decrypts locally. Plaintext never reaches the server, and there are no server-side keys to steal.

**PKI: confidentiality, integrity, attribution.** Public-key cryptography is used so that data can be encrypted to a recipient's public key (only they can read it) and signed with a sender's private key (only they could have sent it). Together this gives confidentiality, integrity, and attribution. A repeated principle in this work is **PKI, not secrets**: authorization comes from being able to decrypt, not from shared passwords or tokens.

**Sovereignty from the keys, not the data.** Because data is only ever clear at its endpoints, where it is stored matters far less than who holds the key. You get sovereignty by protecting the keys, not by controlling the storage location. The server, the cloud, and every intermediary are blind, which also means system admins, database admins, and DevOps cannot read the data.

**Version control and provenance (SGit).** Every change is stored as a signed, chained version, so you can always answer who changed what and when, and data cannot be altered silently. A repeated theme is **the commit log as the audit log** and **integrity over confidentiality** (silent corruption is often worse than deletion).

**Key custody.** Who physically holds the keys is a design choice. In the healthcare context there are three modes: the patient holds the key, both patient and clinician hold it, or the clinician holds it. Many small keys are preferred to one big one, so no single failure is catastrophic.

**Sharing by decryption.** To share, you encrypt to the recipient's public key. One vault can hold data that many different people can each open only the parts they are entitled to.

**Vault features.** Friendly human-readable share tokens (for example "gleam-flint-6001" rather than a long URL), a browsable folder view and a thumbnail gallery view in the browser, view and download tracking, and vault-to-vault communication (vaults can send data to each other).

**Vault-in-vault and mini-apps.** Vaults can be nested, and small applications (mini-apps) and even full HTML apps can run from inside a vault, rendered from an index.html with a window.sg API. There are skills for authoring vault content and vault HTML apps.

**The S3-compatible vault container.** A Docker container for SG/Compute presents an S3-compatible API, a digital twin of S3, so that any code using boto3 or the AWS CLI believes it is talking to real S3 while the files actually come from a vault, from local disk, or from memory, as a swappable backend, with no change to the calling code. This makes the vault a drop-in storage backend for the entire S3 ecosystem.

**Open source, no lock-in, run anywhere.** The whole stack is open source. This is framed as essential to real sovereignty (a system is not sovereign if a third party controls the means to access its data) and it lets the solution run anywhere, including offline.

**Agentic-friendly.** Vaults can be used by AI under the same key controls: decrypt client-side, let a private LLM or Claude or ChatGPT process the content, and the server stays blind. Agents can also sit in the vault's web interface so a user can talk to their data.

## The Data-Protection Pattern

The above is crystallised into a pattern that any data-protection solution should follow, presented to medical audiences with analogies (encryption as a blinded clinical trial; PKI as a sealed-and-signed controlled-drugs prescription; client-side encryption as a sealed specimen carried by blind couriers; sovereignty-from-keys as a safe-deposit box; version control as a controlled-drugs register). The ten principles: encrypt on the client; use public-key cryptography for confidentiality, integrity, and attribution; derive sovereignty from key custody; keep intermediaries blind; version every change with provenance; support patient, shared, or clinician key custody; authorize by decryption; keep keys few and well managed; be open source with no lock-in; and be ready for agents. See the description paper `v0.33.34__arch-brief__sg-send-data-protection-pattern-medical-...` and its infographic guidance.

## The Healthcare MVP, In Depth

The healthcare use case is the leading application of the vault technology.

**The original case study.** A patient assembles their medical records (scans, reports, notes, a timeline) and shares them with a doctor as a single encrypted vault link plus the decryption key. Zero-knowledge means even SG/Send cannot read them. This replaces USB sticks, password-protected zips, and CDs, and an LLM can help structure the records client-side before sharing (for example, building a timeline from scattered notes).

**The patient-doctor vault workflow (the MVP).** The demonstrated flow: the clinician creates the patient's vault, sends the patient a link, the patient fills in their data, the vaults then talk to each other so the data moves securely from one vault to another, and the clinician has an admin view to receive, consolidate, and analyse it. The patient keeps access to their own vault, and never has to see or understand the vault machinery; it is created for them. Self-provisioning (the patient creates their own vault) is also supported.

**Key custody in healthcare.** Patient-held, shared, or clinician-held, as above. This is the main design decision per deployment.

**The medical-partner presentation.** There is a 50-minute presentation for a medical partner, framed deliberately as the pattern and principles (not a product pitch), for a non-technical audience, carried by medical analogies, and intended also as a LinkedIn article with a set of infographics. The principles checklist doubles as a way for a medical organisation to judge any solution put in front of it.

**Agentic-friendly in healthcare.** Vaults can be accessed by private LLMs or Claude or ChatGPT under the same key control, and an agent can help the patient maintain their data.

## Risk Mandate.ai, The Broader Context

You may be asked about the wider product. Risk Mandate.ai is an agent-mandate and risk-acceptance solution. Its mission is behaviour change through risk awareness: surface risk so clearly that the business funds the fix and the act of understanding the risk drives better behaviour. Key elements you should know:

- **The risk-acceptance workflow.** There is no deny button. A risk can only be accepted (in a direction: get more data, reduce, increase, or hold) for a chosen interval (one hour, four hours, two days, two weeks, six months), after which it must be revisited. An executive never accepts a material risk alone; it must be underwritten by the relevant owner, the security function, and GRC, and the acceptance propagates up to the CEO and sometimes the board. Approval attaches to a risk profile, so repeat instances are pre-approved as an FYI until the profile changes. The only escape from an impossible risk is the nuclear option: cease the activity.
- **The agent-mandate graph.** A mandate is a delegation. Risks, actors, assets, and harms are modelled as a navigable graph, which lets you compute blast radius (what an agent or breach can reach) and answer who is carrying which risk on what basis.
- **RiskMandate reviews.** Worked, evidence-backed reviews of real platforms through the risk lens. A completed example reviews Calendly, with and without its AI agent, and the stakeholder-by-stakeholder risks to accept.
- **The personal scenario.** The first concrete scenario walks an individual running a local LLM (such as Claude on their desktop) through a curated question graph that builds their personal risk graph, including the lethal trifecta (an agent that reads untrusted content, reaches the internet, and can act on its own implies data can be exfiltrated, corrupted, or destroyed).
