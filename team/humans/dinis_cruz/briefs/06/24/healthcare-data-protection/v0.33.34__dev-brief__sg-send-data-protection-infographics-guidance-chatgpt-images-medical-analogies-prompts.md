# Infographic Guidance: The Medical Data-Protection Talk, for ChatGPT Images 2

**version** v0.33.34
**date** 24 June 2026
**type** Dev brief (infographic production guidance)

## What This Is

Production guidance for the presentation infographics, built with ChatGPT Images 2: **a consistent visual set that explains each concept from the data-protection description paper for a non-technical medical audience, sharing one visual style and one cast of icons, carrying the medical analogies, and giving a ready-to-paste prompt for each concept, organised in two passes, a section-level hero image per concept and smaller infographics for individual ideas; it includes a reusable style preamble to keep every image consistent, and practical advice for getting clean results from the image model.** It is the companion to the description paper (cross-ref: the v0.33.34 data-protection-pattern brief). New contributions: **the shared style, the per-concept prompts, and the model-handling tips.**

## How To Use This

Generate one concept per image. For each infographic below, paste the **style preamble** followed by that infographic's **prompt** into ChatGPT Images 2. Generate, review the labels, and regenerate if any text is garbled. Where exact wording matters, generate the visual clean and add the precise text yourself in the slide tool. Keep the same preamble across all images so the set looks like one family.

## The Shared Style Preamble

Prepend this to every prompt:

> STYLE: a clean, modern, flat-vector infographic, friendly and professional, designed for a non-technical medical audience. Calm palette of soft teal and blue with a single warm coral accent, on a white background with generous whitespace. Simple, consistent icons. A few short labels only, in large readable sans-serif, in clear correct English with no gibberish or invented words. Wide 16:9 slide format. Keep it uncluttered.

Keep a consistent cast of icons across the set: a **patient** (friendly person), a **clinician** (person with a stethoscope), a **record** (a simple document or health card), a **public key** drawn as a light outline key, a **private key** drawn as a solid dark key, a **padlock**, a **vault or safe**, and **intermediary systems** (servers or a cloud) drawn in muted grey to signal they are blind.

## Practical Tips For The Image Model

- **Text is the weak point.** The model renders a few short words reasonably but mangles long or precise text. Keep labels to two to four words, and plan to overlay exact wording in your slide tool when it matters.
- **One idea per image.** Do not cram multiple concepts into one picture; the set is meant to be many simple images.
- **Reuse the preamble** verbatim for visual consistency, and describe the patient and clinician the same way each time so they look like the same characters.
- **Ask for whitespace and a light background** so the image stays legible projected on a slide.
- **Regenerate freely.** If labels come out wrong, regenerate or remove the text and add it yourself.
- **Generate variants.** Consider a 16:9 version for slides and a square or 4:5 version for LinkedIn.

## The Infographics

### 1. What Encryption Is
**Concept**: readable data is scrambled, then restored with a key. **Analogy**: a blinded clinical trial, revealed only by the unblinding key.
> PROMPT: Three panels left to right. Panel one: a clear, readable health record. Panel two: the same record scrambled into unreadable blocks, with a padlock. Panel three: the record clear and readable again, beside a key unlocking it. Small labels: "Readable", "Scrambled", "Readable again". A small side note icon of a blinded trial vial coded with a label. Title: "Encryption".

### 2. Symmetric vs Asymmetric Keys
**Concept**: one shared key versus a public and private pair. **Analogy**: a shared drug-cabinet key versus a specimen drop-box.
> PROMPT: Two halves. Left half labelled "Same key both sides": two people each holding an identical key, a drug cabinet between them. Right half labelled "A key pair": a light outline "public" key and a solid dark "private" key, with a specimen drop-box where anyone can post through a public slot but only the private key opens it. Title: "Two kinds of keys".

### 3. PKI: Sealed and Signed
**Concept**: encrypt to the recipient and sign as the sender, giving confidentiality, integrity, and attribution. **Analogy**: a controlled-drugs prescription, sealed and signed.
> PROMPT: A medical envelope or prescription in the centre. A wax seal on it formed from a light "public key" padlock, labelled "Sealed: only the recipient can open". A signature stamp on it from a solid "private key", labelled "Signed: only the sender could send it". Three small badges below: "Confidential", "Unaltered", "Attributable". Title: "Sealed and signed".

### 4. Client-Side Encryption
**Concept**: only the endpoint devices see clear text; everything in the middle is scrambled. **Analogy**: a sealed pathology specimen carried by blind couriers.
> PROMPT: A horizontal flow. On the far left a clinician's laptop showing a clear record. On the far right a patient's phone showing a clear record. In between, a chain of muted-grey servers and a cloud, each showing only scrambled blocks and a padlock. Labels: "Clear here", "Scrambled in the middle", "Clear here". A small icon of a sealed specimen tube travelling along the chain. Title: "Only the ends can read it".

### 5. Sovereignty From the Keys, Not the Data
**Concept**: where data is stored matters less than who holds the key. **Analogy**: a safe-deposit box; the bank stores it, you hold the key.
> PROMPT: Several different storage locations across the image, a data centre, a cloud, an overseas server, each holding an identical locked scrambled box. A single person on the right calmly holding one key, with a glowing line connecting the key to all the boxes. Labels: "Stored anywhere", "One key holder". Title: "Sovereignty comes from the keys".

### 6. Everyone in the Middle Is Blind
**Concept**: admins, DBAs, and DevOps cannot read the data. **Analogy**: hospital porters and filing clerks carrying sealed records they cannot read.
> PROMPT: A row of IT staff figures labelled simply "Admin", "Database", "DevOps", each shrugging beside a sealed, scrambled record they cannot open, shown with small crossed-out eye icons. On the right, one key-holder who can read the clear record. Title: "Administrators are blind to the data".

### 7. Version Control and Provenance
**Concept**: every change is a signed, chained version; who changed what, when. **Analogy**: a controlled-drugs register or tamper-evident lab notebook.
> PROMPT: A vertical chain of record cards, each linked to the one above by a small chain link, each card stamped with a tiny signature and a timestamp and a person icon. A muted attempt to alter an earlier card is shown breaking the chain with a warning mark. Labels: "Who", "What", "When". Title: "Every change is signed and linked".

### 8. Who Holds the Keys: Three Modes
**Concept**: patient-held, shared, or clinician-held key custody. **Analogy**: who holds the key to the patient's records.
> PROMPT: Three equal panels. Panel one: a patient holding the key to a vault, a small share arrow to a clinician, labelled "Patient holds the key". Panel two: a patient and a clinician each holding a copy of the key to the same vault, labelled "Both hold the key". Panel three: a clinician holding the key, having created the patient's vault, labelled "Clinician holds the key". Title: "Three modes of key custody".

### 9. Sharing by Decryption, Not Secrets
**Concept**: one vault, many readers, each opening only their slice with their own key. **Analogy**: addressing a sealed sample to a named specialist.
> PROMPT: A central vault holding several differently coloured sealed items. Around it, four people, a secretary, two different specialists, and a GP, each holding their own key and a glowing line to only the items they are allowed to open, the rest staying locked for them. Labels: "Each opens only their part". Title: "Authorized by being able to decrypt".

### 10. Open Source, No Lock-In
**Concept**: an open, portable stack with no supplier lock-in, runs anywhere, even offline. **Analogy**: an open formulary versus a single locked supplier.
> PROMPT: Left side: a closed proprietary black box with a padlock and a single chain tying a hospital to one vendor, labelled "Locked in". Right side: the same system shown as open building blocks running in several places, a clinic, a laptop offline, a university, with no chains, labelled "Open and portable". Title: "Open source, no lock-in".

### 11. The Pattern in Action
**Concept**: clinician creates the vault, sends a link, patient enters data, vaults exchange it, clinician consolidates. **Analogy**: a referral flow.
> PROMPT: A clean left-to-right numbered workflow of five steps with simple icons: 1 a clinician creating a vault, 2 a link being sent to a patient, 3 a patient entering data on a phone, 4 two vaults exchanging a sealed packet between them, 5 a clinician dashboard consolidating the data. Short labels under each step. Title: "How it works".

### 12. Agentic-Friendly
**Concept**: the vaults can be used by AI agents under the same key control; the patient can talk to their data. **Analogy**: an assistant that can read only what it is given the key to.
> PROMPT: A vault in the centre with a friendly AI assistant icon connected to it through a key, with small labels "Private LLM", "Claude", "ChatGPT" on three optional connectors, each gated by a key. To the side, a patient chatting with a helper bubble over their own record. A small note: "Same key control applies to AI". Title: "Ready for agents".

## The Two Passes

- **Pass one, section heroes.** One larger, slightly richer image per concept above, for the section slides.
- **Pass two, concept close-ups.** Smaller, even simpler images that isolate a single idea, for example just the public-and-private key pair, or just the signed-and-chained versions, generated individually for use mid-section or in the LinkedIn article.
