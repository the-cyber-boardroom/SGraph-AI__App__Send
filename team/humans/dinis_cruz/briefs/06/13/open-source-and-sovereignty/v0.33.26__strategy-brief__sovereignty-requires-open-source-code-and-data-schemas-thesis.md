# You Cannot Have Sovereignty Without Open Source

**from** Human (project lead)

## What This Is

A thesis on the relationship between sovereignty and open source: **sovereignty is control over your technological destiny, both the code and the data schemas have to be controllable and ownable rather than merely accessible, proprietary software leaves you one service-level agreement away from losing access and under the jurisdiction of whichever government controls the vendor, company nationality is an unreliable basis for sovereignty because ownership is fuzzy and shifts through acquisition, and therefore only open source preserves the possibility of independence, which makes it a political and societal choice about rent extraction rather than a question of innovation.** It is the philosophical underpinning of SGraph's open-source, no-lock-in, and zero-knowledge positioning, and the principle the recent comparisons turned on (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 access-not-security brief, and the v0.33.21 patientiq-comparison and v0.33.26 redcap-comparison briefs). The project lead's strongest formulations are quoted. New contributions: **the code-and-schemas point, the SLA-and-jurisdiction argument, the ownership-fuzziness point, and the rent-extraction framing.**

## The Premise: Sovereignty Is Control Over Your Technological Destiny

The starting definition: **sovereignty is a degree of control and independence over your own destiny, which today means over technology.**

The project lead: **"the idea of sovereignty is that you have a degree of control, a degree of independence over your destiny, in this case of technology. The problem, as a lot of companies and governments are starting to understand, is that technology more and more controls key parts of our society, and the source code controls how that technology works."** So sovereignty is not abstract: as society runs on technology, controlling the technology is controlling your destiny, and the source code is where that control lives.

## Code And Data Schemas Both Matter, And Access Is Not Control

A refinement that matters: **both the code and the data schemas must be controllable, and access is not the same as control.**

The project lead: **"the data was another big lesson that a lot of companies and governments learned, that the data schemas are as important as the code. Sometimes you say the data is open, you have access to the data, but you do not have access to the code or the schemas. And by access I mean you need to be able to control it, to own it, to have a level of independence over it, without lock-in."** So three things must be ownable, not merely visible: the code, the data, and the schemas. Being allowed to read something you cannot control or take with you is not sovereignty (cross-ref: the deterministic-value-indexes and access-not-security briefs, where schemas and control are central).

## The SLA And Jurisdiction Argument

The core risk of proprietary dependence: **you are one service-level agreement away from losing access, and whoever has jurisdiction over the vendor has power over you.**

The project lead: **"without owning it you are dependent on service-level agreements, so you are one SLA away from losing access. And the way this works is that whichever government has jurisdiction and control over the company that controls the code controls the other countries. It is literally that simple. And this is not just Europe; Europe has a wide dependence, but it goes the other way too, a lot of countries depend on European technology."** Two points: a proprietary dependency can be withdrawn at the vendor's discretion, and the vendor's home government can compel or restrict it, so dependence transfers power along jurisdictional lines. The argument is symmetric, not a claim about one region.

## Company Nationality Is An Unreliable Basis For Sovereignty

A point that undercuts a common assumption: **a national company is not a guarantee of sovereignty, because ownership is fuzzy and moves.**

The project lead: **"who owns a company is already very fuzzy, even without acquisitions, because of the share structure and who controls what. But especially with acquisitions you lose control. A European company gets acquired by a US company and the sovereignty moves, you lose it. And the other way round, a US or Asian company gets acquired and they lose sovereignty."** So buying from a domestic vendor does not secure sovereignty: share structures already obscure control, and an acquisition can move the locus of control across a border overnight. Nationality of the supplier is not a durable guarantee.

## Why Only Open Source Preserves The Possibility Of Sovereignty

The crux: **open source is what gives you a chance if you are cut off.**

The project lead: **"it is only when the code is open source that there is a possibility and the ability to have independence and sovereignty. Because if you suddenly get cut off from the service, at least you have a chance, the ability to create an economic model that supports it and does not lose you access to the technology. That is why you have to have open source in order to have sovereignty."** The argument is not that open source guarantees sovereignty, but that it preserves the possibility of it: if the relationship breaks, you still hold the code, the schemas, and the option to run, fork, or fund it yourself. Proprietary dependence removes even that option.

## It Is A Political Choice, And The Real Issue Is Rent Extraction

The framing the project lead insists on: **this is a political and societal choice, and the real question is rent extraction, not innovation.**

The project lead: **"we lived through a period where technology basically got away with it, and these are all political and societal choices. There are lots of business models, and I do not buy that this will stifle innovation, because the work is there. It is a question of whether there is a rent-extraction process happening, which is what proprietary software provides, especially when it is proprietary."** So the choice between proprietary and open is a policy choice, not a technical inevitability; the innovation-will-suffer objection is rejected on the grounds that the work gets done either way; and the distinguishing feature of proprietary software, on this view, is the rent it extracts from the dependency.

## A New Generation Of Commercial Models

The forward-looking claim: **open-source commercial models already exist and will be supercharged by the sovereignty conversation.**

The project lead: **"it is about having a new generation of commercial models, which already kind of exist, but are going to be supercharged as more and more open source gains traction, especially when you are having conversations about sovereignty."** So the thesis is not anti-commercial: it anticipates that as sovereignty becomes a buying criterion, open-source-based commercial models gain ground over rent-extracting proprietary ones (cross-ref: the open-core and commercialisation notes in the sovereign-data-aggregation-platform and linkedin-agentic-team briefs).

## How This Applies To SGraph

This thesis is the foundation under SGraph's positioning. Open source, no lock-in, zero-knowledge, and portability are the sovereignty argument made concrete: the code is ownable, the schemas are ownable (the deterministic indexes), the data is held by the owner not the vendor, and there is no SLA that can cut off access to your own encrypted data. The recent comparisons turned on exactly this, the difference between a vendor that holds and controls the data and an open, ownable substrate (cross-ref: the patientiq-comparison and redcap-comparison briefs), and the sovereign-aggregation platform is the thesis applied to analytics (cross-ref: the sovereign-data-aggregation-platform brief).

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: open source is necessary but not sufficient.** Holding the code does not confer sovereignty without the skills, people, infrastructure, and funding to run, fork, and maintain it if cut off. A country with the source but no capacity cannot exercise it.

**Tension 2: dependencies persist at other layers.** Open-source software often runs on proprietary cloud, hardware, and chips, and depends on upstream maintainers and package registries; open at one layer does not remove dependence at others.

**Tension 3: the sustainable funding question is unsettled.** The new generation of commercial models is asserted; funding deep open-source infrastructure sustainably at scale is not yet a solved problem.

**Tension 4: the rent-versus-innovation framing is contested.** Proprietary advocates argue the rents fund the research; the project lead disputes this, but it is a genuine debate, not a settled fact.

**Tension 5: sovereignty is a spectrum.** It is not binary, and full sovereignty has real costs; sometimes a proprietary tool is genuinely better or cheaper, and the choice involves trade-offs.
