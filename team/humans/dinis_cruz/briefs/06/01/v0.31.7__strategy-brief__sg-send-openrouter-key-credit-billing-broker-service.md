# An OpenRouter Key, Credit, And Billing Broker Service, With The Vault As The Secret Manager

**version** v0.31.7
**date** 1 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Strategy, Architect, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief

## What This Is

A signal and a commercial opportunity: **a simple service that makes it easy to leverage OpenRouter (and other) keys, handle the purchase of credit, and broker the billing/payment, with the vault used to manage the keys and secrets.** It builds on the OpenRouter-key model already in the platform (cross-ref: the v0.27.60 authentication brief's provided-key-with-credits, the v0.31.5 GDPR brief's provided-credits, and the v0.31.5 Vault App Store's bundled-credits) and turns the key-and-credit handling into a product in its own right.

The project lead's framing: this is a business worth making, the platform needs it for itself, so it can help others, and the vault is a good fit because it can manage the keys and secrets while the payment is brokered from the platform's side.

## The Signal: A Real Gap Observed Today

Worth recording as a signal: **a successful company the project lead spoke with today had a bring-your-own-key solution (server-side) for OpenRouter and others, but had not solved giving people keys and billing them.**

The project lead: **"I was talking today with these guys who have a successful company. They had a solution that allowed people to bring your own key, but it was a server-side key anyway, for OpenRouter and others. The interesting point was that they hadn't sorted out the ability to give people keys and the billing. It's simple, but somebody has to build it."** So a real, successful company has the bring-your-own-key piece but is missing the **give-people-keys-and-bill-them** piece. That gap is the opportunity: it is conceptually simple but nobody has built it cleanly, and there is demand (this company, and presumably others, need it). This is external validation that a key-and-billing broker service is worth building.

## The Opportunity: A Key, Credit, And Billing Broker Service

The business: **a simple service to make it easy to leverage OpenRouter keys, including purchasing credit and brokering the payment.**

The project lead: **"there's an interesting commercial opportunity to create a simple service that I can publish and manage for somebody if they want, or somebody can manage it. The service makes it easy to leverage OpenRouter keys, the whole purchasing of credit, and how that's going to work."** The service handles the things the observed company had not solved:

| Function | What It Does |
|----------|--------------|
| **Give people keys** | Provision and manage keys for users (OpenRouter and others) |
| **Purchase credit** | Handle the buying of credit |
| **Broker billing/payment** | Broker the payment from the platform's side |
| **Manage the keys/secrets** | Store and manage the keys securely (in the vault, below) |

And it can be delivered in two modes, per the project lead: **"a service I can publish and manage for somebody if they want, or somebody can manage it themselves."** So it is either a managed service (the platform runs it for a customer) or a self-managed one (the customer runs it), mirroring the delivery-model flexibility elsewhere (cross-ref: the consulting brief's three delivery models and the open-evaluation deployment models).

The dogfooding point matters: **"we need it ourselves, so I can help others."** The platform already needs key-and-credit management for its own OpenRouter use (cross-ref: the GDPR and visualisation briefs' provided-credits and budgets); building it well for itself produces a service it can offer to others. This is build-for-yourself-then-productise, a low-risk path to a product because the first customer is the platform itself.

## The Vault As The Key And Secret Manager

The angle that makes this a natural fit: **the vault manages the keys and secrets.**

The project lead: **"it's a good use of the vault, because the vault could be used to manage these keys. That's the angle. You go to our environment, and integrating with Google Auth, then maybe GitHub Auth and others makes a big difference, because we can store some of this data in that environment and use it to manage the keys, manage the secret, and then we broker the payment from our side."**

| Element | Role |
|---------|------|
| **The vault** | Stores and manages the keys and secrets (cross-ref: the prompt-context brief's keys-in-the-vault and the authentication brief's user-key storage) |
| **Google Auth / GitHub Auth / others** | The user signs in; the auth integration anchors the identity (cross-ref: the authentication brief's client-side OAuth) |
| **The environment** | Where the user goes to manage their keys and credit (the platform's environment; several iterations exist) |
| **Payment brokering** | The platform brokers the payment from its side |

So the vault is the secure store for the keys and secrets (which is exactly what a zero-knowledge encrypted vault is good at), the auth integrations (Google, GitHub, and others) identify the user and let data be stored against them, and the platform brokers the payment. This reuses capabilities already built: the vault as a secret store, the OAuth/auth integrations (cross-ref: the authentication brief), and the OpenRouter-key handling (cross-ref: the GDPR and Vault App Store briefs). The project lead noted there have already been several iterations of the environment, so the foundation exists.

The vault-as-secret-manager framing is important for trust: keys are sensitive, and a zero-knowledge encrypted vault is a credible place to manage them. This differentiates the service from a generic key broker: the keys are managed in the user's encrypted vault, not just held server-side (which was the limitation of the company the project lead observed).

## How The Pieces Fit

| Capability | Source |
|------------|--------|
| **Secret/key storage** | The vault (zero-knowledge encrypted; cross-ref: the prompt-context and authentication briefs) |
| **User identity** | Google/GitHub/other auth (cross-ref: the authentication brief) |
| **OpenRouter key handling** | The existing OpenRouter-key model (cross-ref: the GDPR and Vault App Store briefs) |
| **Credit and billing** | New: the purchase of credit and payment brokering |
| **Managed or self-managed delivery** | The deployment-model flexibility (cross-ref: the open-evaluation and consulting briefs) |

The only genuinely new build is the **credit purchase and payment brokering**; the rest (vault secret storage, auth, OpenRouter keys) exists. So the service is mostly composition plus a billing/payment layer.

## How It Helps Businesses Be Created Around It

This is itself a business, and it enables others:

- **A standalone broker business.** The key-and-credit-and-billing service is a product the platform can publish and sell (managed or self-managed), addressing the observed gap.
- **It underpins the other commercial models.** The bundled-credits in the Vault App Store (cross-ref: the Vault App Store brief), the provided-credits in assessments (cross-ref: the GDPR and consulting briefs), and the creator economy's paid skills (cross-ref: the base-vault brief) all need key-and-credit-and-billing handling; this service provides it. So it is both a product and the billing substrate for the rest of the ecosystem.
- **It helps the platform's own customers.** Anyone building on the platform who needs to give their users keys and bill them (the observed company's exact need) can use this service rather than build it.

So the service is a foundational piece of the commercial ecosystem (cross-ref: the marketplace and base-vault briefs): the thing that handles keys, credit, and payment for everything that charges money, and a saleable product in its own right.

## What This Asks For

1. **Build the key, credit, and billing broker service** (give people keys, purchase credit, broker payment), starting with OpenRouter and extensible to others.
2. **Use the vault as the secret/key manager** (zero-knowledge storage of the keys and secrets).
3. **Integrate auth** (Google Auth, GitHub Auth, and others) to identify users and store data against them.
4. **Broker the payment from the platform's side** (the new build: credit purchase and payment brokering).
5. **Offer managed and self-managed delivery** (the platform runs it, or the customer does).
6. **Dogfood it** (the platform needs it for itself; build for itself, then productise).
7. **Make it the billing substrate for the ecosystem** (the bundled-credits, provided-credits, and paid skills all use it).
8. **Produce the library page and example vault** (the page explains the service; an example vault managing keys and credit is the MVP).

Estimated effort: moderate; the secret storage, auth, and OpenRouter-key handling exist, so the new work is the credit-purchase and payment-brokering layer and the management UX. The MVP is a vault-backed environment that manages a user's OpenRouter key and credit with brokered billing.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not server-side-only key holding.** The keys are managed in the user's encrypted vault, not just held server-side (the gap in the observed solution).
- **Not only for the platform.** It is a saleable service for others, and the billing substrate for the ecosystem.
- **Not OpenRouter-only.** OpenRouter first; extensible to other providers.
- **Not a heavy build.** Mostly composition (vault, auth, OpenRouter keys) plus a billing/payment layer.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Handling payments and credit is regulated and non-trivial.** Billing, payment brokering, and credit involve financial and compliance considerations. Mitigation: use established payment infrastructure for the brokering; start simple; the credit/payment layer is the focused new build; take appropriate financial/legal advice.

**Risk 2: Keys are highly sensitive.** Managing users' API keys is a serious security responsibility. Mitigation: the zero-knowledge encrypted vault (the keys are encrypted, managed in the user's vault); the auth integrations; least-privilege; this is the differentiator over server-side-only holding.

**Risk 3: Dependence on OpenRouter and providers.** The service brokers third-party provider keys. Mitigation: OpenRouter first (itself a multi-provider aggregator); extensible; the service is the broker, not the provider.

**Risk 4: Managed delivery is an operational commitment.** Running it for customers is ongoing. Mitigation: offer both managed and self-managed; dogfood it first; the vault and auth foundation already exists.
