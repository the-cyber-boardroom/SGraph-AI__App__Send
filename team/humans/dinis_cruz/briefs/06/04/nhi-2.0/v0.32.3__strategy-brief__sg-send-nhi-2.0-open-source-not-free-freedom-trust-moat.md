## What This Is

The open-source strategy memo flagged at the end of the risk-management brief (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-risk-management brief's never-for-free through-line), framed in the NHI 2.0 context but applying platform-wide: **why open source is the right strategy even with zero external contributions, why it is not free (someone is always paying), and how that becomes a business model of agentic mass-customisation, productised deployment and maintenance, and selling trust.** It is the definitive statement of the model the open-evaluation brief began (cross-ref: the v0.27.60 open-evaluation brief) and that recurs across the monetisation, ownership, and partner briefs. New contributions: **open source as right even without contributions, lock-in as a degrader of your own architecture, the freedom to cannibalise your own code, open-source-is-not-free, agentic mass-customisation instead of crazy professional-services fees, the where-is-the-value price-point question, the 10% subset version, productised deployment, and the moat being trust rather than lock-in.**

## Open Source Is Right Even With Zero Contributions

The first reframe, decoupling the case from the usual community argument: **open source is the right strategy even if no one ever contributes a single patch.**

The project lead: **"a lot of people are still not getting the full picture. Even if there are no contributions, no external people submitting code and patches, and there is a lot of AI slop now, with even open-source repos not accepting submissions because of the mess, even if you never got any value from contributions, open source is still the right strategy for what I am about to describe."** So the usual argument for open source (external contributions, community patches) is set aside entirely; in a world of AI slop and repos closing to submissions, contributions may be near zero, and it does not matter. The case for open source here rests on lock-in, freedom, and the business model, not on community code. This is an important clarification because it removes the most common objection ("we will not get contributions") as irrelevant.

## Lock-In Promotes Bad Decisions And Degrades Your Own Architecture

The core anti-lock-in argument, sharpened to show lock-in hurts the vendor, not just the customer: **lock-in is tempting as you gain traction, but it promotes decisions based on locking in the customer rather than serving them, and the barriers it requires degrade your own architecture.**

The project lead: **"open source removes the lock-in of the customer, and that is more important than it seems. It is very tempting, as you gain traction, to want to lock in the customer so the only way to access your technology is through you. That immediately promotes bad decisions, because you make decisions based on what locks in the customer, not what is best for them, and anybody who does not think this happens has never worked in commercial software, professional services, or SaaS. It also means you do not let the customer scale, you do not allow deployment in their environments, you do not allow easy distribution, because you have to put all these barriers, limitations, and licences on top. You introduce attrition, and by introducing attrition you create much worse architectures, because there is a lot of simplicity when you do not have copyrights, licensing, and restrictions."**

| Lock-In Costs The Vendor | Detail |
|--------------------------|--------|
| **Bad decisions** | You optimise for locking in, not for the customer |
| **No scaling or customer-environment deployment** | The customer cannot run it where and how they need |
| **No easy distribution** | Barriers, limitations, and licences block it |
| **Attrition** | The friction lock-in requires |
| **Worse architecture** | Copyrights, licensing, and restrictions remove the simplicity good architecture needs |

So lock-in is not just bad for the customer; it is bad for the vendor's own product. It bends decisions away from quality toward control, it blocks the scaling and in-environment deployment customers need, and the barriers and licences it requires inject attrition that degrades the architecture, because the simplicity that makes software good comes partly from not carrying copyright, licensing, and restriction machinery. Open source removes all of that (cross-ref: the blast-radius brief's no-lock-in and sovereignty, and the Snowflake comparison's lock-in contrast).

## Open Source Frees You To Cannibalise Your Own Code

A consequence the project lead values: **open source removes the false sense of ownership that stops you cutting or replacing your own code, which matters more now that most code can be vibe-coded.**

The project lead: **"it makes you more motivated, and with less false sense of ownership, to cannibalise your own code or remove code you had, because it is open source, the code does not particularly have value, and especially today, where you can vibe-code most of it, that value is less and less. A lot of companies realise they spent millions developing features that someone can replicate in weeks or months. But there is a big difference between something that works as a demo and something running in production, which is where the value is."** So open source frees the team from over-attachment to code: when the code is open and increasingly cheap to regenerate (vibe-coded), you cut and replace it without the sunk-cost paralysis that afflicts proprietary teams who spent millions on it. The value was never the code (which others can replicate) but the production-grade running of it (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief's explorer-to-town-planner: a demo is the explorer, production is where the value is). Holding code loosely produces better software.

## Open Source Is Not Free: Someone Is Always Paying

The centrepiece, and the hinge of the whole business model: **open source is not free, because taking it and running it yourself means paying in people, creating a Genesis custom version you do not understand and cannot run optimally.**

The project lead: **"the most important point is that open source is not free. It is not free to take a piece of open source and run it in your environment, because somebody is always paying for people, somebody is always paying for something. If you clone SGraph and start to use it, the company has to pay somebody to deploy it, which means they are creating a custom version, a Genesis thing in Wardley terms. And they do not understand the platform, they never used it. The probability that somebody who is not the core developer deploys it in the most optimal, cost-effective way is zero. They will not fully understand it, and the more time they spend, the more it costs."**

| The Myth | The Reality |
|----------|-------------|
| Open source is free | Someone always pays, in people and time |
| Clone it and run it | You must pay someone to deploy it |
| You get the product | You get a Genesis custom version you built |
| You can run it well | The probability of optimal self-deployment by a non-core-developer is zero |

So "free" is a myth: cloning open source obliges the company to pay people to deploy and run it, and because they are not the core developers and have never used the platform, they build a one-off Genesis version (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief's Wardley stages) that they almost certainly do not deploy optimally or cost-effectively. The cost is real; it is just paid in internal people and time rather than a licence. Today companies pay that cost because there is no alternative, and most software companies charge enormous professional-services fees on top. This is the gap the model exploits.

## Turn The Tail Around: Agentic Mass-Customisation Instead Of Crazy Fees

The model: **instead of charging crazy professional-services fees, use agentic workflows to deliver mass-customised, supported versions, because the creator is the domain expert across all the phases that the customer is unlikely to have.**

The project lead: **"you turn the tail around. You use agentic workflows to create mass-customised and supported versions of the product, solution, or phase you are implementing. There are already five, six, seven phases in what I am describing, and whoever creates this will be a domain expert in all of them, but the company is unlikely to have a person who understands all those phases, all those requirements, and what needs to happen at each moment. That is why you create skills and customise the skills."** So the creator, who is the domain expert across all the phases (the NHI 2.0 series already has five and more coming: foundations, living-off-the-land, multiple-identities, visualisation, risk; cross-ref: those briefs), uses agentic workflows and customised skills (cross-ref: the skills series) to deliver customised, supported deployments at scale, rather than charging the crazy bespoke professional-services fees the incumbents do. The customer is paying anyway (open source is not free); the model is to be who they pay, and to deliver far more cost-effectively because you are the expert and you use agents.

## The Simple Question: Where Is The Value?

The business model in one question: **there is a price point at which paying the creator (or a partner) to do or maintain it is more cost-effective than doing it yourself, and the whole question is where that value is.**

The project lead: **"there is a price point, it could be 50p, five pounds, a thousand pounds, ten thousand, three hundred thousand, it does not matter, where it is more cost-effective to pay the company who did it, or a company, to maintain it than to do it yourself. The question is, where is that value? That is literally the simple question. And the flow is for the company who develops the software to provide that, or to have a network of partners who provide it, a channel-partner solution."** So the model reduces to a single question: at what price is paying you cheaper than the customer doing it themselves (and the customer is already paying, in people and a suboptimal Genesis deployment)? Operate at that margin and you have a valid business. Delivery is either direct (the creator) or through a network of partners (a channel-partner model; cross-ref: the Tavon and SecurityScorecard briefs).

## The 10% Version: Less Attack Surface, Cheaper, Maintainable

A sharp, concrete form of customisation, with a security and cost benefit: **a customer often needs only a fraction of the features, so you ship a subset version that has less attack surface, costs less to run, and is maintainable.**

The project lead: **"SGraph has tons of features, but a company might only need 10% of them. So we create a version with only 10% of the features. It has much less attack surface, it is much cheaper to run, some complexity simply does not exist, and we can maintain it because we know the software, and we can design the software in ways that make it easier to maintain and deploy."**

| The Full Platform | The 10% Subset Version |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| Tons of features | Only what the customer needs |
| Larger attack surface | Much less attack surface |
| More expensive to run | Cheaper to run |
| More complexity | Complexity that is not needed simply does not exist |
| Harder to maintain generically | Maintainable, because the creator knows the software |

So customisation is not only addition; it is subtraction. Shipping only the 10% a customer needs is a security win (less attack surface; cross-ref: the blast-radius and NHI-2.0 briefs), a cost win (cheaper to run), and a maintainability win, and only the creator, who knows the software, can safely produce that subset. This is customisation that makes the deployment smaller, safer, and cheaper, not larger.

## Productise The Deployment And The Maintainability

What scale forces, and what you build: **maintaining tens of thousands of deployments forces you to productise deployment and maintainability, and the infrastructure that delivers customised versions is what you build.**

The project lead: **"when you have to maintain ten thousand, twenty thousand, a hundred thousand deployments, you start to productise that. You productise the deployment, you productise the maintainability. When you give this problem to the engineers, they develop solutions that are much easier to maintain and deploy, which is the other side effect. We usually have infrastructure that delivers those customised versions, and that is what you build."** So the act of supporting customised versions at scale (tens of thousands of deployments) drives you to productise deployment and maintenance, and a beneficial side effect is that engineers, faced with maintaining that many deployments, build software that is far easier to maintain and deploy in the first place (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief's town-planner industrialisation). The deliverable is the infrastructure that delivers and maintains the customised versions.

## The Moat Is Trust, Not Lock-In

Where the defensibility actually comes from: **the moat is the customers who trust you to deliver and maintain this, and the ongoing maintenance relationship, not lock-in; in effect you sell trust.**

The project lead: **"that workflow and those customers become your moat. Your lock is the fact that you have the customers who trust you to deliver this. When new versions, security vulnerabilities, new features, new workflows, model changes, and new learnings come along, you maintain that for the customer."** So defensibility comes from trust and the maintenance relationship: customers stay because you reliably deliver and keep their customised version current (new versions, security fixes, new capabilities, model changes), not because a licence traps them. The project lead notes a concrete mechanism used in the past: **selling access to private GitHub repos**, the repo is what you sell access to, it is how you communicate with the customer, and the customer simply clones or pulls to get all the scripts and everything needed to run it. The closing frame: open source gives freedom, not free, and no lock-in, and what you sell is trust, being the reputable source the customer relies on.

## Freedom, Not Free: The Summary

The project lead's summary: **open source is not free (someone is always paying), what you get from open source is freedom and no lock-in, and the sweet spot is the margin where it is profitable for the customer to pay you rather than do it themselves, now finally possible at scale with agents.**

The project lead: **"open source is not free, somebody is always paying for it. What you get from open source is freedom, that is different, and you do not get lock-in, which is very important. The sweet spot is to operate at that margin where it is profitable for companies to pay you versus doing it themselves. As long as you operate there, you have a valid business model, and now with agents that is possible in ways it was not before. We play the game of empowering the user and being the reputable source of trust. We are selling trust."**

## What This Asks For

1. **Adopt open source as the strategy, regardless of contributions** (the case rests on lock-in, freedom, and the model, not community patches).
2. **Reject lock-in** (it promotes bad decisions, blocks scaling and in-environment deployment, and degrades your own architecture).
3. **Hold code loosely** (cannibalise and replace freely; the value is production-running, not the code; vibe-coding makes code cheap).
4. **Sell on open-source-is-not-free** (the customer pays in people and a suboptimal Genesis deployment; be who they pay instead).
5. **Deliver agentic mass-customisation and support** (the creator is the domain expert across the phases; use skills and agents instead of crazy fees).
6. **Find the value margin** (the price point where paying beats DIY; operate there; direct or via channel partners).
7. **Ship subset versions** (the 10% a customer needs; less attack surface, cheaper, maintainable).
8. **Productise deployment and maintainability** (the infrastructure that delivers and maintains customised versions at scale).
9. **Build the trust moat** (the customers who trust you to maintain it; sell trust; mechanisms such as private repos).

Estimated effort: this is the business-model thesis, not a build; the open stance, the skills, the agentic customisation, and the vault delivery exist across the corpus; the contribution is the open-source-is-not-free logic and the productised-customisation model. The proof is one customer's subset (10%) version, agentically customised, delivered and maintained through the trust relationship (for example a private repo), priced at the value margin.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a contributions argument.** Open source is right even with zero external patches.
- **Not lock-in.** Freedom and no lock-in; the moat is trust.
- **Not free.** Someone always pays; the model is to be who they pay, more cost-effectively.
- **Not crazy professional-services fees.** Agentic mass-customisation at the value margin.
- **Not the full platform for everyone.** The 10% subset each customer needs.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: "Open source is not free" must be communicated well.** Customers may still expect free. Mitigation: the Genesis-deployment and probability-zero arguments; the real cost is internal people and a suboptimal deployment; show the value margin.

**Risk 2: Competitors can also use the open code.** Open means others can deploy it too. Mitigation: the moat is trust and maintenance, not code; the creator is the domain expert; production-running beats a demo (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief).

**Risk 3: Productising tens of thousands of deployments is hard.** Scale of maintenance is real work. Mitigation: it forces (and rewards) maintainable architecture; agents make it feasible; build the delivery infrastructure as the product.

**Risk 4: The value margin varies by customer.** Where paying beats DIY differs widely. Mitigation: the simple question (where is the value) per customer; subset versions and partners flex the price; the margin is the business.

**Risk 5: Subset versions add variant-management overhead.** Many 10% versions to maintain. Mitigation: productised, version-controlled, connected in the vault; the focused-mini-tool discipline (cross-ref: the risk-management brief); the graph manages variants (cross-ref: the skills-as-graph brief).
