## What This Is

The next in the NHI 2.0 series, the elephant in the room the time brief pointed to (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-time brief's over-privileging flag): **an identity is meaningless without fine-grained permission control, and the dirty secret is that such control is borderline impossible today because the technology stack does not provide it.** It builds on the complex authorization matrix from the semantic-knowledge-graphs brief (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-semantic-knowledge-graphs-of-identity brief) and the duration-bounded permissions from the time brief (cross-ref: the time brief). New contributions: **the dirty secret of impossible fine-grained permissioning, identity granularity being pointless without permission granularity, the chicken-and-egg diagnosis of why the gap exists, mapping every application's authorization model into consumable graphs and cookie-cutter permission sets, the layer in the middle when an application lacks granularity, and the application explosion that makes the matrix harder but truer.**

The semantic-knowledge-graphs brief established that the authorization matrix is complex; this brief says it is impossible today, why, and how to fix it.

## An Identity Does Not Exist In A Vacuum: It Is A Graph

The starting point: **an identity exists to grant privileges, so every identity is connected to a graph of roles, privileges, and policies.**

The project lead: **"an identity does not exist in a vacuum. The whole point of an identity is to allow privileges to be given to something, so every identity is connected to a graph, because it is a graph: a mash-up of roles, privileges, and policies connected to it. We want to make sure that, for that action, the identity just needs the right permission to do that."** So an identity is inseparable from its permissions: it is a graph of the roles, privileges, and policies it carries (cross-ref: the semantic-knowledge-graphs and time briefs), and the goal is least privilege, the identity having exactly the right permission for the action and no more. The rest of this brief is about why that goal is currently out of reach, and how to reach it.

## The Dirty Secret: Fine-Grained Permissioning Is Borderline Impossible Today

The central, candid point: **achieving fine-grained, correct permissioning is borderline impossible today, because the technology stack does not provide good permission mappings.**

The project lead: **"here is the dirty secret of the industry: at the moment that is very hard to do, borderline impossible in a lot of cases, because the technology stack does not help. We do not have platforms that provide just-in-time, easy-to-understand, easy-to-develop-on, easy-to-visualise permission mappings. Even the ones that get close, some cloud providers, even there it is borderline impossible. That is a massive opportunity, another one of those revenue models."**

| What Is Needed | What Exists Today |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Just-in-time permission mappings | Largely absent |
| Easy to understand | Opaque |
| Easy to develop on | Hard |
| Easy to visualise | Rare, even on cloud providers |

So the honest state of the art is that fine-grained permissioning, knowing and controlling exactly what an identity can do, is borderline impossible, because no platform provides permission mappings that are just-in-time, understandable, developable, and visualisable, and even the closest (some cloud providers) fall short. This is stated not as a complaint but as the opportunity: the gap is a revenue model (cross-ref: the sellable-deliverables brief's IAM-graph deliverable), and closing it is the point of this work.

## Identity Granularity Is Pointless Without Permission Granularity

The dependency that makes this urgent: **there is no point having fine-tuned, multiple identities if you cannot fine-tune the permissions, because they would all end up with the same privilege.**

The project lead: **"there is no point of having fine-tuned identities if we cannot fine-tune the permissions. What is the point of having multiple identities if all of them end up having the same privilege? You need to capture the granularity."** So the elaborate identity model of this series (many granular, ephemeral identities; cross-ref: the foundations and living-off-the-land briefs) is worthless without matching permission granularity: many identities that all collapse to the same broad privilege achieve nothing. Identity granularity and permission granularity must advance together, which is why this elephant must be tackled head-on.

## Why The Gap Exists: The Chicken-And-Egg

The diagnosis, and a sharp one: **the gap exists because of a chicken-and-egg, permission-set builders never had flexible identities to connect to, and identity builders never had flexible authentication to use.**

The project lead: **"the reason this gap exists, the reason we do not have identities at the level I am describing, is that we have not had the need, because the applications and environments people build on do not support it. It is a chicken-and-egg: the people developing permission sets never had access to disposable, flexible, dynamic identities, so they could not connect to them, and the people developing identities never had access to authentication systems with that level of flexibility. So we ended up in the situation we are in now."**

| Side | What It Lacked |
|------|----------------|
| **Permission-set builders** | Disposable, flexible, dynamic identities to connect permissions to |
| **Identity builders** | Authentication systems flexible enough to express fine-grained permissions |

So neither side could move without the other: fine-grained permissions had no flexible identities to attach to, and flexible identities had no fine-grained authentication to express. The result is today's coarse status quo. Understanding this chicken-and-egg matters because it shows the gap is not anyone's failure; it is a structural deadlock, and the new stack (LLMs, the vault, ephemeral identities) is what breaks it, because now both sides can be built together.

## Map Every Application's Authorization Into Consumable Graphs

The big, defining ask, and why it must be open source: **we need identity and authorization mappings for every application we use, expressed as consumable graphs, so we can create cookie-cutter permission sets to assign to identities.**

The project lead: **"this is why it needs to be open source. We need identity and authorization mappings for every single application we use, every app: Salesforce, Dropbox, accounting software, an HR application, a vibe-coded app, AWS, Office, SharePoint, the corporate environment. Every one of them needs graphs in consumable ways, so we can create cookie-cutter permission sets to assign to identities. The identity has to come with the permission set, because the permission set is what allows it to do things."**

| The Need | The Detail |
|----------|------------|
| **Authorization mappings for every application** | Salesforce, Dropbox, AWS, Office, SharePoint, HR and accounting apps, vibe-coded apps |
| **Expressed as consumable graphs** | Break down each application's capabilities and the privilege each action needs |
| **Cookie-cutter permission sets** | Reusable permission sets assignable to identities |
| **The identity comes with the permission set** | The permission set is what lets the identity act |

So the work is to map the authorization model of every application into a consumable graph (what you can do, what privilege each action needs; cross-ref: the semantic-knowledge-graphs brief's authorization matrix), and from those graphs build reusable cookie-cutter permission sets that travel with identities. This is an enormous, ongoing body of work across the whole application landscape, which is exactly why it must be open source (cross-ref: the open-source-not-free brief): no single company can map every application; a community and open mappings can. The identity-comes-with-the-permission-set point is the design rule: an identity is issued together with the permission set that defines what it may do.

## The Layer In The Middle

The pragmatic answer when an application falls short, and a sharp one: **where an application lacks the granularity you need, you put a layer in the middle, a proxy, broker, or interceptor, to enforce it, and LLMs make those mini-connectors cheap, small, and solid.**

The project lead: **"the interesting question is whether the application has enough granularity to provide the restrictions we want, or it does not, and we have to build something in the middle. This is where there are good proxy brokers, LLM interceptors and executors, and validators, but they need this granularity. A platform might only let you have granularity at read email, write email, but it does not let you say which emails can be read, how many, per minute, per hour, per second, how many can be sent, which contacts. There are vast numbers of variables. So do we put a layer on top while we wait for the platform to gain those capabilities? This is where LLMs are so powerful, because we can create those mini-connectors, very small, testable, very solid pieces of code that enforce the permission."**

| The Application Provides | The Middle Layer Adds |
|--------------------------|------------------------|
| Read email, write email (coarse) | Which emails, how many, per minute/hour/second, which contacts (fine) |
| A few broad permissions | The vast number of variables real control needs |
| Whatever the vendor exposes | Enforcement while you wait for the vendor to catch up |

So when an application's own permissions are too coarse (it offers read-email and write-email but not which-emails-how-many-per-second; cross-ref: the time brief's duration-bounded permissions), you insert a middle layer, a proxy, broker, interceptor, or validator, that enforces the finer permission the application cannot. And the new stack makes this cheap: LLMs let you build these mini-connectors as small, testable, very solid pieces of code (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief's English-to-code town-planner stage), each enforcing one permission precisely. The middle layer is how you get the granularity now, without waiting for every vendor.

## Graph And Visualise Permissions, Policies, Roles, And Their Intersection

The throughline to visualisation: **you must graph and visualise permissions, policies, roles, and their intersections, because that is what gives the multiple identities real control rather than shifting the needle a little.**

The project lead: **"we need to graph and visualise permissions and policies and the intersection and roles, because that is what ultimately gives the multiple identities the real control we want. Otherwise we are just shifting the needle a little. Sometimes there are solutions you spend a lot of time and money to implement, but you are actually not moving the needle."** So permissions, policies, roles, and crucially their intersections must be graphed and visualised (cross-ref: the visualisation and semantic-knowledge-graphs briefs), because only then do the many fine-grained identities gain real control. The warning is pointed: many security solutions absorb time and money without moving the needle, and the way to avoid that is to address the permission-granularity problem head-on rather than around it.

## Side By Side: Identity And Granularity Together

The principle: **the identity work and the permission-granularity work must go side by side, because there is no point creating fine-grained identities without the granularity to constrain them.**

The project lead: **"there is no point creating the identities I was describing if you do not have the granularity to limit what the identity is going to do. Some of these things have to go side by side. The beauty now is that we can focus on the use cases, the ability to lock things down in ways that scale, related to the actual applications we have in play."** So this brief and the identity briefs are two halves of one thing: granular identities (cross-ref: the foundations and living-off-the-land briefs) and granular permissions (this brief), built side by side, application by application, use case by use case, locking down in ways that scale. Neither half is useful alone.

## The Application Explosion

A forward note, tying to the platform's own thesis: **there will be an explosion of applications as spreadsheets and vibe-coded apps become business applications, which makes the authorization matrix harder but also captures how business really works.**

The project lead: **"there is going to be an explosion of applications. If you think the authorization mapping is complex now, imagine once all these spreadsheets, which are going to become applications, and these vibe-coded apps become business applications, as they get deployed and more mature. The authorization matrix gets even more complicated. But it is okay, because we now start to capture what really happens in business."** So the matrix will get harder, because every spreadsheet-turned-app and vibe-coded app (cross-ref: the webify-spreadsheets brief) adds its own authorization model. But that is acceptable, even good: mapping all of it is how you finally capture how business really works (cross-ref: the skills-as-graph brief). The explosion is a reason to build the consumable-graph and cookie-cutter-permission machinery now, not a reason to avoid it.

## What This Asks For

1. **Treat the identity as a graph of permissions** (roles, privileges, policies; least privilege as the goal).
2. **Name the dirty secret** (fine-grained permissioning is borderline impossible today; the gap is the opportunity).
3. **Advance identity and permission granularity together** (multiple identities are pointless if they share one privilege).
4. **Understand the chicken-and-egg** (neither side had the other's flexibility; the new stack breaks the deadlock).
5. **Map every application's authorization into consumable graphs** (and build cookie-cutter permission sets; the identity comes with its permission set; open source because of the scale).
6. **Build the layer in the middle** (proxy, broker, interceptor, validator; LLM-built small, testable, solid mini-connectors enforcing fine permission).
7. **Graph and visualise permissions, policies, roles, and intersections** (real control, not needle-shifting).
8. **Anticipate the application explosion** (spreadsheets and vibe-coded apps; harder matrix, truer business picture).
9. **Produce the mapping MVP** (one application's authorization graphed, a cookie-cutter permission set, and a middle-layer connector enforcing a fine permission the app lacks).

Estimated effort: large in aggregate (mapping every application is an open-source, community-scale effort), but incremental per application, and the middle-layer connectors are small and LLM-buildable. The contribution is the diagnosis (impossible today, chicken-and-egg) and the approach (consumable graphs, cookie-cutter sets, middle layer). The MVP is one application mapped, with a cookie-cutter permission set and a connector enforcing a permission the application cannot (for example email rate and recipient limits).

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not identities without granularity.** Identity and permission granularity advance side by side.
- **Not reliant on vendors catching up.** The middle layer enforces what applications cannot, now.
- **Not a single-company effort.** Mapping every application is open source and community-scale.
- **Not needle-shifting.** Address the permission-granularity problem head-on; graph and visualise it.
- **Not defeated by the explosion.** More applications make the matrix harder but the business picture truer.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Mapping every application is enormous.** The application landscape is vast and growing. Mitigation: open source and community (cross-ref: the open-source-not-free brief); incremental, highest-value applications first; LLMs accelerate mapping.

**Risk 2: Middle layers add moving parts.** Proxies and interceptors are infrastructure. Mitigation: small, testable, solid mini-connectors (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief); one permission each; retire them as vendors catch up.

**Risk 3: Application authorization models change.** Mappings can go stale. Mitigation: consumable graphs that can be re-derived; community maintenance; version control (cross-ref: the FAQ brief).

**Risk 4: The intersection of permissions, policies, and roles is complex.** Visualising it is hard. Mitigation: the graph is built for it (cross-ref: the semantic-knowledge-graphs brief); start with high-risk capabilities (send, delete, read sensitive data).

**Risk 5: The explosion could outpace the mapping.** New apps appear faster than they are mapped. Mitigation: cookie-cutter reuse; LLM-assisted mapping; the middle layer for the unmapped; prioritise by use and risk.
