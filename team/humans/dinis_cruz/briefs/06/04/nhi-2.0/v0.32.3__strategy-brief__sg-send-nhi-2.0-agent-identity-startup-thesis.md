## What This Is

A synthesis thesis: **how the open-source SG/Vault stack could create and power a startup focused on NHI 2.0, the next generation of non-human identity, for agents and agentic teams, swarms, and flows.** Where today's NHI (call it 1.0) is mostly about securing static machine credentials (secrets, API keys, OAuth tokens, service accounts), NHI 2.0 is about governing dynamic, autonomous, communicating, team-forming agents: their identity, their authorization, how they talk to each other, how their actions are made accountable, and how their blast radius is contained. This brief pulls together the relevant threads from across the corpus (PKI-not-secrets, vault-to-vault communication, Email-FS-lite coordination, the blast-radius work, vault-in-vault sandboxing, provenance, and the platform-of-primitives) into a single company thesis. The NHI market facts are from public material as researched; the thesis is the project lead's.

## The Category Today (NHI 1.0) And Why 2.0 Is Coming

NHI (non-human identity) became a top security priority in 2025: it was recognised as its own category, attracted heavy funding, and gained an OWASP NHI Top 10 and regulatory attention. A non-human identity is any digital identity that authenticates and acts without direct human control: service accounts, API keys, OAuth tokens, certificates, bots, and, increasingly, AI agents. NHIs already outnumber humans by large multiples and are growing fastest in the agent class.

Today's NHI tooling (NHI 1.0) is largely about **securing static credentials**: discover secrets and service accounts, rotate and expire them, scope permissions, flag over-privileged or orphaned identities, and map each back to a human owner for accountability. This is necessary and valuable, but it is built around the credential as the unit, a relatively static thing an automated process holds.

The industry itself names the coming shift, and it is the opening for NHI 2.0. The fastest-growing, hardest NHI class is now AI agents, and the recommended controls the industry is converging on are telling: least-privilege scoped to specific tasks, short-lived automatically-rotated credentials, action-level audit trails for every agent decision, and policy at the business-logic level, not just the API level. An over-permissioned agent that hallucinates or is manipulated by prompt injection can act far beyond its intended scope. The problem is no longer a static credential; it is a dynamic, autonomous actor.

| NHI 1.0 (Today) | NHI 2.0 (The Thesis) |
|-----------------|----------------------|
| The unit is a credential (secret, key, token, service account) | The unit is an agent (and a team, swarm, or flow of agents) |
| Static, relatively long-lived | Dynamic, autonomous, acting and reasoning |
| Secure the secret (rotate, scope, discover) | Govern the actor (identity, authorization, comms, accountability, containment) |
| Map to a human owner | Cryptographic chain of custody across agents |
| Permission at the API level | Policy and gating at the action and business-logic level |
| Agents authenticate to systems | Agents also authenticate to and act on behalf of each other |

NHI 2.0 is the governance layer for autonomous agents and agentic teams, swarms, and flows, and it needs primitives NHI 1.0 does not have. The thesis: the SG/Vault stack already is those primitives.

## The Core Insight: Identity As The Travelling, Key-Gated Envelope

The unifying idea (cross-ref: the PKI-not-secrets brief): **an agent's identity, authorization, and chain of custody travel with the work, in the vault, gated by keys, rather than being a static credential checked at a gate.** Today's NHI loses context at the gate (a token is presented, access is granted, the chain is lost). The vault inverts it: the identity, the entitlements (as keys), the evidence, and the signatures of every prior agent travel inside the envelope. So:

- An agent is **identified by the keys it holds**, granular, per-task, often ephemeral, not by a long-lived secret.
- An agent is **authorized surgically**: it gets the key to do the one thing, and the privilege evaporates when the key expires.
- A flow or swarm is **a key-gated supply chain**: the vault travels the agents, each reads and acts on only what its key permits, and the sequence is cryptographically enforced (an agent acts only on data signed by the right predecessors).
- Every action is **provenanced and accountable**: the version history and signatures are the audit trail, who (which key) did what, when, in what order, revertible.
- A rogue or prompt-injected agent is **contained**: its blast radius is the one action its key unlocks, nothing more.

This directly answers the industry's named NHI 2.0 controls (least-privilege per task, short-lived credentials, action-level audit, business-logic-level policy), and it does so with cryptography rather than configuration, so the guarantees hold even against the platform and against a misbehaving agent.

## Why The Vault Stack Is The Right Foundation

Several properties make the stack particularly suited to NHI 2.0:

- **PKI, not secrets.** The whole category's pain is secret sprawl and over-privileged static credentials; the stack is built on many granular keys and surgical, ephemeral grants, the post-secret model (cross-ref: the PKI-not-secrets brief).
- **Agent-native communication.** NHI 1.0 secures agents talking to systems; NHI 2.0 needs agents talking to each other, which the vault-to-vault comms and Email-FS-lite coordination already do, with least-data (messaging beats access) and full provenance (cross-ref: the vault-to-vault-comms, three-agent-demo, and FAQ briefs).
- **Accountability is the medium.** The hardest NHI 2.0 requirement is action-level audit for autonomous actors; in the stack the commit log is the audit trail, provenance is not a bolt-on (cross-ref: the FAQ's Email-FS-lite case study).
- **Containment is cryptographic.** Blast-radius containment via keys and sandboxing means a manipulated agent is bounded by construction, not by policy it might evade (cross-ref: the blast-radius and vault-in-vault briefs).
- **Open source and no lock-in.** NHI is infrastructure; it must run inside companies and across vendors without lock-in, which is the stack's stance (cross-ref: the open-evaluation and blast-radius briefs), and it lets identity-provider partners build on it (cross-ref: the PKI-not-secrets brief's identity-provider partnership).
- **A substrate, not a point tool.** NHI 2.0 spans identity, comms, audit, and containment; the stack is the platform of primitives those are assembled from, not a single feature (cross-ref: the platform-of-primitives brief).

Pulling it into a company:

- **The wedge:** the problems the industry already names and cannot yet solve well, over-permissioned agents, static long-lived credentials, no action-level audit, prompt-injection blast radius, built on the vault's PKI, provenance, and containment.
- **The model:** open source and runs inside the company (sovereignty, no lock-in); partner with identity providers who distribute and manage the keys while the platform gates the agents and their flows (cross-ref: the PKI-not-secrets brief).
- **The proof points:** the three-agent comms demo scaling to hundreds and thousands of agents (cross-ref: the three-agent-demo brief); the Email-FS-lite agent team running on the stack today (cross-ref: the FAQ); the identity blast radius and containment (cross-ref: the blast-radius briefs).
- **The relationship to NHI 1.0:** complementary at the boundary (1.0 secures the static credentials; 2.0 governs the autonomous actors) and a successor in trajectory (as agents become the dominant NHI class, the actor, not the credential, becomes the unit).

## Why Now

The timing is strong and externally validated: NHI is a recognised, well-funded category; AI agents are its fastest-growing and least-governed class; the industry has named the controls agents need (least-privilege per task, short-lived credentials, action-level audit, business-logic policy) but is still mostly applying credential-era tooling to them; and regulation (the OWASP NHI Top 10, sector frameworks) is arriving. The gap between machine-speed agent proliferation and human-speed governance is the opening, and the vault stack's primitives are aimed precisely at it. As with the blast-radius company thesis (cross-ref: the blast-radius brief), the right position is to get better as everything gets better: the more capable and numerous agents become, the more this layer is needed.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not NHI 1.0.** It governs the autonomous actor, not just the static credential; complementary, not a replacement, at the boundary.
- **Not a point tool.** A platform assembled from the vault's primitives, spanning identity, comms, audit, and containment.
- **Not configuration-based security.** Cryptographic guarantees (keys, signatures, provenance) that hold against the platform and a misbehaving agent.
- **Not closed.** Open source, inside the company, with identity-provider partners; sovereignty and no lock-in.
- **Not a finished company.** A synthesis thesis pulling the corpus together; the assembly and go-to-market are the work.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: A crowded, well-funded category.** NHI has many funded players. Mitigation: NHI 2.0 (the autonomous actor and the agentic flow) is the under-served frontier, not the static-credential core; differentiate on agent-to-agent comms, cryptographic chain-of-custody, and containment, which credential-era tools lack.

**Risk 2: Incumbents will move toward agents.** NHI 1.0 vendors are adding agent features. Mitigation: the stack's PKI-not-secrets, provenance-as-medium, and agent-native comms are architectural, not features bolted onto a credential model; open source and the substrate position are hard to copy quickly.

**Risk 3: Standards and interoperability.** Agent identity standards are emerging (and contested). Mitigation: build on open standards where they exist; partner with identity providers; open source aids interoperability and adoption.

**Risk 4: The agent-to-agent comms is still maturing.** Some proof rests on the comms work at build/demo stage. Mitigation: be clear on what exists versus what is coming (cross-ref: the vault-to-vault-comms and three-agent-demo briefs); the demo is the proof path.

**Risk 5: Security claims must hold.** This is security infrastructure. Mitigation: cryptographic, not configuration, guarantees; provenance and audit; open-source scrutiny; the blast-radius containment is by construction.
