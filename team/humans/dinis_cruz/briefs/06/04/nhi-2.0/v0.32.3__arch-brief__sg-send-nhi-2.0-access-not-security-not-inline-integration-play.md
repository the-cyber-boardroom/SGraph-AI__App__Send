## What This Is

The next in the NHI 2.0 series (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0 foundations, living-off-the-land, visualisation, risk-management, and open-source briefs): **the key design principle that this is about access control, not security, and that the solution must never be inline, centralised, or a single point of failure; instead it lives off the land and plays in the integration space, where the better the underlying platforms and models get, the better it gets.** It sharpens two ideas from the blast-radius brief (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 blast-radius brief's not-a-choke-point and get-better-as-everything-gets-better) for the identity context. New contributions: **the access-control-not-security positioning, the never-inline design principle, the integration-space play, the strategy-must-not-depend-on-competitors-failing principle, components-versus-end-to-end, and leverage-not-replacement.** The project lead flags the next memo (the commercial model) and another topic (what to track and how to value the company); this brief develops neither.

## Access Control, Not Security

The positioning, and a tagline the project lead likes: **this is about controlling and allowing access, not about security.**

The project lead: **"this identity solution is basically about allowing and controlling access, not security, which is a good tagline. It is all about access, not about security."** So the framing is deliberate: the platform governs access (who and what can reach which data and capabilities, under which keys and identities; cross-ref: the foundations brief), and positions itself as access control rather than as a security product. This is sharper and more honest about what it does, and it sidesteps the crowded, fear-driven security positioning in favour of the concrete thing it actually delivers: controlled access. It pairs with the visualisation and risk framing (cross-ref: the visualisation and risk-management briefs): you see and govern access, and access is the lever.

## Never Inline, Centralised, Or A Single Point Of Failure

The key design principle: **the solution must not be inline and must not be centralised, because being a central, inline single point of failure is the last thing you want.**

The project lead: **"one of the key principles is that we do not want a solution to be inline, and we do not want it centralised. From a de-risking, scaling, deployment, and adoption point of view, the last thing you want is to be a central point, to be inline, to be a single point of failure. The advantage of not being that is that it is much easier to deploy, to evaluate, to try, and to be flexible. That is a very key design principle."**

| Inline / Centralised | Never Inline (Living Off The Land) |
|----------------------|-------------------------------------|
| A single point of failure | No single point of failure |
| Hard to deploy and adopt | Much easier to deploy, evaluate, and try |
| Rigid | Flexible |
| In the critical path | Alongside, leveraging what exists |

So the architecture must sit alongside and on top of what exists, not in the critical path. Being inline and central makes you a single point of failure and makes you hard to deploy, evaluate, and adopt; not being inline makes the platform easy to try and flexible (cross-ref: the platform-of-primitives brief's substrate stance and the blast-radius brief's not-a-choke-point). The project lead allows the exception: there may be solutions that end up inline and central, but that should never be the end state; it is custom-built for a while, fine for demos and low-risk situations, but not the design of the platform. The default and the design are never-inline.

## Adoption Is The Valuation

What this buys, and what matters (flagging a topic for later): **not being inline gains speed and adoption, and the company's value is adoption and usage, the identities identified, mapped, connected, and helped.**

The project lead: **"by doing that we gain a lot of speed and adoption, because the valuation of the company here is about adoption, about usage, about how many identities have been identified, mapped, connected, defined, and helped. That is the name of the game: how to help companies implement all this. That is what we should be tracking, which is actually another topic, what we should track and how we should measure and value the company."** So the metric that matters is adoption and usage (identities mapped and helped), and never-being-inline is what unlocks it (easy to try, deploy, and adopt). The project lead notes that what exactly to track and how to value the company is a separate topic for a later memo; here the point is that the never-inline design exists in service of adoption, which is the name of the game.

## The Better They Are, The Better We Are

The core strategic principle, and a sharp formulation: **the strategy must be that the better the underlying platforms and models get, the better we get, never a strategy that depends on competitors failing to execute.**

The project lead: **"take into account what is going to happen with LLMs and the models. We fundamentally want to work such that the better the models are, and the better the identity companies are, the better we are. Your strategy should not depend on competitors not executing well. The strategy should not be based on Google not getting their act together on identity, or a couple of other companies not improving. The strategy should be that the better they are, the better our solution is. The platform should sit on top of that capability of improving what is there."**

| A Fragile Strategy | The Right Strategy |
|--------------------|--------------------|
| Depends on competitors failing | Improves as competitors improve |
| Bets on Google not fixing identity | Sits on top of whatever Google and others build |
| Threatened by better models | Strengthened by better models |
| Zero-sum against the platforms | Compounds with the platforms |

So the platform must be designed so that improvements in the identity companies and the models make it better, not worse, the opposite of a strategy that quietly hopes competitors stumble (cross-ref: the blast-radius brief's get-better-as-everything-gets-better). The project lead is explicit that betting on Google or others failing to improve identity is the wrong strategy; the right one sits on top of their improving capability and compounds with it. This is both more robust (you are not exposed to competitors executing well) and more powerful (you ride the entire industry's progress).

## The Integration Play: Make Their Platforms Work Better

How you sit on top, and the space you own: **you play in the integration space, making the underlying platforms work better, and tight integration with whoever runs the agent or the identity de-risks you because they do the heavy lifting.**

The project lead: **"you live off the land and operate where you add value, leveraging what exists and making their solutions better. You play on the integration space, making these tools work better. If you have a tight integration with Claude, or ChatGPT, or Codex, or whoever runs the computer, whoever runs the agent, whoever is the identity, you de-risk your platform, because those platforms do the heavy lifting and you are leveraging them."** So the platform integrates tightly with the agent-runners and identity providers (the systems running the agents and holding the identities), and that integration de-risks it: the heavy lifting (running the models, running the agents, holding the core identities) is done by those platforms, and the NHI 2.0 platform adds the access-control, integration, and end-to-end value on top (cross-ref: the living-off-the-land and connectors briefs). Living off the land here means integrating with, and amplifying, the platforms that are already doing the hard parts.

## Components Versus End-To-End: Where You Win

Why the integration space is defensible: **the underlying platforms are better at their individual components, but they are not as focused as you on the integration, the end-to-end solution, and the specific use cases.**

The project lead: **"those platforms will be better at their individual components, but they usually will not be as focused as you are on the integration, on the end-to-end solution, on figuring out those use cases. That is where you want to focus, that marketplace, that connection, that ability to know how to solve that specific problem and focus on that specific solution."**

| The Platforms Own | You Own |
|-------------------|---------|
| The individual components (models, agent runtimes, core identity) | The integration across them |
| Best-in-class at each piece | The end-to-end solution |
| Broad, not use-case-focused | The specific use cases and how to solve them |

So the defensible ground is the integration and the end-to-end: the platforms excel at their components but are not focused on stitching them into a solution for a specific identity problem, and that focus (the marketplace, the connection, the use-case expertise) is where the NHI 2.0 platform wins. You are not trying to be better than Claude at being Claude, or better than an identity provider at core identity; you are the one who knows how to connect them into the end-to-end access-control solution for the use case.

## Leverage, Not Replacement

The go-to-market posture, especially early: **do not play the replacement card; leverage the customer's and the platforms' existing investment, and note that some of these companies could be acquisition targets.**

The project lead: **"at least in the beginning, do not play the replacement card, because that is always a tough play. Play that we can leverage the company's investment in those platforms and solutions, and the better those solutions become, the better you become. Some of these companies could be acquisition targets, they could acquire this company, which is cool, but the logic is to leverage, not replace."** So early on you position as leverage and amplification, not replacement, because replacement is a hard sell and pits you against entrenched investment, whereas leverage rides it (cross-ref: the SecurityScorecard brief's adopt-the-open-layer-not-compete and the open-source-not-free brief). The project lead notes the strategic upside: by making these companies better, you become valuable to them, and some could be acquisition targets (or acquirers), which is an additional outcome rather than the plan.

## More Strategy Than Technology, And Value Straight Away

Two closing points: **this is more a strategy than a technological decision (it is writing many integrations), and the solution must add value straight away in specific use cases, without massive deployment, permissions, or even much identity.**

The project lead: **"the good news is this is not that hard to do, because it is more a strategy than a technological decision, it is now a case of writing lots of integrations and connections. And it is very important that the solution adds value straight away in very specific use cases, so it does not require massive deployment, does not require lots of permissions, and ironically does not require lots of identity in order to be evaluated."** So the work is largely integration engineering (lots of connectors; cross-ref: the connectors briefs), a strategic choice more than a hard technical one, and the design constraint is immediate value in specific use cases with a low-touch footprint: no massive deployment, few permissions, and (the irony for an identity product) not much identity needed to evaluate it (cross-ref: the visualisation brief's low-touch, just-a-data-dump entry). Easy to try, valuable immediately, never inline.

## The Through-Line To The Next Memos

The project lead flags two follow-ups, developed in neither here: **the commercial model (the immediate next memo), and, separately, what to track and how to measure and value the company (adoption, usage, identities mapped and helped).** Both are noted as the through-line: the never-inline, integration-space, leverage design exists to drive the adoption that the valuation rests on, and the commercial model monetises it (cross-ref: the open-source-not-free brief).

## What This Asks For

1. **Position as access control, not security** (it is about access; the tagline).
2. **Design to never be inline or centralised** (no single point of failure; inline-central only for demos and low-risk, never the end state).
3. **Optimise for adoption** (easy to deploy, evaluate, and try; adoption and identities-helped are the valuation).
4. **Adopt the better-they-are-the-better-we-are principle** (the strategy must not depend on competitors failing; sit on top of their improvement).
5. **Play the integration space** (make the underlying platforms work better; tight integration with the agent-runners and identity providers de-risks you).
6. **Win on end-to-end and use cases** (they own the components; you own the integration, the end-to-end, the specific solutions).
7. **Leverage, not replace** (early on; ride their investment; note acquisition-target potential).
8. **Treat it as strategy plus integration engineering** (lots of connectors; value straight away in specific use cases; low-touch).
9. **Carry the through-line** (the commercial model and the what-to-track topic, the next memos).

Estimated effort: moderate and mostly integration engineering; the substrate, connectors, and live-off-the-land machinery exist (cross-ref: the platform-of-primitives, connectors, and living-off-the-land briefs); the contribution is the never-inline design principle and the integration-space, leverage-not-replace strategy. The proof is a specific use case delivered by integrating with an agent-runner and an identity provider, adding access-control value straight away, never in the critical path, with a low-touch footprint.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a security product.** Access control, not security; that is the positioning.
- **Not inline or central.** Never a single point of failure; inline-central is only for demos and custom, low-risk cases.
- **Not dependent on competitors failing.** The better they are, the better we are.
- **Not a replacement play.** Leverage and amplify; ride their investment.
- **Not the commercial-model or what-to-track memo.** Those are next; only flagged here.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Some deployments will pull you inline.** Real use cases may demand it. Mitigation: allow it as custom, temporary, low-risk; never make it the platform design; default to alongside-and-on-top.

**Risk 2: Integration-space players can be squeezed.** Platforms may absorb the integration. Mitigation: focus on end-to-end and use-case expertise the platforms are not focused on; the better-they-are-the-better-we-are compounding; acquisition optionality.

**Risk 3: Depending on platforms is its own exposure.** Tight integration creates dependency. Mitigation: integrate with many (not one); live off the land with your own compatible fallback (cross-ref: the living-off-the-land and open-source-not-free briefs); no single point of failure applies to your dependencies too.

**Risk 4: Leverage-not-replace may cap ambition.** Always leveraging could limit the platform. Mitigation: it is the early posture, not forever; adoption first, then expand; acquisition or category leadership are later options.

**Risk 5: Immediate value in specific use cases must be real.** Low-touch must still deliver. Mitigation: the visualisation low-touch entry (cross-ref: the visualisation brief); concrete use cases; no massive deployment or permissions needed to see value.
