## Time Is A First-Class Property Of Identity

The framing: **time matters deeply for identity, in several concepts including the lifecycle, sequence, duration, and even time travel through the digital twin.**

The project lead: **"time is very important here, because there are multiple concepts of how to think about time and connect it, including time travel, which is connected to the digital-twin concept."** So time is not a detail; it is a property that runs through identity in several forms (the lifecycle of an identity, the sequence of its actions, the duration of its permissions, and the ability to look back in time through the twin), each of which the rest of this brief develops.

## The Identity Lifecycle In Time

The first concept: **an identity has a lifecycle in time, a creation, a usage period, and a destruction or archival, which already raises questions of validity and the period of acceptance.**

The project lead: **"time on identities already brings several primitives. An identity has a lifecycle: when it was created, the timing of its usage, and when it is destroyed or archived. Even there we have an interesting concept of time, because: I just got an identity, is it valid, is it within the period of acceptance, or is it invalid?"** So every identity has a temporal lifecycle (created, used, destroyed or archived), and the basic temporal questions follow: is this identity valid right now, is it within its accepted period, or has it expired? This connects to the time-boxed acceptance from the risk-management brief (cross-ref: the risk-management brief) and the ephemeral keys from the foundations brief (cross-ref: the NHI-2.0-foundations brief): an identity, like a risk acceptance and like a key, has a validity window.

## Temporal Anomaly Detection

A sharp security application: **a great deal of malicious activity can be detected just by taking time into account, because a compromised identity acts out of order and at times that make no sense.**

The project lead: **"a lot of security vulnerabilities could be easily detected if you just take time into account. A compromised identity is going to access things out of order, access things when the timing does not make sense, at times of day that are not the ones that should be used for this. If a set of actions is normally done by humans, or by a particular environment, the timings matter. Another great way to detect malicious activity is actions happening at timings that do not make sense."**

| Normal | Anomalous (A Compromise Signal) |
|--------|----------------------------------|
| Actions in their usual order | Actions out of order |
| Activity at expected times of day | Activity at times that make no sense |
| Human-paced where humans act | Machine-paced where humans should be |

So time is a security signal in its own right: a compromised identity tends to act out of sequence and at the wrong times, and comparing actual timing against the expected pattern (the order, the time of day, the human-versus-machine pace) detects malicious activity that other checks miss. This is anomaly detection on the temporal dimension, and the digital twin and identity graph (cross-ref: the visualisation and semantic-knowledge-graphs briefs) are what make the expected pattern knowable.

## Temporal And Duration-Bounded Permissions

A central new control: **when you grant a permission or capability, grant it with a temporal and rate concept, not as an unbounded yes or no.**

The project lead: **"when we allow permissions and assign capabilities to an agent, it is very important to assign them with a temporal and duration concept. If we give an agent the ability to send an email, are we saying it should send emails as fast as it can be invoked, 5, 10, 20, 50 emails per second because the platform supports it? If an action is triggered by a human or by particular things, does it make sense to send more than one a minute, one every five minutes, one per hour? And the other way around: maybe we expect five per second, or five a minute, and we do not get them."**

| Unbounded Permission | Duration-Bounded Permission |
|----------------------|------------------------------|
| Can send email | Can send email at a sensible rate |
| As fast as invoked (50 per second) | One a minute, or one per hour, as the context warrants |
| No expectation of cadence | A both-ways expectation: too many, or too few, is a signal |

So a permission should carry timing: not just "can send email" but "can send email at this rate, within this cadence." An agent able to send email should not therefore be able to send fifty a second just because the platform allows it; the rate should match what makes sense for the trigger (a human-triggered action might warrant one a minute). And the expectation runs both ways: if you expect a certain cadence and do not see it, that too is a signal. Duration-bounded permissions are a powerful, simple control that the binary permission model (cross-ref: the semantic-knowledge-graphs brief's complex authorization matrix) misses.

## Time Travel Through The Digital Twin

A striking capability: **with the history captured, you can time travel through the digital twin, reconstructing what the identities and their permissions looked like at any past moment.**

The project lead: **"once you have all this information, you can ask: what did the identities look like at this time, what was actually happening at this moment, what was the status in this situation? We can time travel, especially with the digital twins, to get a much better understanding of what was supposed to happen at that moment. It adds temporal visibility: what happened at that moment, what did we understand, what was the identity at that moment, what permissions did it have?"** So because the digital twin (cross-ref: the visualisation brief) captures state over time, you can reconstruct the past: what an identity was, what permissions it held, and what was happening, at any chosen moment. This temporal visibility is valuable for investigation (what did this identity look like when the incident happened), for understanding (what did we know then), and for the lock-down payoff below. Time travel is the twin extended along the time axis.

## The Evolution Of Privileges Over Time

Why the time axis matters for security: **identity permissions change over time, and change itself, resets, elevations, new access, is where vulnerabilities occur, so you must track the evolution of privileges.**

The project lead: **"identity permissions change, or can change, especially when you use non-ephemeral, non-focused identities. So what was the identity at that moment, at that place, and what happens when you change? Vulnerabilities occur when identities change, permissions change, a password gets reset, users get access to things, or there is an elevation of privilege. We should be connecting the identities and understanding the evolution of the privileges of the identities."** So a non-ephemeral identity's privileges drift over time (resets, grants, elevations), and those change moments are precisely when vulnerabilities appear. Tracking the evolution of privileges (not just the current state) is therefore essential, and it is the time-travel capability that makes the evolution visible. This reinforces the case for ephemeral, focused identities (cross-ref: the foundations and living-off-the-land briefs): an identity that exists briefly and narrowly has little room to drift.

## The Payoff: Lock Down Without Side Effects

The major payoff, and a key principle: **by observing which privileges of an identity were actually used, and when, over a period, you can lock the identity down to exactly what it needs, without side effects.**

The project lead: **"an identity does not exist in a vacuum; it provides access, it has roles, capabilities, and privileges. The interesting question is: what privileges of the identity were used at what moment in time? If you run this visualisation mapping over a day, a week, or a period, you can tell exactly when the identity was used, and exactly what privileges were actually used. That means we can lock it down without side effects, which is a key principle: the more visibility you have, the more you can lock things down without side effects. And you can do simulations: if I give you this permission, will it work?"**

| Without Temporal Usage Data | With Temporal Usage Data |
|-----------------------------|--------------------------|
| Lock down by guesswork; risk breaking things | Lock down to exactly what was used; no side effects |
| Fear of removing a needed privilege | Evidence of which privileges were never used |
| Hope-driven change | Observed, simulated, safe change |

So the time axis delivers empirically-derived least privilege: observe an identity over a representative period, see which of its privileges were actually used and when, and then remove the rest, confident you are not breaking anything because the data shows those privileges were never used. This is the lock-down-without-side-effects principle (cross-ref: the visualisation and risk-management briefs) made concrete and safe through temporal observation, and it can be simulated first (would this permission set still work?). The project lead adds the Wardley context (cross-ref: the skills-as-graph and skill-lifecycle briefs): an identity in an explorer environment can be over-privileged (it is fine there), but an identity in a town-planner environment should be locked down hard, and the temporal usage data is how you do that lock-down safely.

## Over-Privileging (Flagged For The Next Memo)

The project lead flags the next memo: **why over-privileging happens**, especially the identities created by authentication tokens, which tend to be the union (or far more than the union) of everything needed to run in an environment, because platforms do not give good visibility on what is actually used. The three-dimensional, graph-based, time-aware views in this series are the answer (cross-ref: the foundations brief's union-of-privileges and the living-off-the-land brief's Uber identity). This brief notes the through-line; the next memo develops over-privileging.

## What This Asks For

1. **Treat time as a first-class property of identity** (lifecycle, sequence, duration, time travel).
2. **Model the identity lifecycle** (created, used, destroyed or archived; validity and period of acceptance).
3. **Detect temporal anomalies** (out-of-order and wrong-time access as compromise signals; expected pattern from the twin).
4. **Grant duration-bounded permissions** (a temporal and rate concept; the email cadence example; both-ways expectations).
5. **Build time travel into the twin** (reconstruct identities and permissions at any past moment; temporal visibility).
6. **Track the evolution of privileges** (change as a vulnerability moment; favour ephemeral, focused identities).
7. **Lock down using temporal usage data** (observe what was used, remove the rest, no side effects; simulate first).
8. **Apply the Wardley context** (over-privilege acceptable in explorer; locked down in town-planner).
9. **Carry the through-line** (over-privileging and the token union, the next memo).

Estimated effort: moderate, building on the digital twin, the identity graph, and the visualisation work; the contribution is the temporal model (anomaly detection, duration-bounded permissions, time travel, usage-based lock-down). The proof is observing an identity over a period, time-travelling its state, detecting a temporal anomaly, and locking it down to its observed usage without side effects, simulated first.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not time as a detail.** Time is a first-class property, in several forms.
- **Not binary permissions.** Permissions carry a temporal and rate concept.
- **Not guesswork lock-down.** Lock-down based on observed temporal usage; no side effects.
- **Not static identity.** Privileges evolve over time; change is a vulnerability moment.
- **Not the over-privileging memo.** That is flagged separately.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Capturing history at scale is real work.** Time travel needs state captured over time. Mitigation: the digital twin and version control (cross-ref: the visualisation and FAQ briefs); capture what assurance needs; ephemerality bounds it.

**Risk 2: Temporal anomaly detection can false-positive.** Unusual-but-legitimate timing happens. Mitigation: the expected pattern from the graph and twin; tune to the context (human versus machine pace); use as a signal, not a hard block.

**Risk 3: Duration-bounded permissions add complexity.** Rate and cadence per permission is more to model. Mitigation: the complex authorization matrix already needs the graph (cross-ref: the semantic-knowledge-graphs brief); start with the high-risk capabilities (sending, deleting).

**Risk 4: A representative observation period is needed for safe lock-down.** Too short a window misses rare-but-needed privileges. Mitigation: observe over a representative period; simulate before removing; keep the ability to grant back quickly (ephemeral keys; cross-ref: the foundations brief).
