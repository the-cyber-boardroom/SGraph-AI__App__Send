## What This Is

The next step in the NHI 2.0 series, the one that follows visualisation (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-visualisation brief): **risk management for non-human identity, where, once you can visualise the blast radius, the next move is to get risks mapped and accepted by the business, with time-boxed sign-off as the execution mandate, the change underwritten so no individual bets their career, and before-and-after ROI on blast-radius reduction.** It builds on the blast-radius thesis (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 blast-radius brief) and the digital twin from the visualisation brief. New contributions: **risk acceptance because the risk already exists, time-boxed sign-off as priority and mandate, underwriting the side effects of a change, before-and-after ROI on single points of failure, and focused just-enough mini-tools such as a minimal asset register.** The project lead flags the next memo (the never-for-free principle); this brief does not develop it.

## Risk Follows Visualisation

The sequence: **once you can visualise, the next step is to get risks mapped and accepted by the business, because in identity every change carries blast radius and can break things.**

The project lead: **"the next big one is risk management, because subsequent tools today do not take into account how things are actually done in organisations and the risk of doing them. The main step, once you have visualisation, is to get risks accepted and mapped by the business, because every change, especially in identity, is connected to blast radius. Every change has the potential to break or brick something, so you need a positive risk-reward, and you need somebody to have accepted the risk."** So risk management is the step after visibility: you have shown the blast radius (cross-ref: the visualisation brief), and now each proposed change to an identity must be weighed (it can break things) and accepted by the business, with a positive risk-reward. This addresses a gap the project lead sees in existing tools: they do not account for how organisations actually operate or the real risk of making a change.

## Acceptance: The Risk Already Exists, So Someone Has Already Accepted It

A reframing of risk acceptance: **a risk that already exists has, by default, already been accepted by the business; the job is to make that acceptance explicit and time-boxed.**

The project lead: **"the way I look at accepting risks is that everybody needs to want a risk to exist. If a key in identity exists, it needs to be accepted, because it already exists. So by default somebody has already accepted it. What we need is a way for the business exec to decide: do they want to accept it for a day, an hour, a week, a month? Because that gives you the mandate. If somebody says I am not happy with this risk and I am signing off for an hour, then in the next hour it should be fixed."**

| The Shift | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **The risk already exists** | An identity, a key, an access that is live has, by default, been accepted |
| **Make acceptance explicit** | The business decides, consciously, to accept it (or not) |
| **Time-box the acceptance** | Accept for an hour, a day, a week, a month |
| **The time-box is the mandate** | A short acceptance window means a short deadline to fix |

So acceptance is not asking permission for a new risk; it is making explicit the acceptance that already exists implicitly (the access is live, so the business is already carrying the risk). The new element is the time-box: an executive decides how long they are willing to accept it, and that window becomes the mandate and the priority. If someone signs off for only an hour (or, realistically, a day), that is a commitment that it will be fixed within that window, which gives execution its priority. Acceptance becomes a dial (how long), not just a yes or no.

## Underwriting: No One Should Bet Their Career On A Change

A genuinely important human insight: **the person making a risky change must not be betting their career on it; their boss, or their boss's boss, must underwrite the side effects, or change does not happen.**

The project lead: **"you cannot make the person who has to make a security change bet their career on it. You want that person to feel safe, to feel their back is covered, which means they need their boss, or their boss's boss, to underwrite the side effect of the change they are doing. If you do not have that, people do not want to make change, and that is often the problem. Making a change is not just flicking something; you need to underwrite what can go wrong. If you do that, people are much more willing to make changes."**

So a major reason identity risk goes unaddressed is human: the person asked to make the change (close an access, rotate a key, narrow a privilege) fears being blamed if it breaks something, so they do nothing. The fix is underwriting: someone senior enough explicitly accepts the side effects, so the individual is covered and safe to act. This is why the time-boxed acceptance (above) matters operationally: the executive's signed acceptance is the underwriting, the record that the business, not the individual, owns the consequence. The digital twin and simulation make this underwritable, because the underwriter can see the likely side effects before signing (cross-ref: the visualisation brief's digital twin). And when it goes right, you celebrate it, which reinforces the willingness to make changes.

## Simulation: See The Side Effects Before You Act

The digital twin's role in risk (cross-ref: the visualisation brief): **the twin lets you simulate a change and see its side effects before making it, which is what makes underwriting possible.**

The project lead: **"the digital twins are very connected to risk, because based on the visualisation and discovery we create a good understanding of what is happening, the mappings, the flows, the architecture, who owns what, and the side effects. Then you can simulate it: if I close that, what happens? Sometimes you are surprised where the impact occurs. And what is the risk?"** So before a change, the twin answers "if I close or change this, what breaks?", and the side effects are sometimes surprising (the impact lands somewhere unexpected). This is what lets the underwriter sign with eyes open and lets the contextual-risk judgement from the visualisation brief (cross-ref: the visualisation brief's contextual risk) be made concretely: the same change is safe or dangerous depending on what the simulation shows it touches.

## ROI: Before And After, Single Points Of Failure Reduced

The payoff to the business, and the proof of value: **as you implement changes, you show before-and-after ROI, the reduction in single points of failure and blast radius.**

The project lead: **"once you start to implement and change identities, you can go back and provide ROI. You can say, here is the risk reduction we have done, here is the before, here is the after, here is the blast radius. You went from having 25 or 100 or 1000 single points of failure, where if that account was compromised, that key was stolen, or that agent went rogue it would be a disaster, to now five or ten, and now the ones you have are monitored where before nobody was monitoring them."**

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| 25, 100, or 1000 single points of failure | 5 or 10 |
| Compromise, theft, or a rogue agent is a disaster | Contained, far fewer critical paths |
| Unmonitored | Monitored |

So the value is demonstrable: the visualisation gave the before picture, the changes reduce the single points of failure and blast radius, and the after picture proves the risk reduction. A vivid worked example the project lead gives: **map how many agents can delete the production database** (a visualisation you should have), then tell the business **"you have ten agents running right now that can delete your production database, do you accept that risk?"**, and when they say no, you have the mandate and a plan. The before-and-after on single points of failure is the ROI story that justifies the whole engagement (cross-ref: the evidence and blast-radius briefs).

A nuance the project lead adds on honest ROI: when you fix the database-deletion risk, the person who now owns the fix could, in theory, themselves delete the database by accident, because they operate where that is possible, but that risk already existed, so you are not adding new risk, you are concentrating and governing existing risk. Honest accounting matters: you are reducing and containing risk, not pretending it vanished.

## Focused, Just-Enough Mini-Tools: The Minimal Asset Register

How the risk workflow is delivered, and a sharp principle: **plug into a mature risk workflow if one exists, and if not, create focused, just-enough mini-tools, an asset register that maps only identities to assets, not a full-blown one.**

The project lead: **"if the company already has a mature risk workflow, a risk dashboard, risk and asset management, we feed it. If not, we add it. With our ability to create mini-apps and mini-tools, if the company does not have an asset register, we give them one, created on the fly, and it is still better than what they have. And every time you create one of these, you focus only on the need: if they do not have an asset register, we do not create a full-blown one, we create one that maps identities to certain assets, which is quite simple."**

| The Principle | Applied |
|---------------|---------|
| **Plug into what exists** | Feed a mature risk workflow, dashboard, or asset management if present |
| **Add it if it is missing** | Create the missing piece with mini-apps (cross-ref: the visualisation and webify briefs) |
| **Focus on the need only** | Not a full asset register, just identities mapped to assets |
| **Still better than nothing** | The focused tool beats what they have (often nothing) |

So the risk workflow is delivered either by feeding the organisation's existing tooling or, where it is missing, by creating focused mini-tools that do exactly the needed job and no more, a minimal asset register that maps identities to assets, not a general-purpose one. This is the focused-mini-app power (cross-ref: the visualisation brief): you create only what the need requires, which is fast, valuable, and streamlined, and it is hosted, distributed, version-controlled, and connected in the vault.

## The Vault's Role In The Risk Workflow

Pulling it together (cross-ref: the visualisation and foundations briefs): **the vault distributes and manages the risk information, connects it, and makes every change version-controlled, auditable, and traceable, which is what enables the underwriting.**

The project lead: **"the vaults allow the information to be distributed, managed, and connected one to the other. Everything should be version-controlled, auditable, and traceable when we make changes and accept risks, and this allows the underwriting of the actions, the visualisation of what will happen, the celebration when we add value, and the de-risking."** So the vault is where the risk acceptances, the time-boxes, the underwriting records, and the before-and-after evidence live, version-controlled and auditable (cross-ref: the FAQ's commit-log-as-audit-trail and the foundations brief). The audit trail is what makes underwriting real: who accepted which risk, for how long, who underwrote the change, what the before and after were. Risk management is a workflow on the vault, not a separate system.

## What This Asks For

1. **Make risk the step after visualisation** (map and accept risks; every identity change carries blast radius; require a positive risk-reward).
2. **Reframe acceptance** (the risk already exists, so it is already accepted; make it explicit).
3. **Time-box the acceptance** (an hour, a day, a week; the window is the mandate and the priority).
4. **Underwrite the change** (the boss or the boss's boss accepts the side effects; no one bets their career; celebrate success).
5. **Simulate before acting** (the digital twin shows the side effects; underwrite with eyes open; contextual risk made concrete).
6. **Prove before-and-after ROI** (single points of failure and blast radius reduced; the database-deletion example; honest accounting).
7. **Deliver focused mini-tools** (feed a mature risk workflow, or create just-enough tools such as an identities-to-assets register).
8. **Run risk on the vault** (distribute, connect, version-control, and audit the acceptances, underwriting, and evidence).
9. **Carry the through-line** (revenue opportunities and the never-for-free principle, the next memo).

Estimated effort: moderate; the digital twin and visualisation exist (cross-ref: the visualisation brief), the vault provides the audited workflow and mini-tools, and the contribution is the risk-acceptance, time-box, and underwriting model. The MVP is a risk workflow on the vault: surface a blast-radius finding (agents that can delete the production database), record a time-boxed executive acceptance and the underwriting, simulate the fix, make the change, and show the before-and-after.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not risk theatre.** Real, contextual risk, measured against the surrounding controls and simulated side effects.
- **Not a permanent yes or no.** Time-boxed acceptance; the window is the mandate.
- **Not career-betting for individuals.** The change is underwritten by someone senior.
- **Not a full-blown asset register.** Focused, just-enough tools that map only what the need requires.
- **Not the never-for-free memo.** That is next; only flagged here.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Executives may resist accepting risks explicitly.** Naming and time-boxing a risk forces a decision. Mitigation: the framing that the risk already exists (they already carry it); the vivid examples (agents that can delete production); the before-and-after ROI.

**Risk 2: Underwriting requires the right authority.** The underwriter must be senior enough. Mitigation: the time-boxed acceptance is the underwriting record; escalate to the boss's boss where needed; the audit trail makes ownership clear.

**Risk 3: Simulation may miss side effects.** The twin may not capture everything. Mitigation: honest accounting (you reduce and contain, not eliminate); start with the clear cases; deepen the twin over time.

**Risk 4: ROI claims must be defensible.** Before-and-after numbers are sensitive. Mitigation: ground in the visualisation data; the audit trail; conservative, evidenced claims (cross-ref: the evidence briefs).

**Risk 5: Mini-tools must not become unmanaged sprawl.** Many focused tools can proliferate. Mitigation: version-controlled and connected in the vault; focused on the need; the library and graph manage them (cross-ref: the skills-as-graph brief).
