## What This Is

The next in the NHI 2.0 series: **semantic knowledge graphs applied to identities, why identity needs them, and the reciprocal insight that the semantic graph needs identity too.** It builds on the graph thinking from the skills series (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 skill-as-projection-of-graph and skills-as-graph briefs, and the v0.31.7 semantic-knowledge-graphs-for-agentic-skills brief) and the PKI foundations (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-PKI-foundations brief), but takes the new turn into identity. New contributions: **the taxonomy of identity types, why semantic graphs failed before for the same reason as PKI, the complex authorization and authentication matrix, programmatic and deterministic navigation as the source of assurance (code, not an LLM), the apples-to-apples reconciliation of differing identity definitions, the reciprocal insight that graphs need identities (the semantic-web verification gap), and the explosion of identities when every node needs one.** The project lead flags identities-over-time as a separate topic.

## A Semantic Knowledge Graph, Applied To Identity

The starting definition, applied to the domain: **a semantic knowledge graph is the taxonomy and ontology that define the classes, what an identity is, what it has and should have, its relationships, and how identities interact.**

The project lead: **"what a semantic knowledge graph is, fundamentally, is the ability to create a taxonomy, an ontology that defines the pieces of the puzzle, the classes. In this case, what is an identity, what does it have, what should it have, what are the relationships, what is it made of, the fundamental nodes, edges, and relationships, and then the mapping of the taxonomy, how they interact and relate to each other."** So applied to identity, the semantic knowledge graph defines what an identity is (the class), its attributes, its relationships, and how identities interact, the ontology and taxonomy of identity. The project lead's view is that identity needs exactly this, because identity is not one simple thing.

## The Taxonomy Of Identity Types

The first reason identity needs the graph, and a genuinely new point: **there are many kinds of identity, and they all need describing through the semantic knowledge graph.**

The project lead: **"there is a difference between a corporate identity, a physical identity, an agentic identity, an application identity, a department identity, and a role. All of those should be described using semantic knowledge graphs."**

| Identity Type | Example |
|---------------|---------|
| **Corporate** | The company as an identity |
| **Physical** | A person |
| **Agentic** | An agent (the NHI 2.0 focus) |
| **Application** | An application or service |
| **Department** | A department |
| **Role** | A role someone or something holds |

So identity is a class with many subtypes, each with different attributes and relationships, and the semantic knowledge graph is how you describe and relate them coherently. This is why a graph (not a flat list) is needed: the types interrelate (a role within a department within a company, an agent acting under an application identity), and only an ontology captures that structure.

## Why Now: The Same Story As PKI

The timing argument, parallel to the PKI one (cross-ref: the NHI-2.0-foundations brief): **semantic knowledge graphs were well thought through but failed before for lack of the right infrastructure, which LLMs, cloud scale, and ephemerality now provide.**

The project lead: **"semantic knowledge graphs were really well thought through, there is a lot of great history and thinking behind them. The problem in the past was the same as PKI: they did not have the correct infrastructure and technology stack, which LLMs now provide, plus the scalability we now have with cloud and computing, and the ephemerality, like the ephemeral keys I described. The same as PKI, it is about having lots and lots of identities."** So semantic knowledge graphs, like PKI, are not new ideas; they are good ideas that lacked the infrastructure to scale. What is new is the enabling stack: LLMs (to build and navigate ontologies), cloud scale (to handle the volume), and ephemerality (graphs and identities that exist for a time and are torn down). The parallel to PKI is exact, both were right and early, and both become practical now, at the scale of lots and lots of identities.

## Graphs Of Graphs, Applied To The Organisation

The structure, applying graphs-of-graphs to the org (cross-ref: the skill-as-projection brief's graphs of graphs): **the identity graph is an ontology of ontologies, because every department, company, supplier, and application has its own ontology and permission and authentication mappings.**

The project lead: **"the semantic graph of identities is about graphs of graphs, ontology of ontology of ontologies. Every department needs its own ontology and taxonomy, every company has that, and there are times when companies are made of companies. Then you have third-party suppliers, then applications, then the apps you use, each with its own permission mappings and its own authentication."** So the identity graph nests: each department has an ontology, each company (and companies are made of companies) has one, each supplier and application has one, each with its own permissions and authentication. The organisation is an ontology of ontologies, and the identity graph must compose them. This is graphs-of-graphs (cross-ref: the skill-as-projection brief) applied to organisational and supply-chain structure.

## The Authorization And Authentication Matrix Is Complex

A sharp, practical point: **authorization is not binary, the authorization and authentication matrix across products, companies, departments, and the supply chain is genuinely complex, and the graph must capture it.**

The project lead: **"the authorization, the authentication of a particular action, the authentication layer, the authorization matrix that exists on applications is actually very complex. Authorization gets very complex very fast, because it is not just full access to something. We need to describe the authorization and authentication model of the products, the company, the department, the interactions, how things flow, the supply chain, all of that has this matrix."**

So a major reason the graph is needed is that authorization is not a simple yes or no; real applications have complex authorization matrices (granular permissions, conditional access, role hierarchies), and the full picture spans products, companies, departments, their interactions, the flows between them, and the supply chain. Describing this requires the semantic graph, because the matrix is too complex and too interconnected for anything flatter. This connects the identity graph to the permission, authentication, and identity graph types from the skill-as-projection brief (cross-ref: the skill-as-projection brief) and to the key-gated authorization of the foundations brief.

## Determinism Is The Source Of Assurance

The major new payoff, and an important one: **once identity is a navigable graph, you can query and navigate it deterministically with code, and that determinism is the source of assurance, which an LLM performing an action cannot give.**

The project lead: **"the beauty of having taxonomies and ontologies moving into a knowledge graph is that you first have something that can be programmatically navigated. You can write queries, you can navigate the system in a deterministic way, and that is very important, because that is when you have assurance. You cannot have assurance if you have an LLM performing an action. You can only have assurance, determinism, and repeatability when you have code writing this. The semantic graphs of identity are critical because that is what gives us context."**

| LLM Performing The Action | Code Navigating The Graph |
|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| No determinism | Deterministic |
| No repeatability | Repeatable |
| No assurance | Assurance |
| Contextless | Context from the graph |

So the graph is what makes identity governable with assurance: because it can be navigated programmatically (queried deterministically by code), you get repeatability and assurance, which you cannot get from an LLM performing actions directly. This is the English-to-code maturation (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief) applied to identity: the LLM helps build and navigate the ontology, but the assurance comes from deterministic code over the graph. And the graph gives context, the meaning that lets the deterministic navigation be correct. Assurance, in identity, comes from the graph plus code, not from an LLM acting.

## Reconciling Differing Definitions: Apples To Apples

Why the shared ontology matters, applying meaning-through-connectivity (cross-ref: the skill-as-projection brief): **every company defines identity differently, and the semantic graph lets you compare apples to apples, confirming you mean the same thing.**

The project lead: **"the semantic graph lets us understand apples to apples, are we talking about the same thing, the same requirement, the same logic, the same weight? It matters because companies, corporations, small companies, individuals, and applications all have different definitions of identity. I bet that even today, if you ask ten experts what an identity is, you get ten definitions, and ten companies will each define it based on its own view, usually designed for what they do well. This is one of the key concepts of my graphs of graphs of graphs, you get meaning through connectivity. A public key in isolation does not give you anything; it is the graph it is connected to, the information nodes, the understanding of what connects to it."**

So because there is no single agreed definition of identity (ten experts, ten definitions; each company's definition shaped by what it does well), you need the semantic graph to reconcile them, to confirm two parties mean the same thing by comparing the structure, not the label. This is meaning-through-connectivity (the graphs-of-graphs-of-graphs concept; cross-ref: the skill-as-projection brief): a public key alone is meaningless; its meaning comes from the graph around it (what it connects to, what it represents). Identity gets its meaning from connectivity, which is why the graph, not the label, is the unit.

## The Reciprocal Insight: Graphs Need Identities Too

A major and elegant insight: **it goes both ways, identities need semantic graphs for meaning and scale, and the semantic graph needs identity, because the first generation of the semantic web failed for lack of verifiable identity.**

The project lead: **"one of the fundamental problems the first generation of the semantic web had is that there was no strong way of verifying things, very little hashing, very few identities connected to it. If you have a node that means the company, it should be connected somewhere to a public key that represents that company, because that is how you really get assurance. So it goes both ways: not only do identities need semantic graphs to get meaning and scale, but the semantic web also needs identity, especially the way we are describing non-human identity. The ontology defines the rules of the game, and the power of an ontology of ontologies connecting to each other is that we can figure out the rules, connect them, and arrive at meaning and compatibility."**

| Direction | The Need |
|-----------|----------|
| **Identities need graphs** | For meaning, scale, transfer, and to define the rules of the game (the ontology) |
| **Graphs need identities** | The semantic web 1.0 could not verify nodes; a node should link to a public key that proves what it is |

So the two are reciprocal. Identities need the semantic graph (for meaning, scale, and reconciliation, above). And the semantic web needs identity: its first generation failed partly because nodes were not verifiable (little hashing, few identities), so a node claiming to be the company could not be trusted; connecting that node to a public key representing the company gives the assurance the semantic web lacked (cross-ref: the foundations brief's chain of authenticity). The NHI 2.0 work, by attaching verifiable identity to graph nodes, supplies what the semantic web was missing.

## Every Node Needs An Identity: The Explosion

The wild consequence the project lead draws: **if every node of a graph needs an identity to be verifiable, then every concept, document, and definition is a non-human identity, an explosion of identities.**

The project lead: **"this is now quite crazy, but every definition of a graph, every node, a Wikipedia article, a document, a concept, needs an identity. The Wikipedia definition of a topic should have an identity connected to the source, Wikipedia, and connected to the sources that provided it. So you end up with a crazy explosion of identities, and that is also a non-human identity."** So taken to its conclusion, verifiability requires that every node (every concept, document, definition) has an identity linking it to its source and provenance, a Wikipedia article's node linked to Wikipedia and to its sources. This produces an explosion of identities, and they are non-human identities, which dramatically expands the scope of NHI 2.0: not just agents and services, but every node of every knowledge graph. It is also the deepest unification of the two threads, knowledge and identity become the same fabric, every piece of knowledge a verifiable identity with provenance.

## Identities Over Time (Flagged)

The project lead flags a separate topic without developing it: **identities over time**, that identities change over time and the model must take that into account. Noted here as a future topic, not developed in this brief.

## What This Asks For

1. **Model identity as a semantic knowledge graph** (the ontology and taxonomy of identity: classes, attributes, relationships).
2. **Capture the taxonomy of identity types** (corporate, physical, agentic, application, department, role).
3. **Treat the why-now as parallel to PKI** (the idea is old and good; LLMs, cloud scale, and ephemerality now make it practical).
4. **Compose the ontology of ontologies** (department, company, supplier, and application ontologies, nested; companies of companies; the supply chain).
5. **Capture the full authorization and authentication matrix** (not binary; products, company, department, interactions, flows, supply chain).
6. **Make assurance come from deterministic code over the graph** (queryable, repeatable navigation; not an LLM performing actions; the graph gives context).
7. **Reconcile differing definitions** (apples to apples; meaning through connectivity; the graph, not the label).
8. **Attach verifiable identity to graph nodes** (the reciprocal need; nodes linked to public keys; what the semantic web 1.0 lacked).
9. **Anticipate the explosion** (every node an identity with provenance; knowledge and identity as one fabric; identities-over-time as a future topic).

Estimated effort: substantial architecture, but on existing semantic-graph and PKI machinery (cross-ref: the semantic-graphs-for-skills, skill-as-projection, and foundations briefs); the contribution is the identity ontology, the authorization matrix, the determinism-for-assurance principle, and the reciprocal node-identity link. The proof is an identity semantic graph navigable deterministically by code, with nodes linked to verifiable keys, reconciling two differing definitions of identity.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a single definition of identity.** A graph that reconciles many definitions (ten experts, ten definitions).
- **Not binary authorization.** The full, complex authorization and authentication matrix.
- **Not LLM-performed assurance.** Assurance from deterministic code over the graph; the LLM helps build and navigate.
- **Not identity without verification, or graphs without identity.** The two are reciprocal; nodes link to keys.
- **Not the identities-over-time memo.** That is flagged separately.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Building the identity ontology is hard.** Ontologies are real work, and identity has no agreed definition. Mitigation: LLMs now make ontology-building feasible (the why-now); reconcile via the graph; start with the core identity types.

**Risk 2: The authorization matrix is genuinely complex.** Capturing full auth models is difficult. Mitigation: the graph is built for this complexity; start with the highest-value products and flows; deterministic navigation manages it.

**Risk 3: Determinism requires the code, not just the graph.** Assurance needs the deterministic navigation built. Mitigation: the English-to-code maturation (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief); LLMs help write the navigation; the graph gives the context.

**Risk 4: Every-node-an-identity could explode unboundedly.** The explosion is real and could overwhelm. Mitigation: identities scale (the PKI and ephemerality model; cross-ref: the foundations brief); apply verifiable identity where assurance is needed; ephemerality bounds it.

**Risk 5: Reciprocal identity-and-graph is ambitious.** Unifying the two is a large claim. Mitigation: deliver the identity graph first; attach keys to the nodes that need assurance; the unification is the direction, not the day-one deliverable.
