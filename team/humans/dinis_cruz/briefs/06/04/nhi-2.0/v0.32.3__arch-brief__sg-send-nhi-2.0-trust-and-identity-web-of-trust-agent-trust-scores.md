## What This Is

The next in the NHI 2.0 series: **trust and identity, the idea that trust, like meaning, comes through connectivity, and that a distributed web of trust, with trust scores earned over time, is how you deal with rogue identities and choose which agents to use.** It extends meaning-through-connectivity (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-semantic-knowledge-graphs and skill-as-projection briefs) into trust, builds on the chain of authenticity (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-PKI-foundations brief), the agent registry (cross-ref: the v0.31.12 three-agent-comms-demo brief), and trust over time (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-time brief). New contributions: **trust by connectivity, the multi-dimensional web of trust that makes rogue identities expensive, multi-party sign-off inside organisations, choosing agents by their declared capability, agent trust scores with the unproven-new-model problem, distributed (not centralised) identity, and earning trust over time.** OAuth-not-fit-for-purpose remains the separate memo flagged earlier; this brief is on trust.

## Trust By Connectivity

The anchor idea, paralleling meaning-through-connectivity: **the trust you place in an identity should be connected to the trusted nodes it is connected to.**

The project lead: **"connected to the whole meaning-through-connectivity, you could also have this idea of trust through connectivity. The trust you have in an individual, an agent, or a particular identity should be connected to the nodes it is connected to that you trust."** So just as a thing's meaning comes from its connections in the graph (cross-ref: the skill-as-projection and semantic-knowledge-graphs briefs), a thing's trustworthiness comes from the trusted things it connects to. Trust propagates through the graph: an identity is trusted to the extent it is connected to nodes you already trust. This makes trust computable from the same graph that carries identity and meaning.

## The Multi-Dimensional Web Of Trust

How an identity proves itself, extending the chain of authenticity (cross-ref: the foundations brief): **an identity provides a chain of trust, like TLS but better and more three-dimensional, not a single connection but a web.**

The project lead: **"how do you know this identity is correct? What the identity can provide is a chain of trust. The identity can say, here is the company I work for, here is the rest. It is like what we did with TLS, but in a much better way, and it could be a lot more three-dimensional, it does not just need to be one connection."** So an identity verifies itself by presenting a chain of trust (this key signed by the company, the company verifiable in turn; cross-ref: the foundations brief's chain of authenticity), but unlike TLS's single chain to a root, this is multi-dimensional: many connections, a web, not one line. The three-dimensional web is both stronger (more independent signals) and the basis for the rogue-identity defence below.

## Rogue Identities Become Expensive

The security payoff, and a strong one: **the web of trust makes rogue identities expensive, because a fake must replicate a whole web of connections, and no single compromised signal is enough.**

The project lead: **"this is how you deal with rogue identities, which we need to deal with: make it very expensive for them to exist in a meaningful way, because they need to replicate tons of connections, a web of trust that becomes almost impossible. Somebody can compromise one of your email addresses, but if the email address is not the only signal I use, maybe I also get something from the DNS record, the website, the company, then the compromise does not get them far."**

| Single-Signal Identity | Web-Of-Trust Identity |
|------------------------|------------------------|
| Compromise one email and you are in | One compromised signal is not enough |
| Cheap to fake | Expensive: a whole web must be replicated |
| One point of failure | Many independent signals |

So the web of trust defends against rogue and compromised identities by requiring many independent signals (the email, the DNS record, the website, the company): compromising one is not enough, and faking a whole credible web is almost impossible and very expensive. This is the multi-dimensional web turned into a defence, raise the cost of a fake identity beyond what an attacker will pay.

## Multi-Party Sign-Off Inside Organisations

The web applied internally: **inside organisations, a web of trust means transactions are signed off and verified by multiple identities, which prevents fraud, account compromise, and phishing.**

The project lead: **"I like this idea of the web of trust even inside organisations. You want to do a transaction, it needs to be signed off by multiple people, verified. A lot of business account compromise, fraud, transactions, and even phishing can be prevented and dealt with much more effectively if you have this web of trust."** So within a company, requiring multiple identities to sign off and verify a transaction (cross-ref: the PKI key-gated supply chain and the multiple-identities-chain briefs) defeats the single-point attacks that drive business email compromise, fraud, and phishing: no one compromised identity can complete a sensitive action alone. The web of trust is both an external verification model and an internal control.

## Choosing Agents By Their Declared Capability

A sharp, positive application to the agent registry (cross-ref: the three-agent-demo brief): **when an agent presents what its identity can do, you can reject it for asking too much privilege and pick the least-privileged agent that fits.**

The project lead: **"a nice positive way of doing this: an agent has an identity, and when it presents what the identity can do, we turn around and say, no, you can do too much, I do not want you to deal with this. In the example with an agent registry, where agents come online and you pick the one you want, maybe verified or certified, imagine one says, I can do anything to an inbox if you give me the credentials, and another says, I can only read these particular things, x, y, z. You can say, I do not want that agent, because it has too much privilege."**

| Agent A | Agent B |
|---------|---------|
| Asks for anything-to-the-inbox with credentials | Asks only to read specific things (x, y, z) |
| Rejected: too much privilege | Chosen: least privilege that fits |

So the agent registry (cross-ref: the three-agent-demo brief) becomes a place where you choose by declared capability: an agent presents the privilege its identity requests, and you pick the one that asks for the least while still doing the job, rejecting the one that asks for too much. This makes least privilege a selection criterion, you do not just constrain an agent, you prefer the agent that constrains itself, and it is a positive, transparent mechanism (the agent declares; you choose).

## Agent Trust Scores

A central new mechanism: **an agent earns a trust score from its execution history, quality, time in service, and the vouching of others, all on a cryptographic trail, and a brand-new agent with a better model is unproven.**

The project lead: **"trust can be how many times executed, how long it has been around, what other signals I can get to build a trust score for an agent, for a particular capability. This gets interesting with agents: this agent, with this model and these resources, has executed these tasks with a quality and satisfaction above 95 percent, and has been around x amount of time, so has earned credibility points in the formula. Now another agent turns up with a better, latest model, but it has not been tested, it has not gone through the motions, because by definition it just arrived. It could be better or worse, we do not know how it fails. All of this leaves a cryptographic trail, a hash we can connect to."**

| Signal | Contributes To The Trust Score |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| Times executed | Track record |
| Quality and satisfaction (for example above 95 percent) | Demonstrated performance |
| Time in service | Maturity |
| Vouching by others | Web-of-trust endorsement |
| The cryptographic trail | Every action leaves a verifiable hash |

So an agent has a trust score computed from verifiable signals: how often it has run, at what quality, for how long, and who vouches for it, all recorded on a cryptographic trail (cross-ref: the foundations brief's provenance and the marketplace-scoring brief). And a key nuance: a brand-new agent with the latest, better model is unproven, it has no track record, and we do not yet know how it fails, so it may be better or worse than a lower-spec but proven agent. Trust is earned through demonstrated behaviour, not granted on specifications, which is exactly why trust-by-connectivity and trust scores matter: they let you weigh a proven agent against a promising-but-untested one.

## Distributed, Not Centralised Identity

A principle the project lead flags as under-discussed: **identity must be distributed, not centralised, with verification drawing on many independent sources, though some centralization pockets exist.**

The project lead: **"a principle we have not talked about much is that identity also has to be distributed, it cannot be centralised in a lot of these ways. There are some centralization pockets, for example a company: I should be able to verify it because I can reference Companies House, government documents, registrars, a bunch of other places. Trust by connectivity is not just that I go to the company website, download the public key, and that is it. There are a lot of different ways to think about this."** So identity verification should be distributed: to trust that an identity is a given company, you draw on many independent sources (Companies House, government records, registrars, the company's own key), not a single authority. There are centralization pockets (authoritative registries like Companies House), but the trust comes from the convergence of many sources, which is the web of trust again, and which is what makes the model robust and hard to fake.

## Earning Trust Over Time

The resonant real-world parallel, now enforceable: **like a new hire who does not get the keys on day one, an identity earns the right to do things over time, and dynamic permissions let us actually enforce that.**

The project lead: **"some identities, and some agents connected to identities, will need to gain trust over time, to make sure they do not do certain things or behave in certain ways, or to meet a certain quality control. This happens in the real world: when somebody joins a company or a role, even if they will eventually have the keys to the castle and access to highly privileged information, they do not get it on day one. There is a learning, experience, and trust evolution, you earn the right to do a particular action. With identities, policies, dynamic permission sets, roles, and responsibilities, we can actually enforce this."**

So trust is earned over time, mirroring how organisations already work: a new joiner earns access as they gain experience and demonstrate trustworthiness, rather than receiving full privilege immediately. What is new is that identities, dynamic permission sets, and roles let us enforce this for agents and non-human identities (cross-ref: the time brief's evolution of privileges): an agent starts narrow and earns broader privilege as its trust score grows. The project lead cites a primitive, real example: some GitHub repositories already only accept submissions from a pre-approved list of committers, or from people accepted by a related project, a hard-coded, not-very-scalable, barely-PKI form of the web of trust. Done properly, with PKI, dynamic permissions, and trust scores, this becomes a general, scalable mechanism: it is this graph, the web of trust, that makes it work.

## What This Asks For

1. **Compute trust by connectivity** (trust propagates through trusted nodes, as meaning does).
2. **Build the multi-dimensional web of trust** (an identity presents a chain of trust; many connections, not one; better than TLS).
3. **Make rogue identities expensive** (many independent signals; one compromised signal is not enough).
4. **Use multi-party sign-off internally** (transactions verified by multiple identities; defeat fraud, account compromise, phishing).
5. **Choose agents by declared capability** (reject too much privilege; prefer the least-privileged that fits; the registry).
6. **Compute agent trust scores** (execution history, quality, time, vouching, on a cryptographic trail; treat new-but-better-model agents as unproven).
7. **Keep identity distributed** (many independent verification sources; centralization pockets like Companies House; convergence, not a single authority).
8. **Let identities earn trust over time** (start narrow, earn privilege; enforce with dynamic permissions; beyond the GitHub-committer primitive).
9. **Make the web-of-trust graph first-class** (it is the graph that makes all of this work).

Estimated effort: moderate-to-substantial, building on the chain of authenticity, the agent registry, provenance, and dynamic permissions; the contribution is trust-by-connectivity, the web of trust, trust scores, and earned trust. The proof is an agent chosen by declared capability, carrying a trust score from its cryptographic execution trail, verified through a multi-source web of trust, and earning broader privilege over time.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not single-signal trust.** A multi-dimensional web; one compromised signal is not enough.
- **Not specification-based trust.** Trust is earned from demonstrated behaviour; a new better model is unproven.
- **Not centralised identity.** Distributed verification from many sources; convergence, not one authority.
- **Not day-one full privilege.** Trust and privilege are earned over time, enforced by dynamic permissions.
- **Not the OAuth memo.** That remains the separately flagged brief.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Trust scores can be gamed.** Agents might farm a track record. Mitigation: the cryptographic trail and vouching (cross-ref: the foundations and marketplace briefs); multiple signals; the web makes gaming expensive.

**Risk 2: The unproven-new-model problem cuts both ways.** A better model may genuinely be better, but unproven. Mitigation: weigh proven versus promising explicitly; let new agents earn trust on low-risk tasks first (earning over time).

**Risk 3: Distributed verification is complex.** Many sources to reconcile. Mitigation: the web of trust and the semantic graph reconcile them (cross-ref: the semantic-knowledge-graphs brief); use centralization pockets where authoritative.

**Risk 4: Multi-party sign-off adds friction.** Requiring multiple identities slows transactions. Mitigation: apply to sensitive actions; the friction is the point for high-risk operations; tune by risk.

**Risk 5: Earned-trust onboarding must not block legitimate work.** New identities need to do something. Mitigation: start narrow but useful; earn broader privilege quickly with good behaviour; dynamic permissions grant just-in-time.
