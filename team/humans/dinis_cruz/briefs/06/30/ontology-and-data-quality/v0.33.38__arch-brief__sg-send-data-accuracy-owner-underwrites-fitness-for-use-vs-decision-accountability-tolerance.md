## What This Is

The distinction between two kinds of underwriting, and why the graph must carry both: **when someone submits a claim, a fact, or a measurement, it enters the graph and becomes the base of a cascade, facts to vulnerabilities to risks, so a great deal of weight ends up resting on specific facts; the person accountable for a decision underwrites its consequences, but that is a different act from the one this brief is about, the domain expert or evidence creator underwriting that the fact is true, was collected with enough granularity, and is fit to be used the way it is being used, and keeping that verified over time; the danger is that every traversal of the graph adds an abstraction layer, like zooming out on a map, so it becomes easy to load a piece of evidence with more weight than it can bear and use it beyond the tolerance it was collected for, which is the same failure as a module used in a way it was never designed for; rich decisions must therefore be underwritten by the people who actually know the maths and the tolerances, and facts must not be silently extrapolated across domains; and the point is foundational, because decision accountability is only legitimate if the data underneath it is correct.** It is the sixth brief of 30 June and the data-ownership follow-up flagged in the confidence brief (cross-ref: the v0.33.38 confidence brief, the v0.33.36 grounding-ladder brief, and the v0.33.35 risk-register propagation brief). New contributions: **the two-underwriting distinction, fitness-for-use, and abstraction-layer weight drift.**

## Two Kinds of Underwriting

There are two separate acts of underwriting, and the model needs both. The first is decision accountability: an executive putting their name to accepting a risk and owning its consequences, which the earlier briefs cover. The second is what this brief is about. The project lead: **"they need to underwrite from the point of view of, is this metric good enough to be used like that, and that is very important."** The domain expert who produced a fact underwrites that it is accurate and fit for the use being made of it. This is a factual-accuracy underwriting, not an accountability one. The project lead: **"this is from a factual accuracy base, not from accountability and decision point of view."** The two are different jobs done by different people, and conflating them is a mistake.

## The Fact Enters a Cascade

A submitted fact does not sit in isolation; it becomes load-bearing. The project lead: **"we have a whole cascading sequence of events that happens with the facts, which then lead to the fundamental vulnerabilities and lead to the risks, so you start to see that we are going to really put a lot of weight into specific facts."** Because the register is a cascade from facts up to risks, the accuracy of a single fact can carry an entire chain of conclusions, which is why the creator of that fact has to be tracked as a first-class part of the node, ideally as a signed attribution, so the graph always knows whose statement it is resting on.

## Abstraction Layers and Weight Drift

The core hazard is structural. The project lead: **"every time that you traverse the graph into an analysis, you introduce an abstraction layer, which is natural, because like in a map you start to zoom out and do not see the details, but the problem is that it is easy to then not put the right weight."** Each step up the graph loses detail, and with the detail goes the memory of how strong the underlying evidence actually was. The result is that a piece of evidence gets used for something it was never strong enough to support. The project lead: **"it is easy to then use a particular evidence for something the evidence is not strong enough to do."** And the people most likely to make that error are the ones furthest from the source, at the upper levels, who lack the domain expertise to know the evidence is being overloaded.

## Fitness for Use: The Granularity Problem

Accuracy is not only about whether a fact is true, but whether it is precise enough for the use being made of it. The project lead: **"there are situations where a metric exists but was not collected with enough granularity to make big decisions."** A measurement can be perfectly correct at the resolution it was taken and still be entirely unfit to anchor a major decision. So the owner underwrites two things: that the fact is true, and that it is fit for this particular use, and crucially keeps verifying both, because a fact that was fit yesterday may not be fit today.

## Never Designed to Be Used Like That

This failure has a signature that anyone who has run an incident post-mortem will recognise. The project lead: **"you talk to the people who developed the module, and invariably they will say, that was never supposed to be used like that, or this was never designed to be connected to external partners, this was a purely internal system."** The component was sound within its intended envelope and failed only because it was used outside it. The project lead offers the physical analogies: **"this screw was never designed to be used under this load, or this metal was never designed to evaporate like this, or this system was never designed to have this kind of load."** The fault is rarely in the part; it is in using the part beyond what its owner would have underwritten.

## Tolerance: Things Do Not Fail Linearly

The reason this is so easy to miss is that systems do not degrade smoothly. The project lead: **"things sometimes do not fall linearly, you can say this is a great system if you reboot it every day, or if you never exceed a number of requests, but as soon as you go a bit higher, no."** There is a tolerance, a set of constraints within which the system behaves and beyond which it does not, and the behaviour past the edge is often nothing like the behaviour inside it. The project lead: **"in physics there is a tolerance level, and if you are operating within those constraints you are good; the problem is those details sometimes get lost."** The constraints are exactly the detail that the abstraction layers strip away, so the graph must keep them attached to the fact.

## Underwritten by the People Who Know the Maths

The safeguard is to route rich decisions back to the experts who understand the tolerances. The project lead: **"it is critical that once you start making rich decisions, it has to be underwritten by the people who actually know the maths."** The model for this is the moment in Hidden Figures when, with the machine's numbers already in hand, the pilot insists that the mathematician personally verify them before he will fly. Having a computed answer is not the same as having a trusted one, and the person who can convert the former into the latter is the subject-matter expert who owns the underlying maths.

## Facts Do Not Travel Across Domains for Free

The same fact, moved into a domain it was not made for, quietly becomes false. The clearest public example is the ten-thousand-hour rule. Anders Ericsson and colleagues studied violinists at a Berlin academy in 1993 and found that the best students had accumulated, on average, around ten thousand hours of deliberate practice, a specific, expert-guided kind of practice, and that the figure was an average from which about half the group fell short. Popularised in Malcolm Gladwell's Outliers, it became a universal magic number, that anyone becomes an expert with ten thousand hours of practice of any kind, and Ericsson has since objected that this distorts the research. A precise finding about elite violinists and deliberate practice was extrapolated into a claim its owner would never underwrite. The project lead's rule follows directly: **"we need to keep going back to the person who actually should be underwriting the statements."**

## Why This Is Foundational: Accountability Needs Correct Data

The two underwritings meet at the end. The project lead: **"the only way we can make people who are making decisions accountable is if the data that they are operating on is absolutely correct."** Decision accountability is only fair, and only meaningful, if the facts beneath it are accurate and fit for use. Factual-accuracy underwriting is therefore not a parallel nicety; it is the foundation that makes decision accountability legitimate at all.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not decision accountability.** That is the executive's separate act; this is the expert's.
- **Not distrust of data.** It is structured trust, with the source and the tolerances attached.
- **Not blame of the builders.** The fault is usually the use beyond design, not the part.
- **Not a claim experts are always right.** It is a claim that the right underwriter must be asked.

## Honest Tensions

| Tension | Note |
|---------|------|
| Two underwritings | Keeping factual-accuracy and decision-accountability distinct is vital and easy to blur |
| Expert availability | Routing rich decisions to the owner is right but does not scale without the graph carrying the attribution |
| Fitness decays | A fact fit yesterday may not be fit today, so verification must be continuous, not one-off |
| Tolerances versus abstraction | The graph must keep constraints attached even as higher levels zoom out |
