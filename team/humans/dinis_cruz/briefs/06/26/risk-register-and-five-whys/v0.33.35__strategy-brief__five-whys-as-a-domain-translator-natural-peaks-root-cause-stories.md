# The Five Whys as a Translator Between Domains

**date** 26 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Architect, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief

## What This Is

The five whys, why asked repeatedly, understood as a translator that moves a statement from one domain into another and, by following the chain, reaches the natural peak of that domain: **a vulnerability is a fact with security implications, measurable and specific, and asking why it matters translates it into a risk, which is the business impact; you keep asking, not exactly five times but as many as it takes to reach the top of the domain, and because risk in an organisation forms a highly interconnected graph with a tree-like structure, the chain converges on natural peaks, the company's top risks and ultimately the single risk of staying in business, which is why a security leader can honestly give one number; the same chain can be aimed at any domain, asking why a CFO cares translates the same fact into the CFO's world, and the graph holds all these translation nodes at once, which the project lead believes is close to what happens inside an LLM, relationships and semantic mappings; the natural peaks appear again when following ownership up the org chart to the board, the owners, society, or the law; and the chain is just as powerful going downward, capturing the second, third, and fourth stories, the real root causes of why a vulnerability exists, no project, no funding, no business decision, no attack yet.** It is the mechanism underneath the risk register (cross-ref: the v0.33.35 risk-register brief and the facts-and-evidence work). New contributions: **the why-as-translator framing, the natural-peaks structure, and the downward root-cause stories.**

## The Technique

Take any statement, someone says they have a problem with something, or you pick a vulnerability, and ask why it matters. The project lead: **"the why is a great way to move from one domain to another, and to really translate it."** Each why is a step that carries the statement out of the domain it started in and into the next one, and the answer reframes it in the new domain's terms.

## Vulnerabilities Are Facts, Risks Are Business Impact

The first translation the why performs is from the technical to the business. The project lead: **"I tend to think about vulnerabilities as facts that have security implications, they should be measurable, a specific situation. The risks are when you start to talk about the business impact."** So a missing two-factor authentication is a vulnerability, a measurable, specific fact. Ask why it matters and you cross into risk: someone can log in unauthorised, someone can access data they should not, and those are risks because a risk carries an impact. The why is what moves you from the fact to the impact.

## Not Five, But As Many As It Takes

The number is not the point. The project lead: **"is it five? It should be as many as it takes, until you reach the top of that particular domain."** You keep asking until the chain reaches the natural top of the domain you are climbing, however many steps that takes.

## The Natural Peaks of the Graph

The reason the chain terminates is structural. The project lead: **"these are highly interconnected graphs that have natural peaks, because a tree-like structure eventually emerges. As you navigate upward on risk you arrive at the top risks of the company's register, and in fact at one top risk, the risk of operating the business."** So although the graph is densely interconnected, climbing the why-chain converges on a small number of peaks, and on risk it converges all the way to a single apex, the risk of staying in business.

## The One Number

This is what makes a single headline figure legitimate rather than glib. The project lead: **"sometimes you get the request, give me one number so we know if we are going up or down, and I do not have a problem with that, you should be able to have that number."** Because the chain converges on a peak, you can carry a number up to it; what the number means depends on everything beneath it, and the five whys is exactly what connects the dots from the detail to the headline.

## The Why Aimed at Any Domain

The same technique translates into whichever domain you choose. Ask why a CFO cares about a given fact, and the chain redirects the answer into the CFO's world and arrives at the CFO's peak. The project lead: **"the why lets you redirect the answer into specific domains, and the graph holds all these nodes and connections, because they create translations. I believe this is close to what happens inside an LLM, it is about relationships, semantic graphs of evidence."** The graph can hold every translation at once, a fact connected outward to its meaning in the security domain, the financial domain, the legal domain, and so on, and each why is a translation edge.

## Natural Peaks in Ownership Too

The same convergence governs responsibility, not just impact. Follow ownership up the org chart and you reach the top: the board members, or beyond them the owners, or beyond them society, or beyond them the law. The project lead notes that even standards and document structures organise themselves into these layers. So the why-chain that assigns who owns a risk climbs the same kind of tree to the same kind of peak.

## The Why Going Down: The Second, Third, and Fourth Stories

The chain is just as powerful aimed downward, into causes. The project lead: **"it is a great way to capture the root causes of why things are really happening, the second, third, and fourth stories."** Take the vulnerability: two-factor authentication is not enabled. Why? Because no one made the change. Why? Because the system does not support it, or there was no project for it, or it was not a requirement, or there was attrition, or there was no funding, or there was no business decision. Why? Because the company has not been attacked and has not felt the consequences. Why? Because there are not enough attackers, or it has simply been lucky. Each why uncovers a deeper story, and those second, third, and fourth stories are where the real causes live.

## Why This Matters

For the register, the five whys is the engine that does two jobs at once. Upward and sideways it is a translator, carrying a single technical fact into the risk register, into the CFO's world, into the board's, into the law's, and converging each on its natural peak. Downward it is an excavator, surfacing the real reasons a vulnerability exists. Both directions are edges in the same graph, which is why the register can be a graph of graphs rather than a list: the whys are the connective tissue.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a fixed count.** It is as many whys as the domain takes, not literally five.
- **Not only upward.** It excavates root causes as readily as it climbs to peaks.
- **Not single-domain.** The same fact translates into many domains at once.
- **Not a replacement for evidence.** Each step still rests on facts and evidence.

## Honest Notes

- **Peaks are a model.** Real graphs are messy; the tree-like convergence is a strong tendency, not a guarantee.
- **The one number hides a graph.** The headline figure is only as good as the chain beneath it, and should stay traceable to it.
- **The LLM parallel is a belief, not a claim.** The resemblance to semantic graphs is offered as intuition, not proof.
