## What This Is

The risk register for Risk Mandate.ai, built as a hyperlinked graph of graphs: **it is the semantic, ontology-driven risk register the project lead has always wanted to build, now buildable for real because the vaults supply the storage and hyperlink layer that was missing and PKI solves the rest; it does what security teams and products do not, it refuses to stop at the vulnerability and instead maps everything that comes after it, accepting the risk, funding the fix, and finding who does it, from simple cases to complex ones; phase one is facts only, real vulnerabilities that exist, so the risk already exists and must be accepted for an interval, never denied, with the five-whys drilling why it matters; every risk is validated by its technical owner but owned and accepted by its business owner, and it propagates up the chain to the CEO and the board, aggregating into a consolidated view; the model is recursive, because a fact such as the wrong person accepting a risk spawns a new governance risk, and any risk not connected to the register is an air gap; every change to any risk, fact, or piece of evidence must cascade to the top, or the register has an air gap and the business is deciding on bad data; and the blast radius is expanded through confidentiality, integrity, and availability, each spawning its own risks, a data breach and the ICO, salary tampering, missing backups, all looping up the chain; the whole thing is told as a narrative, a story played out through queries, timestamps, and commits, like a commentator calling a match or a whodunit.** It is carried end to end by one worked example, admin accounts without two-factor authentication (cross-ref: the v0.33.33 risk-acceptance-service and risk-acceptance-workflow briefs, the v0.33.34 personal-risk-acceptance brief, and the v0.33.32 agent-mandate-graph brief). New contributions: **the graph-of-graphs register, the facts-only no-deny phase, the cascading side-effect principle, and the confidentiality-integrity-availability blast-radius expansion.**

## Movement 1: The Vision and the Frame

The register is the risk register the project lead has wanted to build for years. The project lead: **"the way I always wanted to build a risk register is a hyperlinked graph, graphs of graphs, semantic graphs of ontologies, the kind we have built before for Jira and a few other places. Now we get to do it for real."** What makes it real now is the vault: the vaults give a storage layer in a much simpler and far more hyperlinked way than before, and PKI solves a whole set of other problems alongside it. Risk Mandate.ai is driven by agentic risks, and that framing turns out to match this older vision exactly.

The frame is what sets it apart. The project lead: **"a lot of security teams and products end on the vulnerability, and what I want to show is the multiple layers involved in fixing it, but even before that, in accepting the risk, funding the solution, and finding who is going to do it."** So the register begins where scanners stop. And this is precisely the kind of thing that becomes realistic with generative AI and agents in the mix, because the flows and the users can be simulated, and the LLMs can help maintain the interconnection of the whole graph.

## Movement 2: Facts Only, No Deny, and the Five Whys

Phase one has one hard rule: only real vulnerabilities, things that exist, facts, never hypotheticals. The root source has to be binary, partly because when the fix arrives it has to permeate the register and change everything downstream, all the way out.

The worked example runs the whole brief. System A has a vulnerability: a number of admin accounts do not have two-factor authentication. From that vulnerability the first risks map directly: the accounts could be compromised, and the system is exposed to credential-stuffing attacks because leaked or reused passwords can be tried at scale. The five-whys then drills the chain: why does it matter that passwords can be reused; because there are many password leaks; because an admin may have reused a password; because an attacker could then log in unauthorised; and so on, each answer a node.

Now the rule that defines the workflow. Because the vulnerability is real, the risk is real, and a real risk cannot be denied. The project lead: **"the mistake of a lot of risk registers is that they allow the risk to be denied, which can only happen when the risk has not materialised. Once the vulnerability exists, the risk exists to the business."** The probability may be low, but the risk exists. So the only move is to accept it, for an interval, one hour, four hours, a day or two, a week, a month, or six months, where the interval is set by how much urgency there is and how much money it takes to fund the fix. There is no point refusing to accept a risk and then not funding the fix; refusal without funding is the gap the no-deny rule closes.

## Movement 3: Propagation, and the Recursive Governance Risk

The first stakeholder is the technical owner of the affected accounts, the person who owns Active Directory, the sysadmin or IT director. The first question is whether it is even their risk to accept. Because every risk, once accepted, is given to and also accepted by the acceptor's boss, and so on up, every risk eventually reaches the CEO and the board. The board's risk register is the ultimate home; every risk ends up on a register. At the top the view is consolidated, but nothing escapes the chain.

The register starts empty. With no vulnerability there is no risk, and that is the baseline. The goal is never zero risk; the goal is to operate within the business's risk appetite, because risk is information the business needs to make decisions. The story is told by a series of links, a series of queries, each query advancing the scenario: start here, now we are at the IT director, the graph is this size, and so on.

Then the recursive twist, the move that makes this a graph of graphs rather than a list. A new fact is introduced: this risk was accepted by someone who should not accept it. This has to be handled at the level of evidence, because evidence can be challenged. So it begins as a proposition stated as a fact, this risk should not have been accepted by that person, which then requires evidence: the IT director validates that the vulnerability exists, but accepting the business risk is not their function, it is a business function. That can be argued, and the argument is where the opinion layer forms.

From that fact a new risk is born: gaps in accountability and governance. The project lead: **"any risk that exists in the business that is not connected to the risk register is fundamentally an air gap."** A register with air gaps is not effective, and the severity of each air gap then has to be assessed. This new risk goes high, to the head of GRC, governance, risk, and compliance, who must accept it because it exists, we have demonstrably assigned a risk to the wrong person. For how long depends on the action: to redo the mapping properly is a P1, so four hours. That acceptance then propagates, from the head of GRC to their boss the CIO, to the CEO, to the board, each accepting at four hours because that is the only option open to them. The result is the first full end-to-end flow: a low-level risk that was accepted, which created a second risk that travelled all the way to the top.

## Movement 4: Technical Owner versus Business Owner, and Cascading Side Effects

With the governance risk accepted for four hours and underwritten by the exec team, the original risk can be put where it belongs. It cannot sit with the head of IT, because IT is the technical owner: IT validates that the vulnerability exists, but the business risk belongs to the business owner. The project lead: **"most of IT should be technical owners of something, but the business owners are the ones that actually own the risk."** This distinction, technical owner versus business owner, is one the register has to hold explicitly.

So the impacted systems are mapped. The missing two-factor authentication affects an HR system whose product simply does not support it, it is not offered by the manufacturer. The graph grows the system and its owners: the HR system was commissioned by the HR director, who is the first-line business owner, whose boss is the Chief People Officer, whose chain reaches the CEO. The risk, that someone could log in as an admin, is accepted by the head of HR and then by the Chief People Officer.

Here the interval cannot be four hours, for two reasons that the register records as evidence. First, a fix is not technically possible in four hours, so that option is struck off, no amount of money or urgency buys it. Second, there is no clear and present danger, because there is no evidence the accounts are being compromised, and a new piece of evidence is added to the graph to say exactly that, the accounts have not been compromised, a fact that will matter again shortly. The interval then follows the funding: a funded project is a month or two, doing nothing is six months, treating it as something to fix in a couple of weeks is an incident, and demanding a fix in forty-eight hours is a P1 with the budget and resources that implies. Budget matters here because there is no good answer: the only mitigation is to put something in front of the HR system, and no good solution for that is currently in play.

This sets up the principle the project lead calls one of the most important in the whole register. The project lead: **"every time any risk, any fact, any evidence changes, you have to trigger a cascade that reaches the top. If you do not have that, you have an air gap, which means you do not have good data, and you cannot make good decisions."** Every change has a cascading side effect, a sequence of events that propagates upward; without it the register silently drifts out of date.

The cascade is then shown working in reverse, by closing a risk. Once the original risk is correctly re-assigned to the business owner, the condition behind the governance risk, that it was accepted by the wrong person, no longer holds. The evidence is gone, so that risk is removed, and its removal cascades up and clears it from the CEO's and the board's view. A risk appearing is a cascade up; a risk resolving is a cascade up too.

## Movement 5: Blast Radius through Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability

This is where most registers stop and where this one begins to do its real work. Do not stop at the accounts could be compromised; ask why it matters and what the business impact is. This stays fact-driven, with one important addition to what counts as a fact. The project lead: **"not knowing a fact is also a fact. Lack of evidence is also evidence, because then we say we do not know, and somebody needs to investigate until we do."** So a gap in knowledge is itself recordable, and it spawns its own risk: the full extent of the impact has not been captured, which means the risk is not yet correctly classified or correctly accepted. To capture it you look at the assets, the side effects, the possible attacks and attackers, and you connect to the threat agents in the model.

What drives the depth is what data the system holds. A full HR system, passports, salaries, bonuses, performance improvement plans, reviews, hires, fires, and dismissals, is a very different blast radius from anonymised timesheets. The way to expand it cleanly is the classic triad, confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

**Confidentiality.** How bad is it if this data is leaked to the world, exposed between employees, or accessed by someone who should not see it? If leaking the data puts the company in breach of GDPR, then it must be reported to the ICO, and two connected risks appear: a data incident, and a GDPR breach. There is a real argument that because GDPR requires data to be protected, the inadequate protection already constitutes a breach, which is why these are two distinct risks rather than one. These pull in more underwriters: a potential ICO fine brings in the CFO, and the prospect of not being GDPR compliant brings in the CEO. So the same risk, followed through confidentiality, reaches the board through a different door.

**Integrity.** Now run the scenarios for modification. What happens if salaries are modified, if performance data is altered, if someone can create employees and effectively hire people? What is the business impact of the data being wrong rather than merely seen? Each of these is its own risk.

**Availability.** What happens if the system is unavailable, or if trust in it is lost? This opens the backup chain, and each link is a risk. What backups exist, and how often, weekly backups mean a standing risk of losing everything between the last backup and the moment of compromise. And the sharper question, when was the last time a restore was actually tested? If the answer is never, that is a risk in its own right, we have never proven we can restore from backup.

Every one of these risks follows the same loop: validated by the technical owner who confirms the fact and the evidence, accepted by the business owner, and propagated up the business chain to the CEO and the board. That loop, repeated continuously as facts and evidence change, is the risk register.

## The Narrative Engine

The register is meant to be experienced as a story, not read as a spreadsheet. The project lead: **"I want to show this story played as a narrative, almost like a football commentator, this happens and then that happens, almost like a whodunit, like investigative journalism."** Technically this is done by replaying the change history, through timestamps, through commits, or through a series of queries, so the analyst can watch the register evolve: the vulnerability appears, risks propagate, a governance risk fires and then resolves, the blast radius blooms through confidentiality, integrity, and availability, and everything settles into the board's consolidated view. The commit log of the graph is the script, and the query is what advances the scene.

## The Demo

The end-to-end demonstration is the worked example played as that narrative: start from a register at zero risk; introduce the no-2FA vulnerability; watch the first risks map and propagate to the IT director; introduce the fact that the wrong person accepted it and watch the governance air-gap risk travel to the board and then resolve once the risk is re-assigned; re-home the risk with the HR business owners; and expand the blast radius through confidentiality, integrity, and availability until the full picture, GDPR and the ICO, salary tampering, untested backups, sits consolidated at the top.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a vulnerability scanner.** It begins where scanners stop, at the vulnerability.
- **Not hypothetical.** Phase one is facts only, real vulnerabilities that exist.
- **Not a deny gate.** A real risk cannot be denied, only accepted for an interval.
- **Not a static spreadsheet.** It is a live graph whose changes cascade to the top.

## Honest Tensions and Open Questions

| Question or tension | Note |
|---------------------|------|
| Facts only is phase one | Probabilistic and hypothetical risks come in a later phase |
| The narrative mechanism | Timestamps, commits, or queries; to be decided technically |
| Detecting air gaps | Finding risks not yet connected to the register is itself hard |
| Combinatorial blow-up | The confidentiality, integrity, and availability expansion must be curated, not exhaustive |
| Owner mapping | Technical and business owners must be modelled accurately for the chain to hold |

## Acceptance Criteria

1. A vulnerability is recorded only as a fact, something that exists, with a binary root source.
2. Risks derive from the vulnerability and are linked in a graph, with five-whys chains.
3. No risk can be denied; each is accepted for an interval set by urgency and funding.
4. Each risk is validated by a technical owner and accepted by a business owner.
5. Acceptance propagates up the chain to the CEO and the board, aggregating into a consolidated view.
6. A change to any risk, fact, or evidence triggers a cascade to the top; the absence of one is an air gap.
7. Resolving a risk cascades upward and removes it from higher views.
8. Blast radius expands through confidentiality, integrity, and availability, each spawning linked risks.
9. The history can be replayed as a narrative through timestamps, commits, or queries.
10. The register is stored in the vault graph, with provenance on every change.
