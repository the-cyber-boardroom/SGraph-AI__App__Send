# Learning From Games: World Models for the Risk Simulation

**version** v0.33.35
**date** 26 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Architect, @Dev, Product
**type** Strategy brief

---

## What This Is

The game-design and world-model approach to the digital-twin risk simulation: **the digital twins, world models, company models, and behaviours can be assembled into a simulated world, powered by a vault that holds the capabilities and by agents that take actions, so the whole dynamic of risk and its evolution, including attacks, can be explored, especially when connected to MITRE ATT&CK and attack trees; the model to borrow from is the strategy game, a Civilization-style world where a company operates, with an org chart, actions, risks taken, compromises, and clients buying, built from mini agents; the vault is what makes this powerful, because every action is version controlled and every connection is mapped by folders and files without a database, a showcase of serverless databases that still answer queries efficiently; LLMs are good at generating worlds and games, and game-design architecture, which is built on a world model, is the explicit analogy, with the cybersecurity reality layered on top; and the goal is to represent reality so the risks can be applied on top of it and connected up.** It is the simulation engine and the demo for the register (cross-ref: the v0.33.35 digital-twins and risk-register briefs). New contributions: **the world-model framing, the game-design analogy, and the vault as a serverless simulation engine.**

## The Framing

This is about learning from games. The working name may improve, but the idea is to take the digital twins, the world models, the company models, and the behaviours, and build a living world from them. It is the natural next step from the twin model: once anything can be a twin, a whole world of twins can be run.

## A World Powered by Vaults and Agents

The world is not static; it is played. The project lead: **"we could create these interesting world models, powered by a vault, and especially powered by agents that can be doing things."** With agents taking actions inside it, the world lets you explore the dynamics that a static register cannot show. The project lead: **"it lets us explore the whole dynamic of the risks and the evolution of what happens, including with attacks, and if you connect it with MITRE and attack trees you get some interesting scenarios."** So the simulation becomes a place to watch risk evolve and to play attacks through, grounded in the established attacker frameworks.

## The Civilization Analogy

The shape to aim for is a strategy game. The project lead: **"a bit like the strategy game Civilization, you want to show a company operating, the org chart, the actions, pick an industry and run with it, and then risks are taken, and compromises, and clients buying things."** You choose an industry, stand up the company with its org chart, and let the world run: actions happen, risks are taken, compromises occur, clients buy. The actors are mini agents, each doing its part, and the whole thing behaves like a turn-based world rather than a spreadsheet.

## The Vault as a Serverless Database

This is where the vault stops being storage and becomes the engine, and a showcase in its own right. The project lead: **"this is where the vault becomes super powerful, because every action is version controlled, everything creates a connection, all mapped by folders and files, without databases, a great example of serverless databases where you still get the queries and the efficiency."** Every action the world takes is a versioned change, every relationship is a link in the folder-and-file graph, and there is no live database behind it, yet the world is still queryable and efficient. The simulation is therefore also the strongest possible demonstration of what the vaults can do.

## Game Design as the Architecture

The discipline to borrow from is game design. The project lead: **"the LLMs are very good at creating these things, and at creating games, and this is like creating a game."** And the analogy is exact. The project lead: **"that should be the analogy, game design architecture works on a world model, so let's leverage that thinking and apply the reality of our cybersecurity digital world on top of it."** Game engines already solve how to represent a world, its entities, their state, and their interactions, so the build can lean on that mature thinking and add the cybersecurity layer, the vulnerabilities, the risks, the attackers, on top.

## The Point: Represent Reality, Then Apply Risk

For all the game framing, the purpose is sober. The project lead: **"ultimately we need to represent reality, so that we can apply the risks on top of it, and then connect it up."** The world model is not entertainment; it is a faithful representation of a real organisation onto which the risks attach and through which they connect. This keeps it tied to the discipline of reality from the twin model: the world is made of real, existing things, and the game is how you make them legible and explorable.

## Why This Matters

A playable world model does two jobs at once. It is the most compelling possible demo, because a stakeholder can watch a company operate, see an attack unfold, and see the risk cascade to the board in real time. And it is the simulation engine the register needs, the place where risks of risks and the consequences of actions can actually be run rather than merely described. The vault makes both possible without standing up any database infrastructure.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not entertainment.** The game framing serves a faithful model of reality.
- **Not speculative.** The world is built from real, existing things, as in the twin model.
- **Not database-backed.** It runs on the vault, version controlled, as a serverless graph.
- **Not the final name.** The world-model framing is a working name, open to improvement.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the game framing can drift from reality.** A fun world is not automatically a true one. Mitigation: hold to the discipline of reality, only real things enter.

**Tension 2: simulation depth versus fidelity.** A rich world can outrun how well its twins are connected to reality. Mitigation: surface low connectedness as a tracked gap.

**Tension 3: scope.** A world model can expand without limit. Mitigation: pick one industry and one use case, and grow from there.
