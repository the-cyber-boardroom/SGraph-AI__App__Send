## What This Is

The positioning companion to the protocol-harness-workflow FAQ (cross-ref: the v0.31.12 sgraph-protocol-harness-or-workflow FAQ). The FAQ answers what SGraph is *not* (not a coordination protocol, harness, or workflow) and where it sits (the substrate beneath all three). This brief makes the positive claim the FAQ only gestures at: **SG/Vault is a platform of primitives, like a cloud hyperscaler, that commoditises the user experience so that any kind of workflow, agent, or system can run underneath it, and on which use cases, products, and whole companies can be built.** It draws together the Wardley-style commoditisation argument, the bottom-middle-top model, the backend-less-but-reaches-any-backend property, the restrict-to-unleash flywheel, and the rare advantage that the team building the platform is also the team using it.

## The Claim: A Platform, Not A Category

The instinct to ask whether SGraph is a protocol, a harness, or a workflow (cross-ref: the FAQ) is the instinct to find its category. The more useful frame is that it is not in any of those categories because it is a **platform**: a set of primitives that those categories are built from. The right comparison is a cloud hyperscaler. A hyperscaler is not a database, a queue, or a function; it is the platform of primitives (storage, compute, identity, networking) from which databases, queues, functions, applications, and entire companies are built, some primitives layered on others. SG/Vault is the same kind of thing for shared state: a small set of primitives (an encrypted vault, version control, provenance, access control, vault-in-vault, connectors, rendering) from which coordination protocols, harnesses, workflows, experiences, and companies are built.

| Cloud Hyperscaler | SG/Vault |
|-------------------|----------|
| Primitives: storage, compute, identity, networking | Primitives: encrypted vault, version control, provenance, access control, vault-in-vault, connectors, rendering |
| Some primitives built on others | Some primitives built on others (e.g. vault-in-vault on the vault; public preview on the read-only key) |
| Services, apps, and companies built on top | Protocols, harnesses, workflows, experiences, and companies built on top (cross-ref: the base-vault, skills, blast-radius, and spreadsheets briefs) |
| You do not ask "is AWS a database" | You should not ask "is SGraph a workflow" |

So the category question dissolves once the platform frame is adopted: a platform is, by design, the thing many categories are built on, and asking which single category it belongs to is the wrong question.

## Commoditising The Experience (The Wardley Move)

The strategic heart of the positioning: **SG/Vault commoditises the user experience so that everything underneath can be whatever the use case needs, including manual steps.**

In Wardley terms, the platform pushes the value chain down the map: it takes the parts that would otherwise be custom-built and bespoke (storage, sharing, provenance, access control, rendering, coordination) and commoditises them into reusable primitives, so the only thing left to be custom is the top line, the experience, which is exactly where the differentiated value for a specific use case lives. The result is that the top can be highly focused and bespoke (a clinical tool, a blast-radius viewer, a pen-testing delivery) precisely because everything beneath it is commoditised and shared.

| Layer | What Lives There | Custom Or Commoditised |
|-------|------------------|------------------------|
| **Top: the experience** | The bespoke UX for a specific use case | Custom (this is the differentiated value) |
| **Middle: the machinery** | Agents, harnesses, workflows, protocols, and manual steps | Mix-and-match per use case |
| **Bottom: the substrate** | The vault primitives (state, version control, provenance, access control) | Commoditised (shared by everything) |

Two consequences worth stating. First, the middle layer is deliberately heterogeneous: it can be agentic, it can be a conventional workflow, and it can be manual, and the platform does not care, the commoditised bottom and the bespoke top do not need to know which. Manual steps are a supported part of the middle, not a deficiency. Second, this is why the same platform serves a clinical workflow, a security viewer, and a consulting delivery without being rebuilt for each: only the top line changes.

## The Two Views (And Why The Vault Is Invisible)

The platform frame explains the two views from the FAQ (cross-ref: the FAQ). From the top, the vault is **invisible**: the user sees a bespoke experience and no vault at all. From the side, the platform view, the layers are visible: the experience on top, the heterogeneous machinery in the middle, the vault primitives at the bottom. Both are true at different altitudes, and the invisibility is the point, a commoditised platform should disappear beneath the experience it enables, exactly as a hyperscaler disappears beneath the app running on it.

This also resolves the apparent tension between "the power is that you do not see the vault" and the existence of a vault browser. The vault browser is the **builder's view**, the power-and-development surface where the primitives are visible and composable; the shipped experience is the **invisible-vault view**, where the user sees only the product. The platform serves both: it gives builders the primitives and the visibility to compose them, and it lets the resulting experience hide the vault entirely. The emphasis on enablement matters: the vault browser is not just for inspection, it is where developers wield the primitives to build, which is the hyperscaler relationship (the console and the APIs are how you build on the platform).

## Backend-Less Substrate, Any-Backend Reach

A precise property that makes the platform claim hold (cross-ref: the FAQ's Q4): **the vault and the assets it holds have no backend, while the workflows built on it can reach any backend.**

The vault itself, and the assets inside it such as Vault Apps, are client-side and as simple as file storage; there is no backend business logic in the vault (the sgit model has no server-side computation, which is what pushed everything to the client and made the environment powerful). But the platform freely enables workflows that call out, from the vault experience, to systems that do have backends: an LLM, a security SaaS such as a blast-radius product, any API or SaaS. So the substrate stays simple and backend-less, and the power comes from what the workflows on top reach. This is the hyperscaler pattern again: the primitive is simple and uniform; the richness is in what is composed on it.

## The Restrict-To-Unleash Flywheel

A property the platform makes possible, and a counterintuitive one worth naming (cross-ref: the blast-radius briefs): **the more tightly the platform scopes an agent, the more freedom that agent can safely be given.** Least-privilege boundaries, vault-to-vault permissions, and constrained write zones contain the blast radius of any agent, which is precisely what makes it safe to let the agent act with greater latitude. The Email-FS-lite single-writer model (cross-ref: the FAQ's case study) is a concrete instance: each agent writes only in its own zone, which both prevents conflict and lets the agents run freely. Restriction is the enabler of capability, not its opposite, and a platform that makes scoping cheap and precise is a platform that can safely host powerful, autonomous workflows.

## The Rare Superpower: The Builders Are The Users

A genuine and uncommon advantage in how this platform is being developed: **the team building the platform is also the team using it, and the feedback loop is visible in the product.**

Most platform companies build primitives and then wait to learn how others use them. Here the same team builds the platform and builds on it, which means the platform is shaped by its own demanding first customer in real time. The evidence is concrete: many of the most recent SG/Vault features were added specifically to make Vault Apps work and to make them more powerful, the usage drove the primitives. The Email-FS-lite coordination the agent team runs on (cross-ref: the FAQ's case study) is another instance: the team coordinating through the vault is the team building the vault. This tight loop is rare and valuable: the primitives are validated by real use as they are built, not in theory, and the platform earns its "built on sound technology" claim (cross-ref: the v0.31.9 investment-strategy brief) by being its own most exacting user.

## Why This Matters

The platform-of-primitives frame is the right one for several audiences at once:

- **For a technical evaluator** (cross-ref: the FAQ): it answers the category question by dissolving it, SGraph is the platform layer, so the protocol-harness-workflow question is exactly what you would expect to ask of a platform.
- **For a builder**: it says what you can do, build your use case on the primitives, with a bespoke top line and whatever machinery you need underneath.
- **For an investor** (cross-ref: the investment-strategy brief): it is the foundational-platform-play thesis, a platform that enables others, on which use cases and companies are built, validated by being its own first user.
- **For a partner or customer**: it explains why the same platform serves clinical, security, consulting, and other use cases without being rebuilt.

## What This Asks For

1. **Adopt the platform-of-primitives frame** (a hyperscaler for shared state; primitives, some built on others; the category question dissolves).
2. **Commoditise the experience (Wardley)** (push the value chain down; the bottom commoditised, the middle heterogeneous, the top bespoke; manual steps supported).
3. **Support and explain the two views** (the invisible-vault experience and the builder's platform view; the vault browser as the enablement surface).
4. **Hold the backend-less-but-any-backend property** (the vault simple and client-side; workflows reach any backend they need).
5. **Make scoping cheap and precise** (the restrict-to-unleash flywheel; least privilege, vault-to-vault permissions, write zones).
6. **Use the builders-are-the-users superpower** (the feedback loop; features driven by real use; the platform as its own first customer).
7. **Frame for each audience** (evaluator, builder, investor, partner).
8. **Produce the library page and example** (the page explains the platform thesis; the existing Vault Apps and the Email-FS-lite team are the living examples).

Estimated effort: this is positioning, not a new build; the primitives exist and the examples are already running (Vault Apps, Email-FS-lite, the use-case briefs). The contribution is the platform frame and its articulation.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a category.** It is the platform many categories are built on.
- **Not a heavy top line.** The experience is commoditised down to bespoke simplicity; the machinery hides underneath.
- **Not a backend.** The vault is backend-less; workflows reach backends.
- **Not theoretical.** The builders are the users; the primitives are validated in production.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: "Platform" can sound abstract.** A platform claim without concrete enablement is hollow. Mitigation: the running examples (Vault Apps, Email-FS-lite, the use-case briefs); the hyperscaler analogy; the builder's view shows the primitives.

**Risk 2: Commoditising the experience must still leave room for differentiation.** Mitigation: the differentiation is the top line (the bespoke experience); commoditising the bottom is what frees the top to differentiate.

**Risk 3: The backend-less claim must stay scoped.** Mitigation: it applies to the vault and its assets; workflows reach real backends; state it precisely (cross-ref: the FAQ's Q4).

**Risk 4: The builders-are-users loop must keep producing primitives others want.** Mitigation: the loop is the strength, but watch that the platform stays general, not over-fitted to internal use; the use-case briefs (clinical, security, consulting, spreadsheets) keep it broad.
