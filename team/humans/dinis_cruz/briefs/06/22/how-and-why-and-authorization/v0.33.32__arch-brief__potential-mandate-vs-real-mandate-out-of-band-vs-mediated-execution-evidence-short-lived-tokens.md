# Potential Mandate Versus Real Mandate: Splitting The Right To Ask From The Means To Act

**version** v0.33.32
**date** 22 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Architect, Security, Strategy, @Dev
**type** Arch brief (in the series)

---

## What This Is

A variation on the moment-of-authorisation model that splits the authorisation to ask from the means to act: **because every decision after a privilege is assigned is hope-based, the centre of control is the moment the privilege is assigned, and this brief introduces giving a potential mandate, an authorisation to request a permission, rather than the real mandate, the credential itself, so the holder is authorised to ask for an action but does not yet hold the means to perform it; the plain credential-distribution model works fully out of band with no code changes, which matters, while the potential-mandate model assumes the executing code cooperates and goes to request the real mandate at the moment of use, which re-opens a just-in-time control point where evidence can be supplied and the request mediated by a short-lived token, a synthetic or simulated execution through a digital twin, or a third party that holds the credentials and executes on the requester's behalf, optionally gated by delegation certificates checked centrally; it separates three capabilities that are usually merged, to execute, to map the meaning and side-effects, and to decide; and it yields real-time monitoring of how privileges are used.** It extends the authorization vocabulary into an execution model (cross-ref: the v0.33.31 moment-of-authorisation and authorization-ontology briefs, the v0.32.3 nhi-2.0 brief, the ephemeral-compute work, and the side-effects evidence brief). New contributions: **the potential-versus-real-mandate split, the out-of-band-versus-mediated tradeoff, and the mediation options.**

## The Framing

What the solution is about: **distributing privileges and mapping risks.**

The project lead: **"this solution is all about the distribution of the privileges and the mapping of the risks. Once the privilege has been assigned, every decision after that is a hope-based decision: you hope you can make the right call, that you have the right data to make the decision. We should not be fully relying on that, because we cannot fully control it. So the centre of control is the privilege being assigned. That is the moment of decision."** This restates the moment-of-authorisation principle as the ground for what follows (cross-ref: the moment-of-authorisation brief).

## The New Idea: Potential Mandate Versus Real Mandate

The variation: **give the right to ask, not the credential.**

The project lead: **"instead of giving the actual credentials, the actual mandate straight away, we could give a potential mandate. It says: you are authorised to ask for this, you are authorised to make this call, to delete this data, to access this data, but that does not mean that the entity, or whoever it gets delegated down to, has access to it now."** So the potential mandate is a standing authorisation to request an action, held separately from the real mandate, the credential or access that actually performs it. The two are usually the same thing; this splits them. The name may need work.

## The Crucial Tradeoff: Out Of Band Versus Mediated

The cost of the split, stated plainly: **plain credentials need no code changes; the potential mandate does.**

The project lead: **"this is important: if we just distribute credentials, just distribute normal permissions, the solution works one hundred percent out of band. It requires no code changes, no change to how things work, because credentials just get distributed. The potential-mandate idea assumes we can change the code being executed, so that the code understands at that moment that it needs to go and get the credentials, or make the request through a particular place."** So the plain model is the one that works everywhere with nothing to change, and the potential-mandate model buys a control point at the cost of requiring the executing code to cooperate. The first is the default; the second is the opt-in upgrade.

## The Moment Of Use: Evidence And Mediation

What the cooperation buys: **a just-in-time control point.**

When the code goes to exchange a potential mandate for a real one, the requester can supply evidence, and the request can be mediated. The project lead: **"at that moment you could introduce providing evidence: here is what I have, here is what I know, here is what I think it is, let me do this. And that is where it gets interesting."** The mediation options:

| Mediation at the moment of use | What happens |
|--------------------------------|--------------|
| Short-lived token | The requester is issued a token that expires quickly |
| Synthetic or simulated execution | A digital twin simulates the effect, for server-side checks |
| Third-party executor | A third party holds the credentials and executes on the requester's behalf, so the agent never holds them |
| Delegation certificate or hash | A delegation token checked centrally before execution |

The evidence step connects to the facts, hypotheses, and certainty model: the requester states what it knows and intends, and that is recorded and checked at the moment of use (cross-ref: the side-effects evidence brief).

## The Three Separated Capabilities

What the model pulls apart: **execute, map, and decide.**

The project lead: **"it matters who has the right to execute the action. We are defining almost three players: the capability to actually do something, the capability of mapping what that means in practice, and the capability of the one that decides whether to do it. These can be separate. In our plan we keep them separate, the agentic mandates are mapped offline, out of band, the side-effects and what the privilege means, whereas a lot of the time you mix the execution with where the decision sits."**

| Capability | Role |
|------------|------|
| Execute | Performs the action |
| Map | Maps what the action means in practice, and its side-effects |
| Decide | Decides whether it happens |

Usually execution and decision are merged in the same place; the model separates them, and maps the meaning out of band, so the decision is informed rather than hopeful.

## The OAuth Analogy And Granular Consent

Where this sits relative to today: **OAuth, with more levels.**

The project lead: **"imagine if OAuth had extra levels, where you could control more granularity. Google has started doing a bit of that, you cannot give access to all your photos or documents, you select which ones, which is good. But this is more interesting: imagine the token you got, the AWS credential, the account you were given, is all potential. It says you could do this, you have the rights to do this, but you do not hold it yet."** So per-item consent, where platforms increasingly let a user grant access to selected items rather than everything, is a step in this direction, and the potential mandate goes further, making the grant itself a right to ask rather than a holding.

## The Simulated Endpoint Option

A further mode: **a proxy that simulates the real service.**

The project lead: **"you could have a mode where a proxy simulates, for example, an AWS endpoint, like the digital-twin work, so you think you are talking to the real thing but you are not."** So on the digital-twin pattern, the caller can be given what looks like the real service while the request is actually intercepted, simulated, and checked, which is one way to realise the synthetic-execution mediation above.

## Why This Matters: Real-Time Monitoring

The payoff, teased for the next topic: **splitting potential from real gives live control.**

The project lead: **"the power of this is that it gives us real-time monitoring, real-time control of the use of the privilege, much better data on who is asking for what, who is controlling what, and better ways to detect the impact of the token over time."** So beyond the just-in-time control point, the split turns privilege use into an observable stream, which is the subject of the next brief.

## What This Asks For

1. **Keep the out-of-band credential model as the default**, since it needs no code changes.
2. **Offer the potential-mandate model as an opt-in upgrade** where the code can cooperate.
3. **Define the exchange at the moment of use**, with an evidence step.
4. **Support the mediation options**: short-lived token, synthetic execution, third-party executor, delegation certificate.
5. **Separate execute, map, and decide** as distinct capabilities.
6. **Capture the monitoring stream** the split produces.

Estimated effort: significant; the out-of-band model is the near-term default, and the potential-mandate model is a platform-maturity feature.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a replacement for the out-of-band model.** It is an opt-in upgrade alongside it.
- **Not zero-code-change.** It assumes the executing code cooperates.
- **Not a single mediation.** Several modes are offered.
- **Not the monitoring brief.** That is the next topic.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: it gives up the zero-code-change property.** The plain model's strength is that it changes nothing. Mitigation: keep it the default; offer the potential mandate only where cooperation is possible.

**Tension 2: a third-party executor is a new trust dependency.** Holding everyone's credentials centralises risk. Mitigation: it is one option among several, chosen per workflow.

**Tension 3: simulated execution can diverge from reality.** A digital twin is only as good as its fidelity. Mitigation: use it for checks, not as the source of truth.

**Tension 4: the name.** Potential mandate and real mandate may not be the right terms. Mitigation: treat them as working names.
