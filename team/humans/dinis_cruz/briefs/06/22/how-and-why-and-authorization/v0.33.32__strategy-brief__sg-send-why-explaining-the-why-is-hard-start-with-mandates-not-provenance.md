# Why Explaining The Why Is So Hard, And Why We Start With Mandates

**version** v0.33.32
**date** 22 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Architect, Security, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (positioning)

---

## What This Is

The reasons explaining why an agent did something is so hard, and why that underpins starting with mandates rather than the why: **the platforms do not help; we still do not understand how the models reach decisions, the models rationalise after the fact like the classic tacit-knowledge experts who could act but not explain, they are non-deterministic and exposed to prompt injection, and the decision, execution, and business-logic platforms expose neither the logic of why something should happen nor a good mapping of the privileges an action needed; provenance is generations away and needs semantic knowledge graphs, ontologies of ontologies, control-flow graphs, codified rules, and all the evidence preserved in a temporal state, which SG/Vault can do but few attempt; understanding the why also needs the business context most organisations have never formalised, which is why the context-graph idea faded; the more privileges an agent has, the harder it gets; and pursuing the why forces a solution in line at the centre of the data, a single point of failure and a data-sovereignty problem, a bad place for a startup; the irony is that once you understand the why well enough you can turn it into deterministic code, which removes the explainability problem; so start with mandates, which are binary, objective, and reverse-engineerable, since if an action was possible someone allocated the privilege.** It is the deeper justification for the how-not-why scope (cross-ref: the v0.33.32 how-not-why brief, the v0.33.40 graphs-of-graphs brief, the v0.32.3 nhi-2.0 brief, the v0.33.28 run-everywhere brief, and the v0.33.31 moment-of-authorisation brief). New contributions: **the why-is-hard analysis, the in-line single-point-of-failure argument, and the deterministic-code irony.**

## The Framing

The question, and the short answer: **why is this so hard, and the platforms do not help.**

The project lead: **"why is it so hard to explain why an agent did something? The fundamental reason is that our platforms do not help. In the simple scenarios it is easy, but those are the scenarios where we do not need it, because they have easy inputs and easy outputs."** So the cases where explanation is easy are the ones that do not matter, and the hard cases are exactly where the platforms fall short.

## The Models Are Opaque

The first reason: **we do not know how the models decide, and they rationalise after the fact.**

The project lead: **"we still do not have a good understanding of how the models reach decisions, how they reason on the inside, the mental models or decision trees they form."** He uses the tacit-knowledge analogy: **"there are those stories, the sexing of the chicks, and the spotting of wartime bombers at the coast. People could do it, but when they tried to explain how, they could not. Only when someone sat next to them guessing, and got corrected, did they get better and better. We have the same issue with the models: when a model explains how it reached a conclusion, it is almost post-conclusion, it rationalises how it got there."** So the models act on something we cannot inspect, and their explanations are reconstructions after the fact, which introduces its own problems. On top of that, they are non-deterministic, and prompt injection makes the reality of what the model consumed hard to trust, worse the more data it consumes.

## The Platforms Do Not Expose The Logic

The second reason: **the systems do not give us the why or the privileges.**

The project lead: **"the platforms we use, the decision platforms, the execution platforms, the business-logic platforms, do not give us the logic of why something should happen, and most platforms also do not give a good mapping of the privileges needed to do a specific action."** So even setting the model aside, the surrounding systems do not record the reasoning or the privilege path, which is much of why the why is unrecoverable.

## Provenance Is Generations Away

The third reason: **the proper solution is a long way off.**

The project lead: **"provenance is still a big issue to solve. We are a couple of generations away. The only way to solve it properly is once we have semantic knowledge graphs, ontologies of ontologies, control-flow graphs, and good definitions of rules, so that every micro-decision is captured. It also requires all the evidence used at that moment, across all the data points, to be captured and preserved in a temporal state, which with SG/Vault we have a chance of doing, but few people are."** So real provenance needs a stack we do not yet have, plus temporal evidence capture that the vault can provide but most do not, and it gets harder as the privilege universe grows (cross-ref: the graphs-of-graphs brief).

## The Why Needs Business Context That Is Not Formalised

The fourth reason: **the context simply is not there.**

The project lead: **"to understand the why, you have to understand the business context, why decisions are being made, and most companies do not have that formalised or well defined. A lot of the time it is codified only in software, or in particular human decisions. That is why the context-graph idea started with a lot of energy and then faded: the idea is great, but you just do not have the data."** So the why depends on context that organisations have rarely written down, which is why earlier attempts stalled; LLMs plus this capture approach finally make it tackleable, but it remains far off.

## Why The Why Is A Bad Place To Start

The strategic reason: **it forces you in line, as a single point of failure.**

The project lead: **"the why is an appealing question, customers start there, and many companies chase it, I have heard of ten myself, there must be dozens. It is an easy pitch at the beginning and very hard in reality. And the why requires the model to have access to all the data, to be the centre of the execution flow, in line, the bottleneck, the one that sees the data, which everybody wants because that is the biggest moat, but it is really hard, there are tons of competitors, and you become a single point of failure. Anything implemented in line is more dangerous, more sensitive, more problematic. And a lot of this data can never leave the organisation, or certain parts of it. A hyperscaler can try to play this game, because much of the data is already there; for a startup it is very hard."** So the why pulls you into the most contested, most dangerous, least sovereign position, which is a poor place for a startup to begin (cross-ref: the nhi-2.0 not-in-line brief and the run-everywhere sovereignty brief).

## The Irony

The twist that closes the case: **understanding the why turns into code.**

The project lead: **"the irony is that the more you understand the why, the provenance, and the control flows, the easier it is to transform those into code, into deterministic actions, and then you do not need the explainer, because once it is in code you have far fewer explainability problems."** So the destination of fully understanding the why is deterministic code, which removes the explainability problem altogether, which means the why-explainer is a waypoint, not an end.

## Why Start With Mandates

The conclusion: **mandates are binary, objective, and reverse-engineerable.**

The project lead: **"it is much better to start with mandates, because mandates are very binary, very objective, they exist or they do not. You can even reverse-engineer a privilege, because if something was done, somebody along the line allocated that privilege. The why of an agent, and the provenance of everything, is really hard."** So mandates are the tractable, factual foundation, and the why needs the core components, the permission allocation and the connection of assets and capabilities, built first (cross-ref: the moment-of-authorisation and how-not-why briefs).

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a dismissal of the why.** It is valuable, and part of the market wants it.
- **Not a claim it is unsolvable.** It is generations away, not impossible.
- **Not against in-line forever.** It is against starting there as a startup.
- **Not the competitor research.** That is the companion brief.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: the why is where customers look first.** Demand starts there. Mitigation: meet it with the foundation it needs, and position the why as later.

**Tension 2: in-line is the biggest moat and the biggest risk.** The strongest position is also the most dangerous. Mitigation: stay out of band first; revisit in-line only at maturity.

**Tension 3: we may be early.** The why may become tractable sooner than expected. Mitigation: the core we build is exactly what it will need.
