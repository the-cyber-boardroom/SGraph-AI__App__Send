# A Consumer-Redress Service For Dark-Pattern Subscriptions

**version** v0.33.38
**date** 16 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (service idea)

---

## What This Is

A service idea, following from the image-generation scam: **an evidence-based consumer-redress service that helps people caught by deceptive dark-pattern subscriptions (sign up for pennies, then charged pounds through fine-print monthly subscriptions), by gathering evidence and pursuing redress with the companies, the regulators, and the payment providers, leveraging companies' sensitivity to their brand reputation, with business models such as a percentage of the recovery or pay-per-attempt.** The market is large and growing as these scams proliferate, and the project lead notes he has most of the open-source technology to build it but does not want to build it now, so this is a model capture rather than a plan. It is the sibling of the other somebody-should-build-this service (cross-ref: the v0.33.27 agentic-compromise-incident-response-cleanup-service brief), follows the scam that prompted it (cross-ref: the v0.33.38 image-generation-opportunity brief), and embodies the anti-rent-extraction stance (cross-ref: the v0.33.26 sovereignty and open-source-success-paradox theses). New contributions: **the redress-service concept, the regulator-and-payment-provider leverage, and the business-model options.**

## The Problem

What the service addresses: **deceptive subscriptions that charge far more than the user agreed to.**

The project lead: **"companies are doing rent extraction, charging users per usage, or the deceptive, borderline-illegal thing of join for thirty pence and then charge thirty pounds. People are being ripped off, and the more vulnerable they are, the more sensitive it is, because a lot of people lose tons of money on subscriptions they never wanted or used, and the companies keep charging them."** So the harm is a dark pattern: a tiny advertised price hides a recurring charge, and the people least able to absorb it are hit hardest.

## The Service

What it does, in the project lead's words: **act on law and evidence, gather and submit data, and contact the companies on behalf of users.**

The project lead: **"this is a service that, based on the law and the evidence, starts submitting data and contacting the companies who are doing this. The idea is to create a service that helps users in these situations."** So the core is evidence-based advocacy: collect the evidence of the deceptive charge, and pursue redress for the user.

## The Leverage

Why it can work, beyond just asking: **evidence, plus escalation to regulators and payment providers, plus brand sensitivity.**

The project lead: **"there is power in providing evidence, and in being able to contact the regulators and even the payment providers, and saying, this is not a good customer that you have. A lot of them are sensitive to this, brand-wise, and they should be, so a lot of companies will have a positive interest in having a good brand reputation."**

| Lever | How It Works |
|-------|--------------|
| Evidence | A documented record of the deceptive charge |
| Regulators | Complaints to the relevant consumer-protection bodies |
| Payment providers | Flagging a merchant as a reputational and compliance risk |
| Brand sensitivity | Many companies prefer to resolve rather than be exposed |

## The Business Models

How it could be funded: **a percentage of the recovery, or pay-per-attempt, possibly sponsored.**

The project lead: **"you could have a business model based on getting ten percent of what was recovered, or pay-per-attempt, or pay-per-something, and maybe a small percentage of the money that got refunded. It is interesting to work out the business model, and there is even a service that could eventually be sponsored."**

| Model | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Percentage of recovery | Around ten percent of what is recovered or refunded |
| Pay-per-attempt | A fee per case pursued |
| Sponsored | Funded by a sponsor or public-interest body |

## The Market

Why it is timely: **the scams are proliferating and widely discussed.**

The project lead: **"there is a big market, especially because it is going to be more and more easy to create these kinds of services, and companies keep abusing this. A lot of people talk about this."** As it becomes trivial to spin up a dark-pattern subscription service, the volume of harmed users grows, and so does the demand for redress.

## The Technology

Why the project lead is well-placed to seed it: **most of the technology and open source already exist.**

The project lead: **"I have most of the technology and open source to do this, so they can just use a lot of the stuff I created."** The SGraph stack (vaults for evidence, agents for the workflow, semantic graphs for the evidence and the companies) maps onto the service, so a builder could reuse it (cross-ref: the company-intelligence-vault and agentic-content-website provenance work).

## The Meta

The framing: **somebody should build this; the project lead does not want to build it now.**

The project lead: **"somebody should build this. I do not want to build it right now, but this is the model of a service."** So this is a captured opportunity for a builder, not a current SGraph commitment.

1. **Capture the service model** (evidence-based redress for dark-pattern subscriptions).
2. **Define the leverage** (companies, regulators, payment providers; brand sensitivity).
3. **Work out the business model** (percentage of recovery, pay-per-attempt, sponsored).

5. **Ground it legally** (see the honest tensions).
6. **Decide if SGraph seeds it or a builder takes it** (the project lead does not want to build it now).

Estimated effort: this is a service concept, not a build; the next step would be the legal grounding and the business-model design.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a build now.** A model for someone to build.
- **Not legal advice.** It describes a service, not the law.
- **Not coercion.** Legitimate complaints and redress, not pressure for its own sake.
- **Not an SGraph product yet.** A captured opportunity.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: this is not legal advice, and redress mechanisms vary by jurisdiction.** Chargebacks, regulator complaints, and consumer-protection law differ by country. Mitigation: the service would need proper legal grounding per jurisdiction before operating; nothing here asserts what is lawful.

**Tension 2: the methods must stay legitimate.** Evidence-based complaints and redress are legitimate; pressure that crosses into coercion or extortion is not. Mitigation: keep the leverage to lawful complaints, regulator escalation, and payment-provider flags, with the evidence to back them.

**Tension 3: the business model may itself be regulated.** Percentage-of-recovery claims-management is regulated in some jurisdictions. Mitigation: check the regulatory status of the funding model where it operates.

**Tension 4: vulnerable users need care.** The people most affected are often the most vulnerable. Mitigation: handle their data and cases with particular care and clear consent.

**Tension 5: evidence quality matters.** Weak evidence undermines the redress and the credibility. Mitigation: rigorous, documented evidence (where the provenance and semantic-graph work helps).
