# PBoM Versus MBoM As The Core Name: An Applicability Analysis

**version** v0.33.30
**date** 20 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Product, Founders, @Dev
**type** Strategy brief (naming analysis)

## What This Is

An analysis of how well PBoM, the permissions bill of materials, and MBoM, the mandate bill of materials, work as the core name of the solution and even the company: **both work well as the name of an artifact or a standard within the bill-of-materials family, which supports the augment-existing-standards positioning, but neither is a strong name for the whole solution or for the company; PBoM is conceptually clean, because permissions are the kind of thing a bill of materials inventories, and it sits naturally beside the SBOM and VEX, though Policy SBOM is adjacent; MBoM has a hard collision, because MBOM is the long-established Manufacturing Bill of Materials in the very family we want to align with, and mandate bill of materials is a slightly forced construction, since a mandate is authority rather than materials; and as company names both are acronyms that are hard to say, to brand, and to trademark, and they name an inventory artifact rather than the whole solution, which is the mandate, the blast radius, and the risk acceptance, not just a list; the recommendation is to use PBoM for the artifact and the standard, keep Mandate as the conceptual frame, drop MBoM because of the collision, and choose a separate, brandable name for the solution and the company.** It assesses the two candidates against the naming work and the standards research (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 permissions-bill-of-materials and naming briefs, the v0.33.28 PBOM-adjacent-standards research, and the v0.33.30 ontology brief). Sources are listed. New contributions: **the applicability assessment, the MBOM-collision finding, and the artifact-versus-company distinction.**

## The Framing

What was asked: **assess PBoM and MBoM as the core name, for the solution and the company.**

The question is how applicable each is as the central name, both for the solution and potentially for the startup itself.

## The Two Candidates

What they are: **two ways to place the work in the bill-of-materials family.**

PBoM reads as permissions bill of materials, and MBoM as mandate bill of materials. Both deliberately echo the SBOM, placing the solution in the established bill-of-materials family, which is consistent with the strategy to augment existing standards rather than invent.

## The Criteria

What a core name has to do: it has to be accurate to what the thing is, distinct from existing terms, legible to the audience including non-technical execs, broad enough to cover the whole solution, and, for a company, brandable and trademarkable.

## PBoM Assessed

The case for, and against: **a clean artifact name, a weak company name.**

For: permissions are exactly the kind of thing a bill of materials inventories, so the metaphor fits cleanly; it sits naturally beside the SBOM and VEX; it is technically credible; and it directly carries the augment-the-SBOM positioning. Against: Policy SBOM is an adjacent term that could blur it; permissions is narrower than mandate, which the naming brief preferred as the fuller bundle; bill of materials is supply-chain jargon that a non-technical exec does not immediately parse; and it names the inventory artifact, not the whole solution, so it is weak as a company name.

## MBoM Assessed

The case for, and the disqualifying collision: **the richer word, the wrong acronym.**

For: mandate is the richer, preferred term, the bundle of identity, credentials, and business capability from the naming brief, and it ties to Agent Mandate. Against: MBOM is the long-established acronym for the Manufacturing Bill of Materials, a core member of the very bill-of-materials family the solution wants to align with, sitting beside the EBOM and the others, and used as a BOM type in CycloneDX, so MBoM would be actively confusing in exactly the domain it is trying to join. Worse, mandate bill of materials is a slightly forced construction, because a bill of materials inventories materials or components, while a mandate is authority; what you actually inventory is the permissions and capabilities the mandate contains, which is the PBoM. So the cleaner reading is that a mandate has a permissions bill of materials, not that there is a mandate bill of materials.

## The Comparison

| Criterion | PBoM | MBoM |
|-----------|------|------|
| Fit to the metaphor | Strong; permissions are the materials | Weak; a mandate is authority, not materials |
| Distinctiveness | Good; Policy SBOM is adjacent | Poor; collides with Manufacturing BOM |
| Family alignment | Clean beside SBOM and VEX | Conflicting inside the BOM family |
| Exec legibility | Jargon, but learnable | Jargon, plus confusing |
| Scope of the solution | Names the artifact, not the whole | Names the artifact, not the whole |
| Company and brand fit | Weak acronym | Weak acronym, plus collision |

## As A Company Or Startup Name

The harder test: **neither is a good company name.**

Both are acronyms, which are hard to say aloud, hard to make memorable, and hard to trademark and rank for, and a literal bill of materials names a technical inventory document, not a company. More fundamentally, the solution is broader than any inventory: it is the mandate, the blast radius, the risk acceptance, and the graph that connects them, and naming the company after one artifact inside it undersells the whole. Companies in this space tend to brand with a distinct name and treat the artifact as a feature or a standard they champion.

## The Scope Problem

The core issue underneath both: **the bill of materials is one component, not the whole.**

In the ontology, the bill of materials is the inventory of capabilities a mandate authorises; it is one entity among many, alongside the blast radius, the harms, the parties, and the risk acceptance. A core name drawn from that one entity describes a part, not the solution.

## Recommendation

The suggested split: **PBoM for the artifact, Mandate for the frame, a brand for the company.**

1. **Use PBoM as the name of the artifact and the proposed standard**, the permissions bill of materials that augments the SBOM. It is accurate, family-aligned, and technically credible there.
2. **Keep Mandate, and Agent Mandate, as the conceptual frame** for the bundle of authority, as the naming brief recommended.
3. **Drop MBoM**, because of the hard collision with the Manufacturing Bill of Materials inside the same family, and the forced construction.
4. **Choose a separate, brandable name for the solution and the company**, with the PBoM artifact and the Mandate frame living inside it. The final choice is the project lead's.

## Honest Notes

- **Naming is the project lead's call.** This is an applicability analysis, not a decision.
- **Not a trademark or legal opinion.** A proper trademark, domain, and handle search is needed before committing to any name.
- **The family alignment is a real asset for the artifact.** PBoM gains credibility precisely by sitting in the SBOM family; that same family is what makes MBoM collide.
- **A name can be grown into.** A clear artifact name and frame now does not preclude a strong company brand later.
