## What This Is

The next phase of the LinkedIn analysis vault, now that the vault exists and the first export is loaded: **process the network as a semantic knowledge graph, with graphs of graphs and ontologies of ontologies, to classify the data, surface signals and evidence of who to talk to, and drive a private daily outreach CRM, with a sequence of who to connect and message today.** It continues the LinkedIn vault (cross-ref: the v0.33.16 linkedin-export-analysis-vault brief), feeds the partner-outreach vault (cross-ref: the v0.33.16 partner-outreach-vault brief), and applies the semantic-graph and LETS work (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-semantic-knowledge-graphs and v0.33.16 artefact-driven-security-assessments briefs). Two related ideas are parked for their own briefs: richer LinkedIn data via browsers and proxies, and trade intelligence. New contributions: **the semantic-graph core, the signals-and-evidence analysis, the private outreach CRM, the daily-maintenance sequence, the look-later capture, and the GDPR analysis.**

## Status: The Vault Exists, The First Dump Is In

Where things stand: **the vault is created, the first export is loaded, and it arrives in two parts, contacts first and messages second, with the next phase being comments, messages, and reactions.**

The project lead: **"the vault is created. I added my first data dump, the latest one. LinkedIn sends this in two parts: the first has the contacts, the next has the messages. It is already a salad of stuff. The next phase is where you get the comments, the messages, and the reactions, which is very important, because I have also tagged stuff in there."** So the contacts are in, messages follow, and the richer phase (comments, messages, reactions, and the things tagged) is next, exactly the material the signals analysis needs.

## The Semantic Knowledge Graph As The Core

The central method: **process the network as a semantic knowledge graph, because it is the way to simplify the data, classify it, connect it, see what is missing, and read the signals.**

The project lead: **"semantic knowledge graphs are a way to simplify and process the data in logical ways. They let us classify things, a comment, a like, a job description, past work, connect the data, even see data we are missing, and have a much better understanding of what is going on. The signals are important. We need a way to distinguish between a like, a comment, a follow-up, a contact, and a message."** So the graph is the model (cross-ref: the NHI-2.0-semantic-knowledge-graphs brief): every interaction is a typed, classified node and edge (like, comment, follow, contact, message, job, past work), which makes the salad legible, reveals gaps, and turns raw interactions into readable signals. From a schema and mapping point of view it is the only way to get a real sense of what is going on and who to talk to.

## Graphs Of Graphs, And Ontologies Of Ontologies

The structure of the model, and the visualisation principle: **build graphs of graphs and ontologies of ontologies, domain-specific worlds, and visualise as graphs of graphs of graphs following the semantic web.**

The project lead: **"this might be an example of graphs of graphs and ontology of ontologies. From a visualisation point of view, think graphs of graphs of graphs, following the semantic web logic. I like creating these schemas, these domain-specific worlds, because a password manager company should be different from a secrets manager and a cloud provider."** So different domains get different ontologies (a password manager, a secrets manager, a cloud provider are modelled differently; cross-ref: the integration-landscape briefs' categories), composed into graphs of graphs, and visualised as nested graphs in the semantic-web spirit. The ontology-of-ontologies lets each domain world be precise while still connecting to the others.

## The Signals And Evidence Analysis

The killer use, proven before by hand: **map posts by topic to the people who liked them to where they work, to find hot leads, and build intersection graphs that show the evidence of why each person is relevant.**

The project lead: **"one signal I added that was really good was the people who liked my posts. I mapped the posts I did about a topic, then the people who liked them, then where they worked, and that gave a strong set of signals for people to reach out to. We need the intersection between the state of a person, the industry, a vault, SG/Send, and build rich graphs that map out the evidence, why each one is relevant to what, and which ones are hot."**

| Signal Chain | Yields |
|--------------|--------|
| Posts about a topic | What you are known for |
| People who liked them | Who is interested |
| Where they work | The company and industry |
| The intersection | Hot leads with evidence of relevance |

So the graph computes outreach intelligence: who engaged with what, where they sit, and therefore who is worth approaching and why, with the evidence attached (cross-ref: the partner-outreach-vault company and individual lenses and the artefact-driven brief's evidence-as-artefacts). A lot of these views will look like spreadsheets, which is fine; the data is extracted and derived (cross-ref: the webify-spreadsheets brief).

## The Private Outreach CRM

The second half, the part that always broke down: **a CRM layer tracking what happens after you reach out, replies, last contact, last conversation, and people from the same company.**

The project lead: **"the second part is more CRM-like, the problem I always had: I reach out to somebody, then what? Have they replied, what was the last time I reached out, the last conversation, and people from the same company. Another target is the investors I have connected with, I should go back to them and say, here is what I am doing."** So on top of the who-to-talk-to graph sits an outreach CRM (cross-ref: the v0.33.2 shipping-vault base CRM): per-contact state (reached, replied, last contact, last conversation), grouped by company, including warm groups like the investor contacts to re-approach. This closes the loop the spreadsheets never did.

## A Private Workflow

An important distinction from the partner-outreach vault: **this is a completely private workflow, not a public vault.**

The project lead: **"remember this is a good example, it is a completely private workflow for me, this is not a public vault in this case."** So unlike the partner-outreach vault, which had a publishable company pitch (cross-ref: that brief), this vault is private throughout: it holds the project lead's own network intelligence and CRM, and none of it is published.

## The Daily-Maintenance Sequence

What makes it usable: **a daily sequence, the five people to connect, the five connection requests, the ten messages to send today, with copy-paste text even before any automation.**

The project lead: **"figure out the sequence of events. What are the five people I should connect next, the five I should send a connection request to, the ten messages I should send today, so every day I do a bit of maintenance and the process becomes scalable and usable. Even without a LinkedIn automation workflow, just having the text I copy and paste into LinkedIn is powerful. I just went to a conference and have all these people to follow up. We can add more integration later."** So the vault produces a daily worklist (who to connect, who to request, what to message) with ready copy-paste text, which is valuable on its own even with no LinkedIn automation, and integration can come later. This turns a daunting backlog (for example, post-conference follow-ups) into a small daily routine.

## Capturing Look-Later Posts, The Mini-Website, And Republishing

Three content-recovery jobs: **recover the posts marked to look at or infographic later, visualise the hyperlinked mini-website of posts, and republish the relevant posts elsewhere, grabbing media the export may omit.**

The project lead: **"I tag or mark posts to look later or to infographic later, which I freaking lose. Now I can do something about it. In the past I created a mini website inside LinkedIn, hyperlinking things, it would be cool to visualise that and connect the relationships between my posts. I also want to publish the relevant posts on my website or the SGraph website, to maximise the materials. But I do not think I get all the media, so we may need to grab the media a different way."** So the vault also recovers and connects the project lead's own content: the marked-for-later posts that get lost, the web of hyperlinked posts visualised, and a republishing path to his own sites to maximise the work, with a note that the export may omit media that must be fetched separately.

## A Vault Of Vaults

How to evolve it: **experiment in this first vault, then split into multiple vaults and vault apps, with the LinkedIn dump as the main data source for now and other data added next.**

The project lead: **"one of the powers of the vault of vaults is that we can experiment on this first vault, but eventually have multiple vaults each, and multiple vault apps, and consume one from the other. We can distinguish the data we get from the LinkedIn dump, which is the main data source for now, from what we add next."** So the design starts in one vault and grows into a vault of vaults (cross-ref: the vault-in-vault and the v0.33.2 release-manifest Uber-vault briefs): separate vaults and apps for separate concerns, consuming each other, with the LinkedIn export clearly delineated as the current source and other sources added later.

## The GDPR And Machine-Consumability Analysis

A sharp side investigation: **analyse whether the LinkedIn export is actually machine-consumable and GDPR-complete, because the suspicion is that it is the bare minimum.**

The project lead: **"I would like an interesting GDPR analysis, because my instinct is the data LinkedIn and others give us is not that machine-consumable. I think they did the minimum list they needed to. It would be interesting to see if it even breaks GDPR and other requirements, because there is data missing that they should already give. Maybe they do a great job, let's see."** So a side analysis assesses the export against GDPR and machine-consumability: is it complete, structured, and usable, or the minimum that meets the letter of the rule while omitting data the user is entitled to? This is both useful for this vault and a broader point about data portability.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not just transformed files.** A classified, connected semantic graph.
- **Not a single flat ontology.** Domain-specific ontologies composed into graphs of graphs.
- **Not public.** A completely private workflow.
- **Not blocked on automation.** Copy-paste text works now; integration later.
- **Not a one-off analysis.** A daily, repeatable maintenance routine.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: It is rich personal data about others.** Likes, comments, messages, and employers of real people. Mitigation: it stays private in the user's zero-knowledge vault; nothing is published (cross-ref: the linkedin-export-analysis-vault privacy boundary); the partner-outreach vault is where the public-private split lives.

**Risk 2: LinkedIn automation risks terms violations.** Automating connections and messages can break terms. Mitigation: start with copy-paste only; no automation in this phase; consider terms before any integration.

**Risk 3: The graph can over-model.** Graphs of graphs of ontologies can sprawl. Mitigation: start with the proven signal (posts to likers to employers); add ontologies as needed; the spreadsheet views keep it grounded.

**Risk 4: Outreach intelligence can feel mechanical.** Treating people as leads. Mitigation: these are real relationships and people the project lead has helped; keep the CRM in service of genuine, warm outreach.

**Risk 5: Missing media and incomplete export.** The export may omit media and data. Mitigation: the GDPR analysis surfaces the gaps; fetch media separately; the parked richer-data brief addresses more.
