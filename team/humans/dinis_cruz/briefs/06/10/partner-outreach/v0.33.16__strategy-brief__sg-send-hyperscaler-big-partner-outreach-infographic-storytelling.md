## The Opportunity: The Big Players

Why start here: **the big players are interesting because they have the customers, and they can be integration or consulting partners.**

The project lead: **"when you look at the landscape of companies to integrate with, the very interesting ones are the big players. They have the customers, they could be integration companies, they could be consulting companies."** So the hyperscalers and large vendors are a distinct opportunity (cross-ref: the integration-landscape briefs): not only integration targets but routes to customers and to consulting and integration partnerships. The work starts, as ever, with a mapping: which question to answer, where the answer is, and how the LLMs can do the research agentically.

## The Reach-Versus-Right-Person Problem

The core challenge at scale, and the principle that answers it: **at a big company you can probably reach someone, but they are rarely the right person, so the game is to make it easy for others to help you.**

The project lead: **"once you reach a bigger company, although I likely have a connection or can get one to somebody there, it is very unlikely that the person I can reach and the person who is the right one to talk to are the same. What we need to do is something I always tell people: make it easy for others to help you. Make it easy for companies and individuals to help us."** So the strategy accepts that the reachable contact is usually a bridge, not the target, and optimises for helpability: give the contact something easy to forward and act on, so they can route you to the right person (cross-ref: the company-intelligence-vault key-players targeting). This is why the two parts below matter.

## Two Parts: The Narrative, And Who To Talk To And How To Reach Them

The structure of every big-partner approach: **figure out the narrative, then figure out who to talk to and how to reach them.**

| Part | The Work |
|------|----------|
| The narrative | The story we tell the company; what are the three messages |
| Who and how | Who to talk to, and how to reach them through whom |

The project lead: **"there are two parts: we need to figure out the narrative to talk to those companies, and then who to talk to and how can we reach them."** So the approach separates the message (the narrative and the three core messages per company) from the routing (who is the right person, and which contact bridges to them). The intelligence and LinkedIn graphs supply the second; the infographic carries the first.

## The Per-Provider Porting Narrative

A ready-made story for the cloud providers: **show what we have done on AWS and propose porting it, so we are a live case study for Azure, GCP, and others, including a strong European play.**

The project lead: **"we have a tech stack on AWS and credits on AWS, and credits on GCP, we registered startups on both. For Azure, we explain what we have done with AWS and say we should port this to Azure, we are a great case study. Same for GCP, we already have a registered startup there, we should connect with them. And Digital Ocean, European cloud providers, I have a strong European play, let's reach out to the European players."** So the narrative writes itself per provider: a working AWS deployment (cross-ref: the v0.33.2 AWS-deployment and shipping-vault briefs) becomes the case study for porting to Azure, GCP, Digital Ocean, and the European clouds, with the European angle a deliberate strength. The ask is concrete: help us port and integrate, and use us as a case study.

## Map To What They Have And Are Doing

A discipline that prevents wasted approaches: **map what each provider actually has and is investing in, and do not pitch into a product they are discontinuing.**

The project lead: **"map first to what they have, what they are doing, what products they invest in. There is no point saying I want to talk to the WorkMail people when AWS is discontinuing WorkMail. Connect to their actual direction. Same with the big security vendors, massive or acquired, how do you find the right person?"** So the narrative must align with the provider's direction of travel (cross-ref: the company-intelligence-vault company lens): pitch into what they are growing, not what they are sunsetting, and for large or acquired security vendors, the same mapping finds the right, current person. The intelligence graph is what keeps the pitch aligned with reality.

## The Infographic As The Storytelling Primitive

The medium, emphasised strongly: **lead with an infographic that asks a question and carries a very visible ask, written for a reader with no context, and let it lead to the vault.**

The project lead: **"infographics are super critical. An infographic is a great way to ask a question, to say, here is you, here is how you fit into this, can you help? A wall of text does not work, people skim. The ask should be very visible, big arrows, look here, what am I asking them to do? An infographic, or a series of infographics, is a great way to do it, and then it leads to the vault, which can have more messages, even a video."**

| Infographic Principle | Why |
|-----------------------|-----|
| Ask a question | Here is you, here is how you fit, can you help |
| A very visible ask | Big arrows; what exactly am I asking them to do |
| Assume no context | They behave as if context-switching constantly; assume they do not know what SG/Vault is, that it is encrypted, or how it works |
| Translate to their world | Their environment, culture, experience, and the trust relationship |
| Force the crux | What are the three things you are trying to say |
| Lead to the vault | The infographic opens; the vault carries more, even a video |

The project lead on the reader: **"assume the reader behaves as if they have no time and are context-switching, so assume they have no idea what SG/Vault is, what I have been doing, that it is encrypted, or how it works. Translate it to their environment, their culture, their experience, and take the trust relationship into account, some are more friends, some less."** So the infographic is the primary outreach artifact: it forces good design and the crux (the three things), it carries a single very visible ask, it is written for a no-context, time-poor reader and translated into their world, and it opens into the vault (cross-ref: the partner-outreach-vault targeted experience and the partner-briefing brief), which holds the fuller message and possibly a video. This deserves its own treatment as a skill (below).

## The At-Scale Contact Intersection

How to find the right few among hundreds: **at a big company you may have hundreds of contacts, so intersect the public data and the LinkedIn graph to rank who is relevant, hot, connected, and current, and arrive at the three, five, or ten to approach or tag.**

The project lead: **"for bigger companies I will have 100, 200, 300, sometimes 400 contacts. Which ones are more relevant, which are hotter, which are connected to which, who is the boss of whom? There is the intersection between what we find publicly and my LinkedIn data, the two sources. Which ones have I interacted with closely, which have I not spoken to for five years, which have not moved or are not active? The visualisation is what lets us arrive at: I know the three people to talk to, the five, the ten to tag on a post."**

| Variable | Signal |
|----------|--------|
| Relevance and hotness | Who matters for this topic, and who is engaged now |
| Connection | Who is connected to whom; who is the boss of whom |
| Recency | Who I have spoken to recently versus years ago |
| Activity and moves | Who is active, who has changed roles |

So the two data sources (public intelligence and the LinkedIn graph; cross-ref: the company-intelligence-vault and linkedin-semantic-knowledge-graph briefs) intersect and are visualised to rank a large contact set down to the few that matter: the three to talk to, the five or ten to tag on a post. The visualisation is what turns hundreds of contacts into a decision.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a hope that the reachable contact is the right one.** They are a bridge; make it easy to help.
- **Not a wall of text.** An infographic with one very visible ask.
- **Not a generic pitch.** Per provider, mapped to their direction of travel.
- **Not pitching a discontinued product.** Aligned with what they are growing.
- **Not guesswork on who to approach.** The public and LinkedIn intersection, visualised.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Big companies are hard to penetrate.** The right person is hard to find and reach. Mitigation: the reach-versus-right-person principle; make it easy to help; the intelligence and LinkedIn intersection; the case study as the carrot.

**Risk 2: A misaligned pitch wastes a contact.** Pitching into a sunset product burns goodwill. Mitigation: map to their direction of travel; the company-intelligence graph keeps it current.

**Risk 3: Outreach at scale can feel like spam.** Hundreds of contacts and tagging. Mitigation: rank to the few that matter; warm, relevant, infographic-led; quality over volume (cross-ref: the company-intelligence-vault risk).

**Risk 4: Infographics can oversimplify.** A crux can lose nuance. Mitigation: the infographic opens into the vault, which carries the full message; the infographic is the hook, not the whole story.

**Risk 5: Contact data is personal.** Ranking hundreds of real people. Mitigation: public and the user's own LinkedIn data; private workflow (cross-ref: the linkedin-semantic-knowledge-graph privacy note); proportionate and warm.
