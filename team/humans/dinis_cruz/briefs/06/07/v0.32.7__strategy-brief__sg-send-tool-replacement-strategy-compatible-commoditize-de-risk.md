# Tool Replacement Strategy: Be Compatible, Commoditize The Next Level, And De-Risk The Switch

## What This Is

A strategy concept for how to drive change and replace an incumbent tool: **to replace a tool that already works, matching it feature-by-feature is not enough; you have to be significantly better, which means being almost 100% compatible (ideally invisible), adding features that commoditize the next level up, and de-risking the switch so the user never has to make a risk decision.** It is grounded in Wardley mapping and explains why tool-adoption attrition is so high. It builds on the Wardley evolution thinking (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 skill-lifecycle-explorer-to-town-planner brief) and the moat and integration work (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-moat and access-not-security briefs). New contributions: **the be-significantly-better rule, the working-tool-as-commodity framing, the two primitives (compatible and commoditize-the-next-level), orders-of-magnitude value, connecting the dots across layers, the superpower sweet spot, and de-risking the switch.** A related variation is a separate, forthcoming memo.

## A Working Tool Is A Commodity

The foundation, in Wardley terms: **once a tool works, it has become a commodity for its user, and asking them to switch asks them back to Genesis, which is why change is so hard.**

The project lead: **"if a tool already works, the cost of using it is sunk. When a tool works it becomes a commodity, and it does not matter how complicated and manual it is. That is why, when people do a repeated task five times, they prefer that to trying a new capability that might be faster, because you are asking them, at that moment, to go back to Genesis mode."**

| For The User, A Working Tool Is | Therefore |
|---------------------------------|-----------|
| A commodity (learned, trusted, understood) | They know how to handle it; it is a trusted element |
| The base for higher-order work | They use it to do something else, not in a vacuum |
| Predictable, even if manual | They have already accounted for its cost |

So a working tool, however clunky, is a commodity the user relies on to do higher-order things on top of it (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief's Wardley evolution and the commoditize-to-enable idea). The point of a commodity is to let other things happen. Asking someone to swap it out drags them back to Genesis (unlearned, untrusted, uncertain), which is why the attrition of tool adoption and change is massive: you are not just offering a better tool, you are threatening the stable base they build on.

## The Two Primitives For Replacing Something That Works

The core of the strategy: **to replace a working tool you need two things at once, near-total compatibility and new features that commoditize the next level up.**

### Primitive 1: Almost 100% Compatible, Ideally Invisible

The project lead: **"you have to be almost 100% compatible with what already exists, so you literally have the same feature set, and ideally you are invisible, so that is the best way, where you replace another tool under the hood and things never change."** So the replacement must match the incumbent's feature set so closely that the user loses nothing, and the ideal is to be invisible: slot in under the hood so nothing changes for the user. This is the same have-your-own-compatible-version and never-inline-disruption instinct from elsewhere in the corpus (cross-ref: the v0.32.3 NHI-2.0-living-off-the-land and access-not-security briefs). Compatibility removes the reason not to switch.

### Primitive 2: New Features That Commoditize The Next Level

The project lead: **"you also have to have new features, and the new features have to commoditize something that before was custom-built on top of the previous commodity. Your new features have to be that much better, so you move the user to another level of commodity above. Once you commoditize something, you allow higher-order things to occur."** So compatibility is necessary but not sufficient; you must also add features that take what used to be custom-built or manual on top of the old tool and make it a commodity, lifting the user to a higher level (cross-ref: the skill-lifecycle brief's innovate-leverage-commoditize). Compatibility gives a reason to switch with no loss; commoditizing the next level gives a reason to switch with real gain.

## New Technology Is Orders Of Magnitude Better

Why this is achievable now, and the pattern behind it: **a new technology's benefit is not doing what you could before, but doing it orders of magnitude better, and commoditizing what was custom or manual.**

The project lead: **"the benefits of a new technology are not just what you could do before, but doing it orders of magnitude better. The cloud is a good example: it is not that it provided compute, you always had compute, it provided compute on demand in ways we could not do before. And more than that, you could script and automate it, so you could do things you could not before. You commoditize or productize something that before was custom-built or manual."** So the cloud did not just supply compute; it made compute on demand, scriptable, and automatable, commoditizing what had been manual and enabling entirely new things. That is the template: a replacement earns its place by being orders of magnitude better and by productizing the previously-custom layer, not by being marginally nicer.

## A Better Dropbox Is Not Enough

The lived experience, stated plainly: **a better Dropbox, or a better encrypted file sender, or a better data room, is not enough on its own, because people already have a solution.**

The project lead: **"I come up with a better Dropbox, a better way to send encrypted files, and it is still not good enough, because people already have a solution that does that. Same with the vault, same with data rooms: people use data rooms, they use Google Drive. You have to not just be better, you have to have a couple of higher levels of capability and a couple of higher levels of value proposition. That is the name of the game."** So being a better version of an existing category (a better Dropbox, a better data room) does not move people, because the incumbent is already a commodity for them. The replacement must add higher-level capabilities and a higher-level value proposition, which is exactly the second primitive. This is a direct caution for how the vault is positioned: not as a better Dropbox, but as something that commoditizes a level above.

## The Good News: Connect The Dots Across Layers

Why the game is winnable now: **we can do more than improve one tool; we can connect things and add value across layers, and make the layers above easier, faster, and more integrated.**

The project lead: **"the good news is we can play that game much better now, because we can not just add value to one thing, we can connect and add a lot more value, connect a lot more dots, and give tools that allow those connections to occur. I am productizing this layer, but now I can make the layers above much easier, faster, and more integrated, and those above layers can be more vibe-coded."** So the modern advantage is connection: rather than competing on one tool, you commoditize a layer and make the higher layers easier to build and integrate, including by vibe-coding them (cross-ref: the v0.32.4 coding-as-abstraction-layers and v0.32.3 open-source-not-free briefs). The vault platform is exactly this kind of play: productize the layer, then enable and connect everything above it.

## The Sweet Spot: New Capabilities That Feel Like Superpowers

The best version of the strategy: **the sweet spot is when, on top of being compatible and better, you let users do things they simply could not do before, which feel like superpowers.**

The project lead: **"the best situation is when you allow them to do things they could not do before. There are still primitives that are better than before, but there are now new capabilities that feel like superpowers. That is the sweet spot."** So beyond compatibility and incremental improvement, the strongest position adds genuinely new capabilities that feel like superpowers. The user keeps everything they had (compatibility), gets better primitives, and gains abilities that did not exist before. That combination is what actually overcomes the inertia of a commodity.

## De-Risk The Switch

The point that often decides it, and it is not price: **switching tools is risky for the user in a way that staying is not, so you have to de-risk it completely; the user must never have to make a risk decision to use your product.**

The project lead: **"it is not just the price. We have to de-risk this for the user. If somebody does a manual or consistent task, which for them is a commodity, there is no risk in doing it, they know it and have already accounted for the time. Trying a new solution is risky, because at that moment you have a double negative: you have not done what you were supposed to do, and you have gone off on a tangent. We cannot make the user take a risk decision on using your product. It has to be that smooth and de-risked."**

| Staying With The Commodity | Trying The New Solution |
|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| No risk; known and accounted for | Risky; unproven in their hands |
| The task gets done | A double negative if it fails: task undone and time lost |
| Predictable cost | An uncertain bet |

So the incumbent's hidden advantage is that it is risk-free for the user, while any new tool is a bet that could leave them worse off (the double negative: the work is still not done and they have spent time on a tangent). The replacement therefore has to be so smooth and de-risked that adopting it is not a decision the user has to weigh. Invisibility (primitive 1) is part of this: if the switch changes nothing and risks nothing, there is no decision to make.

## What This Asks For

1. **Do not aim for feature parity; aim to be significantly better** (parity does not move a commodity).
2. **Treat the incumbent as a commodity** (the user builds higher-order work on it; switching drags them to Genesis).
3. **Be almost 100% compatible, ideally invisible** (the same feature set; replace under the hood; nothing changes).
4. **Add features that commoditize the next level** (productize what was custom or manual on top of the old tool; lift the user up a level).
5. **Be orders of magnitude better** (not marginally nicer; the cloud template).
6. **Do not position the vault as a better Dropbox** (add higher-level capability and value, not a nicer version of a category).
7. **Connect the dots across layers** (commoditize a layer; make the layers above easier, integrated, and vibe-codeable).
8. **Reach for superpowers** (let users do what they could not before).
9. **De-risk the switch completely** (no risk decision; smooth and invisible; remove the double negative).

Estimated effort: a strategy and positioning brief, not a build; it informs how the vault and SG Send are positioned against incumbents (Dropbox, data rooms, Google Drive) and how features are chosen. The contribution is the be-significantly-better rule, the two primitives, and the de-risking discipline.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not feature parity.** Parity does not move a commodity; be significantly better.
- **Not a disruptive switch.** Compatible and ideally invisible; nothing changes for the user.
- **Not a better version of a category.** Commoditize a level above, not a nicer Dropbox.
- **Not a one-tool play.** Connect the dots across layers; enable the layers above.
- **Not a risk decision for the user.** De-risk it completely; remove the double negative.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Compatibility is expensive and easy to underestimate.** Matching a commodity feature-for-feature is hard. Mitigation: aim for invisibility under the hood; reuse have-your-own-compatible-version (cross-ref: the living-off-the-land brief).

**Risk 2: Commoditizing the next level may be unclear.** It is not always obvious what the higher level is. Mitigation: look at what users build on top of the incumbent today; commoditize that.

**Risk 3: Superpowers can distract from compatibility.** New capabilities are seductive; without compatibility they do not land. Mitigation: both primitives at once; compatibility first, superpowers on top.

**Risk 4: Under-estimating the user's risk aversion.** Teams over-value features and under-value de-risking. Mitigation: make adoption a non-decision; smooth, invisible, reversible.

**Risk 5: Positioning as a better incumbent.** It is tempting to say better Dropbox. Mitigation: position on the level above and the connections, not the category.
