# A Vault Upload Workflow For Large Files: Chunk, Hash, And Load The Vault Without Downloading It

**version** v0.32.7
**date** 7 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Developer (lead), Architect, Product, @Dev
**type** Dev brief

---

## What This Is

A real, concrete need driving a building block: **a 100% vault upload workflow that shares a large file (the live case is 15 GB) by loading it in the browser, breaking it into chunks, hashing each chunk, and uploading them resumably into a vault, so the recipient can load the vault and see its structure without ever downloading the whole 15 GB.** The headline feature is that a vault can hold many large files, and a recipient can open it, see the folder structure immediately, and pull only the chunks or files they want. New contributions: **the vault upload workflow, loading the vault without downloading the data, the client-side load-chunk-hash pipeline, hash-based resume and deduplication, instant folder-structure visibility, first-frame video thumbnails, and reuse of the existing SGraph.ai tools.** It builds on the vault primitives, deterministic indexes, and prior streaming and sync work (cross-ref: the v0.32.7 deterministic-value-indexes, v0.31.12 vault-as-platform-of-primitives, v0.27.38 encrypted-video-streaming-from-vaults, and v0.27.64 vault-commit-queue-batched-autosync briefs).

## The Real Need: Share A 15 GB File, As A Vault

The trigger, and it is concrete: **the project lead has a 15 GB file to share and wants to do it as a vault, through our own provider, not a third-party file service.**

The project lead: **"I want to share a 15 GB file. I have it on my phone, I can download it to my laptop, but what I really want is a UI that figures out the best way to share this."** And the refinement that frames this revision: **make this 100% a vault upload workflow.** So the deliverable is not a standalone file-sharing experiment; it is a vault workflow. You upload a large file into a vault through our provider, and you share the vault. SG Send, whose 2 GB limit is soft rather than hard, is the natural host (cross-ref: the platform-of-primitives brief): this workflow is how vaults and SG Send handle files far beyond 2 GB.

## The Massive Key Feature: Load The Vault Without Downloading The Data

The headline, and the reason this is a vault workflow rather than a file transfer: **a vault can hold many large files, so the recipient can load the vault and see its folder structure straight away, without downloading the 15 GB.**

The project lead's framing: use the vault's ability to hold multiple files of large size so that **the user can load the vault without downloading 15 GB of data**, and so that the recipient **can see the folder structure straight away.**

| A File-Transfer Link | This Vault Workflow |
|----------------------|---------------------|
| Download the whole file to use any of it | Load the vault; see everything without downloading it |
| No structure until downloaded | The folder structure is visible immediately |
| All or nothing | Pull only the chunks or files you want |
| One opaque blob | Many large files, each addressable |

So the vault holds the file as many large chunks (and many files), and because the vault carries its structure and metadata separately from the bulk data, the recipient opens the vault and immediately sees the folder structure and what is inside, then downloads only the chunks or files they actually want. This is the massive key feature: loading the vault is cheap and instant; downloading the data is selective and on demand. It is the direct-encrypted-file-access pattern (cross-ref: the v0.32.4 search-engine-serverless-stack and public-data briefs) applied to very large files.

## Chunked Upload: Start, Stop, And Continue

The core mechanic, and what it buys: **upload the big file in chunks, which gives the ability to start, stop, and continue the uploads.**

The project lead's framing: **upload the big file in chunks, which would give us the ability to start, stop and continue the uploads.** So chunking is what makes the upload resumable: each chunk is uploaded independently, so an interrupted upload resumes from the next missing chunk rather than restarting the whole 15 GB. A dropped connection, a closed laptop, or a switch from low to high bandwidth costs one chunk, not the file. This is the batched, one-piece-at-a-time pattern from the vault sync work (cross-ref: the vault-commit-queue-batched-autosync brief), now for large-file upload.

## The Key Client-Side Question: Load, Chunk, Hash

The central thing to measure, and the project lead names it as the key question: **how fast can the browser load the 15 GB file into memory, break it into smaller chunks (for example n minutes long for video), and calculate the hash of each chunk?**

The project lead's framing: the key question is **how fast is the load of the 15 GB file into memory in the browser, then break it into smaller chunks (n minutes long) and then calculate the hash of those chunks.**

| Step | What To Measure |
|------|-----------------|
| Load 15 GB into the browser | How fast, and is it even feasible in memory? |
| Break into chunks | n-minute chunks for video; sized blocks otherwise |
| Hash each chunk | The cost of hashing every chunk client-side |

So the first experiment is purely client-side performance: can the browser load 15 GB, chunk it, and hash each chunk, and how fast? This is the make-or-break question, because the whole workflow (resume, deduplication, structure) depends on having a hash per chunk computed on the client. The MVP should measure this directly.

## Hashes Enable Resume And Selective Upload

Why the per-chunk hash matters, and this connects to today's index work: **once each chunk has a hash, you can check whether it has already been uploaded, and then upload chunks one, n, or all at a time.**

The project lead's framing: once we have the chunk hashes, **we should have a way to see if that file has been uploaded, and then upload the files one, n, all at a time.**

| The Chunk Hash Gives You | Result |
|--------------------------|--------|
| A check for whether the chunk is already uploaded | Skip what is already there; true resume and deduplication |
| Per-chunk addressing | Upload one chunk, n chunks, or all at once |
| Deterministic naming | The hash is the chunk's identity |

So the per-chunk hash is the chunk's identity: requesting whether a chunk with that hash already exists answers do we already have this (resume and deduplication), and because each chunk is independently addressable you can upload one, a batch of n, or all of them, depending on bandwidth and urgency. This is exactly the deterministic-value-index pattern from earlier today (cross-ref: the v0.32.7 deterministic-value-indexes brief): the hash of the content is the index, and requesting it answers existence. Identical chunks are never uploaded twice.

## Video: Self-Contained Slices And First-Frame Thumbnails

For video, two capabilities, one carried over and one new: **split a video into self-contained slices with FFmpeg, and take a fast client-side screenshot of the first frame of each.**

The project lead: **"with videos, is it possible to break them into slices and make them self-contained? Say a 40-minute video into five-minute chunks. It might work, because we have FFmpeg."** And the new addition: it **should also be quite fast on the client side to take a screenshot of the first frame of the video.**

| Capability | What It Gives |
|------------|---------------|
| Self-contained slices (FFmpeg) | Each n-minute chunk is independently playable |
| First-frame thumbnail (client-side) | A fast preview image per chunk, computed on the client |

So a video becomes a set of self-contained, independently playable slices (the n-minute chunks above; cross-ref: the encrypted-video-streaming-from-vaults brief), and for each slice the client can quickly grab the first frame as a thumbnail. That thumbnail is part of what makes loading the vault without downloading it so useful: the recipient sees the structure and a preview frame per slice immediately, and chooses what to download.

## Reuse The SGraph.ai Tools: Audio And Chunk Editing

A practical multiplier, because the building blocks exist: **reuse the core blocks already in the SGraph.ai tools section to do things like upload just the audio, or edit the chunks.**

The project lead's framing: we could add the tools (we already have the core blocks in our SGraph.ai tools section) to **just upload the audio, for example, and to do some file editing to those chunks.**

| Tool (Core Blocks Exist) | Use |
|--------------------------|-----|
| Audio extraction | Upload just the audio of a video, not the whole thing |
| Chunk editing | Edit, trim, or transform the chunks |

So the workflow plugs into the existing SGraph.ai tools (cross-ref: the v0.21.3 video-editing-tools brief and the tools work): extract and upload only the audio from a video, or edit the chunks (trim, transform) as part of the workflow. Because the core blocks already exist, these are additions to the upload workflow rather than new builds.

## The Experiments Ladder

How to build it, layering capabilities: **raw upload, then split, then encrypt, then video slices, each adding one capability and each measured.**

The project lead: **"we have the raw upload, which I would like to explore, maybe just raw upload to S3 without encryption: will it work? Then have another one that splits it, then another that encrypts, then another that breaks into five-minute chunks. We just need to experiment."**

| Step | Experiment | Question |
|------|-----------|----------|
| 1 | Raw upload to S3 (no encryption) | Can we upload 15 GB at all, into a vault? |
| 2 | Load, split, and hash on the client | How fast; does resume and deduplication work? |
| 3 | Encrypt | How does encryption scale at this size? |
| 4 | Video slices and thumbnails | Self-contained chunks; first-frame previews |

So the build is incremental and measurable: start with raw upload to establish feasibility and a baseline, then the client-side load-chunk-hash pipeline (the key question), then encryption (measured against the raw baseline), then video slicing and thumbnails. This is the explorer-phase, scaffolding-and-experiments approach (cross-ref: the v0.32.4 coding-as-abstraction-layers brief).

## Measure How Encryption Scales

A specific thing to learn: **the project lead wants to see how the encrypted part scales at 15 GB across the chunks.**

The project lead: **"I like us to see the encrypted part of it, to see how much encrypted scales."** So step 3 is a measurement: how does encrypting and decrypting behave at 15 GB and across the chunks, what is the overhead, and does it stay practical, given the vault is zero-knowledge by default? The raw-upload baseline (step 1) is what the encrypted step is measured against.

## Maximum Visibility, Toward A Simple Powerful UX

The objective, and the discipline: **instrument everything with maximum visibility and logging during the experiments, so the finished workflow is a very simple but very powerful user experience.**

The project lead: **"we need maximum visibility and maximum logging, so we really understand. But ultimately it should become a very, very powerful, simple user experience, which is the objective. We need the primitives to create the UI."** So during development everything is logged and surfaced (load time, chunking, hashing, upload progress, encryption scaling, video slicing), and that understanding lets the finished workflow be simple: the user drops in a 15 GB file and gets a shareable vault, while the workflow quietly handles loading, chunking, hashing, resume, deduplication, encryption, and thumbnails, and the recipient opens the vault and browses without downloading it.

## What This Asks For

1. **Build it as a 100% vault upload workflow** (upload into a vault; share the vault; SG Send as the host).
2. **Deliver the load-without-downloading feature** (a vault of many large files; the recipient sees the folder structure immediately and pulls chunks selectively).
3. **Chunk for start, stop, and continue** (resumable; a dropped upload resumes from the next missing chunk).
4. **Measure the client-side load-chunk-hash pipeline** (how fast the browser loads 15 GB, chunks it, and hashes each chunk: the key question).
5. **Use chunk hashes for resume and deduplication** (check existence by hash; upload one, n, or all; never upload an identical chunk twice; cross-ref: the deterministic-value-indexes brief).
6. **Add video slices and first-frame thumbnails** (self-contained FFmpeg slices; fast client-side first-frame previews).
7. **Reuse the SGraph.ai tools** (audio-only upload; chunk editing; the core blocks already exist).
8. **Build the experiments ladder and measure encryption scaling** (raw upload, load-chunk-hash, encrypt, video; full logging).
9. **Instrument everything, then ship a simple UX** (drop a file, get a shareable vault).

Estimated effort: moderate and experiment-led; the vault, S3 access, encryption, FFmpeg, the SGraph.ai tools, and the deterministic-index pattern exist; the contribution is the client-side load-chunk-hash pipeline, the resumable vault upload, and the load-without-downloading experience. The MVP is the live 15 GB file loaded in the browser, chunked and hashed, uploaded resumably into a vault, with the recipient able to open the vault, see the folder structure and per-slice thumbnails, and download only what they choose.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a third-party file service.** A vault upload workflow through our own provider.
- **Not a download-everything transfer.** Load the vault and its structure without downloading the data; pull chunks selectively.
- **Not a single big upload.** Chunked, hashed, resumable; start, stop, continue.
- **Not re-uploading duplicates.** Chunk hashes give existence checks and deduplication.
- **Not opaque.** Maximum visibility and logging, toward a simple UX.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Loading 15 GB into the browser may be slow or infeasible.** This is the key unknown. Mitigation: it is the first measurement; consider streaming or partial loading; chunk and hash incrementally rather than all at once.

**Risk 2: Client-side hashing of many chunks is costly.** Hashing every chunk takes time. Mitigation: measure it; size chunks sensibly; hash as chunks are produced; the cost buys resume and deduplication.

**Risk 3: Encryption may not scale cleanly.** Encrypting 15 GB could be heavy. Mitigation: that is the measurement (step 3); baseline against raw upload; tune chunk size.

**Risk 4: Raw upload without encryption is a deliberate exception.** Step 1 is unencrypted. Mitigation: it is the baseline experiment; the encrypted step follows; treat raw uploads as explicitly non-private (cross-ref: the public-data brief).

**Risk 5: Video slicing and thumbnails have format pitfalls.** Self-contained playable slices and first-frame capture vary by format. Mitigation: FFmpeg for slices; verify playability and thumbnail capture per format.
