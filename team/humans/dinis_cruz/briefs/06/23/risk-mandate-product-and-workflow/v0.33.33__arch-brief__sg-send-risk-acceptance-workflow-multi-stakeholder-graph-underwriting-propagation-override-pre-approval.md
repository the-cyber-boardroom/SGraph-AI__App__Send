## What This Is

The multi-stakeholder risk acceptance workflow, modelled as a graph: **each acceptance now has two dimensions, a direction, get more data, reduce, or increase the risk, and a revisit interval, when it must be accepted again; an exec never decides a risk alone, it must first be underwritten by the relevant direct-line owner, the CIO, CTO, or CFO by dimension, the CSO, and at least GRC; and once accepted at the right altitude it propagates upward to the boss, the boss's boss, and the CEO, who acts for the board and is where the buck stops, with some risks escalating to the board itself; evidence supplied down the chain can strike intervals off the table when a remediation is physically impossible in the time, so the short options simply disappear; a superior can override a subordinate's acceptance in either direction, tighter or looser, with the original acceptance preserved and the override attributed, though no override can buy a physically impossible timeline, for which the only escape is the nuclear option of ceasing the activity; and risks compound, so once a stakeholder approves a risk profile, further instances of the same profile are pre-approved and become an FYI, with fresh approval needed only when the risk profile changes.** It is the follow-up to the risk acceptance service (cross-ref: the v0.33.33 risk-acceptance-service brief, the v0.33.40 risk-acceptance brief, the v0.33.32 agent-mandate-graph and agentic-cost briefs, and the v0.33.31 mandate-is-delegation brief). New contributions: **the two-dimensional acceptance, the underwriting graph, and the compound pre-approval.**

## The Framing

What this adds: **the real-world complexity of who accepts, and how it moves.**

The project lead: **"this is a follow-up on the risk acceptance workflow, to take into account the real-world complexities. The key point is that we need to graph the multiple stakeholders in the risk acceptance workflow."** So the simple accept-for-an-interval flow becomes a graph of stakeholders, underwriting, and propagation.

## Two Dimensions: Direction And Interval

The refined model: **an acceptance is a direction and a revisit interval.**

The project lead: **"there are at least two core primitives here, accept and get more data, and accept and deal with it and reduce the risk, and actually there should be another, accept and increase the risk, because it is okay to increase the risk if the business is okay with it."** So an acceptance carries two independent facts: a direction, what you intend to do about the risk, and an interval, when you must look at it again. The previous brief's buttons were points in this space; separating the axes makes the model real.

| The two dimensions | Values |
|--------------------|--------|
| Direction (what you intend) | Get more data, reduce, increase, or hold |
| Revisit interval (when you look again) | 1 hour, 4 hours, 2 days, 2 weeks, 6 months |

So, for example, get-more-data at one hour means I need more data and will look again within the hour; reduce at four hours means start a P1 and remediate; reduce at two weeks means fund a project; increase at six months means deliberately take on more risk and review it then; and hold at six months means accept as-is at near-zero cost. The direction says what, the interval says when.

## The Underwriting Graph: The Exec Never Decides Alone

The core governance rule: **a decision must be underwritten before an exec makes it.**

The project lead: **"an exec is not the one who takes the risk, and the exec should never make a decision that has not been underwritten by the relevant player. So the exec should never make a risk decision that has not been accepted, or explicitly not accepted, which also matters, by at least the technical or direct-line element, the CIO or CTO or CFO depending on the dimension, the respective CSO, and at least GRC from a risk point of view."** So before an exec acts, the relevant direct-line owner, the CSO, and GRC must each have recorded an acceptance or a refusal, and a refusal is as important as an acceptance. This is a graph: the decision node depends on underwriting edges from the right players.

## Accept At The Right Altitude, Then Propagate Up

How risk moves: **accepted at the right level, then rolled up.**

The project lead: **"at the start there are no risks. The risk needs to be accepted at the right altitude, and then it propagates out, which means every risk accepted by somebody always gets given to their boss, and their boss's boss, all the way to the CEO. The CEO acts on behalf of the board, so the buck stops with them, although some risks even the CEO has to take up to the board."**

```
   owner accepts at the right altitude
            | rolls up
            v
        manager (informed; may override)
            | rolls up
            v
   direct-line owner + CSO + GRC (must underwrite an exec decision)
            | rolls up
            v
        exec / CEO (acts for the board; the buck stops; some go to the board)
```

So a risk is accepted at the altitude where it belongs, and the acceptance then rolls up the chain, informing each superior, to the CEO, with the board above for the largest risks.

## Evidence Strikes Options Off

A constraint on the intervals: **some choices are physically impossible.**

The project lead: **"some players can impact the options with evidence. Some risks depend on a set of actions that are not possible within an hour, so even if you start the biggest incident on the planet and throw all the money in, you cannot do it in less than an hour. So that option is removed from the equation; suddenly your top-level options are not available, and you need to provide evidence for this, provided further down the chain."** So evidence from the people who know the work can strike intervals off the table: if a remediation cannot physically complete in the time, that interval simply disappears, and options are struck off or struck on as information arrives.

## Override, In Either Direction

Who can change an acceptance: **a superior can override either way, on the record.**

A superior can override a subordinate's acceptance in either direction, making it more urgent or less, and the subordinate's original acceptance is preserved while the override is attributed, so the record shows who accepted what and who changed it. The project lead: **"the CTO might say do this for a month; the CEO can override, I am not comfortable, I want this in a week. A risk can be revised, somebody can say I am not happy with this, which can be overwritten at the top."** Two limits hold regardless of direction: no override can select an interval that evidence has struck off as physically impossible, and the only escape from such a hard constraint is the nuclear option, ceasing the activity, for example shutting the site.

## Compound Risks And Pre-Approval

How it scales: **approve a profile once, then it is an FYI.**

The project lead: **"the way this scales is that you have a compound set of risks that gets approved. Once the CFO approves a risk, say a database deletion or unlimited financial exposure to token consumption, the next one that requires that is just another case of it, so you start to have pre-approval, which is more of an FYI, by the way, now you have four of these instead of one. You only need approval if you change the risk profile of the situation."** So approval attaches to a risk profile, not each instance: further instances of an already-approved profile are pre-approved and surface only as an FYI, and a fresh approval is required only when the risk profile itself changes (cross-ref: the agentic-cost brief on the unlimited-token-exposure profile).

## The Workflow As A Graph

Why it all has to be a graph: **the workflow is the relationships.**

Pulling it together, the workflow is a graph whose nodes are risks, stakeholders, and acceptances, and whose edges are underwriting, propagation, override, and pre-approval. The graph holds who accepted what, at what altitude, in which direction, for how long, underwritten by whom, rolled up to whom, and against which approved profile. This is precisely why the product is a graph: only a graph can answer, for any risk, the full chain of who is carrying it and on what basis.

## Acceptance Criteria

1. An acceptance is modelled as a direction and a revisit interval, independently.
2. Directions are get-more-data, reduce, increase, and hold.
3. An exec decision requires recorded underwriting, acceptance or refusal, by the direct-line owner, the CSO, and GRC.
4. An accepted risk propagates up to the CEO; the largest escalate to the board.
5. Evidence can strike intervals off as physically impossible, for everyone.
6. A superior can override in either direction, with the original preserved and the override attributed.
7. No override can select a struck-off interval; the only escape is ceasing the activity.
8. Approval attaches to a risk profile; further instances are pre-approved as an FYI; a profile change triggers re-approval.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a single decider.** The exec never decides without underwriting.
- **Not a deny gate.** The direction and interval replace deny, as before.
- **Not per-instance approval.** It approves profiles and pre-approves their instances.
- **Not unconstrained.** Physically impossible intervals stay off the table.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: relaxing override could undercut the owner.** A superior can loosen what an owner set. Mitigation: the owner's acceptance is preserved and the override is attributed, so accountability is visible.

**Tension 2: pre-approval can let risk creep.** Many instances under one profile add up. Mitigation: re-approval on any profile change, and the roll-up keeps the totals visible.

**Tension 3: altitude is a judgement.** The right level to accept a risk is not always obvious. Mitigation: the underwriting graph forces the right players in regardless of where it starts.
