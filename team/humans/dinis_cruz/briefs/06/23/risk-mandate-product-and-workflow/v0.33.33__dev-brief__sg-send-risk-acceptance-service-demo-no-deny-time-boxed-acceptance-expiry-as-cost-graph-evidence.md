# The Risk Acceptance Service: No Deny Button, Time-Boxed Acceptance, Expiry As Cost

**version** v0.33.33
**date** 23 June 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** @Dev, Product, Strategy
**type** Dev brief (the first service and demo)

---

## What This Is

The first service, a risk acceptance service, and its demo: **a person chooses a scenario relevant to them and is given a short flow of hairy, evidence-backed risks to accept, full data access, the ability to compromise the whole business, unlimited financial liability from unrestricted token use, and crucially there is no deny button, only a choice of how long to accept the risk for, one hour, four hours, two days, two weeks, or six months, where the interval is when the acceptance expires and must be renewed, and each interval encodes a meaning and a cost, one hour means I need more data, four hours means start a P1 and trigger incident response, two days means a smaller incident, two weeks means a funded project, and six months means do nothing, which costs zero; the demo runs on the existing tech stack, optionally with a vault and a Gen AI layer, and rests on a graph and an evidence structure, because each exec asks questions from their own role, so the graph must hold the granularity to present a customised view per persona, backed by real incidents and concrete examples, and connected to the mandate, including the risk that not all required stakeholders have approved.** It is the first buildable service of Risk Mandate.ai (cross-ref: the v0.33.40 risk-acceptance brief, the v0.33.32 agent-mandate-graph, evidence-mapping-website, and agentic-cost briefs, and the v0.33.33 audience brief). New contributions: **the no-deny time-boxed acceptance, the expiry-as-cost mapping, and the graph-and-evidence demo.**

## The Framing

The first move: **risk acceptance, front and centre.**

The project lead: **"the first service we provide is a risk acceptance service, and the beauty is it can be done simply with the tech stack we have. A vault can connect to it, but it does not need to, to start with."** So the service is small to build and leads the workflow.

## The Service

How it works: **choose a scenario, accept a few risks.**

The project lead: **"if somebody goes to our site, or you hand them an iPad, and they choose a scenario relevant to them, we give them a risk acceptance flow, two or three risks they have to accept, and there may or may not be a Gen AI in this, which we can pass to customers via an OpenRouter key, or not."** So the user picks a scenario, the service presents a short flow of risks, and a Gen AI layer is optional and can be metered to the customer.

## The Hairy, Compound Risks

What they are asked to accept: **the real ones, and they compound.**

The project lead: **"these questions should be hairy: accept that the agentic solutions have full access to your data, accept that they can compromise the entire business. And some are compound risks, a set of risks that compound to the top one. You can have unlimited liability for finance, which is literally what happens when people just use tokens without restrictions, and then the question is, do you have restrictions in place, and that corrects the amount."** So the risks are stated plainly and uncomfortably, they roll up into a top risk, and a mitigating control, such as a spend restriction, adjusts the level rather than removing it.

## The Key Mechanic: No Deny Button

The heart of the service: **you cannot deny, you can only set when you must look again.**

The project lead: **"the most important thing is that there is no deny button. The only buttons are accept for one hour, four hours, two days, two weeks, or a month, and the interval is when the risk acceptance expires, when you have to accept the risk again. So if you accept for an hour, you are really saying I need more data, someone needs to get me more data so I can make a better decision."** Removing deny and replacing it with how long until you must re-accept turns risk acceptance into a forcing function: you cannot make the risk go away, you can only decide how soon you must confront it again, and that interval encodes how seriously you are treating it.

| Accept for | What it means | Action and cost |
|------------|---------------|-----------------|
| 1 hour | I need more data before I can really decide | Someone must go and get more data; near-immediate |
| 4 hours | This is a P1 | Trigger incident response, playbooks, and workflow; remediation within four hours; a real cost |
| 2 days | A smaller incident, but still an incident | Something must be done within two days |
| 2 weeks | A funded project | Plan and fund the fix; less immediate, lower cost |
| 6 months | Do nothing | Review in six months; effectively no action; costs zero |

The project lead on the ends of the scale: **"four hours means start a P1 straight away, trigger your incident response and come back in four hours with a remediation. Two weeks means a funded project. Six months means you review it then, which means not doing anything, and that costs zero, because you are not doing anything about it."**

## The Cost-Of-Remediation Sweet Spot

What the intervals reveal: **how much it costs to reduce the risk.**

The project lead: **"each of these has costs when you look at the scenarios, and that is a really cool thing to show: multiple threat models, multiple scenarios, this costs that, that costs this, almost like how much does it cost to reduce the risk, so you can find the sweet spot."** So the service can show, per scenario, the cost of acting at each interval, and let the user find the point where the cost of remediation and the residual risk balance.

## The Demo Is A Graph And An Evidence Structure

Why the graph is central: **every exec asks from their own role.**

The project lead: **"the demo is, can we create a graph structure and an evidence structure, and the graph is critical because any business exec will ask questions based on their role and what they care about, and our graph has to support it. The graph is fundamentally providing the data required for the person to make the call, and we have known unknowns and unknown unknowns, so the graph needs the granularity to present customised views to each of these, and the risks should be backed by evidence."** So the demo rests on the multi-persona graph from the audience brief and on an evidence structure, presenting each persona a view at the granularity their decision needs.

## Backed By Real Examples

What makes it land: **real incidents and concrete consequences.**

The project lead: **"we can use the demos and data we already have as real-world examples. Say, here is a Replit case, here is what it means. If you run AI tools on your machine, here is what it means. If you are a doctor running AI tools on your machine, any data on it, including patient data, could be compromised. And we connect those dots to mandates: how many people do you need to get the mandate to approve this? There is an interesting case in saying not all required stakeholders have approved this, which is a risk in itself."** So the risks are grounded in documented incidents and concrete situations, and connected to the mandate, including the risk that the approval itself is incomplete (cross-ref: the evidence-mapping-website and agentic-cost briefs).

## Acceptance Criteria

1. A user can pick a scenario relevant to their role.
2. The service presents a short flow of hairy, evidence-backed risks.
3. Risks can compound into a top risk, and a stated control adjusts the level.
4. There is no deny button; only the time-boxed acceptance intervals.
5. Each interval triggers its mapped meaning, action, and cost.
6. The acceptance expires at the interval and must be renewed.
7. The demo can show the cost of acting at each interval across scenarios.
8. The graph presents a customised, role-appropriate view per persona, backed by evidence.
9. A Gen AI layer is optional and metered, and a vault can attach but is not required to start.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a deny gate.** It never lets the user pretend the risk is gone.
- **Not in-line enforcement.** It surfaces and times the acceptance, out of band.
- **Not generic.** It is scenario-specific and role-aware.
- **Not unevidenced.** Every hairy risk is backed by a real example.

## Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: hairy risks can feel like fear-selling.** The discomfort is the point, but it must be fair. Mitigation: every risk is evidence-backed, and a stated control adjusts it.

**Tension 2: the intervals imply costs we have not all sized.** Some costs are immediate, others scenario-dependent. Mitigation: show the cost ranges per scenario, and be honest where unknown.

**Tension 3: the graph must hold real granularity.** A thin graph gives thin answers. Mitigation: build on the multi-persona agent mandate graph and the evidence structure.
