## What This Is

A strategic analysis: given everything we have built and the substrate options now in front of us (Lambda, Fargate, AgentCore Runtime, our own container hosts, dedicated EC2, Nitro Enclaves), **should we add Firecracker as a substrate we operate directly?**

The companion research brief covers what Firecracker is and where it sits. This brief reads that against our direction of travel and answers the question.

Short answer up front: **the answer changed in February 2026**. AWS added nested virtualization support on virtual EC2 instances (C8i, M8i, R8i families), which means a viable Firecracker development host now costs under $0.09/hour on-demand (or under $0.05/hour spot) instead of $3+/hour for bare-metal. This collapses the substrate cost barrier. We should now:

1. **Run a small Firecracker proof-of-concept** to build hands-on understanding (this is now cheap enough to be a no-brainer)
2. **Continue using managed services as our default** for production workloads where Lambda/Fargate/AgentCore are sufficient
3. **Pick one or two specific use cases** where direct Firecracker may meaningfully outperform the managed alternatives and prototype those properly
4. **Architect such that direct Firecracker is a first-class substrate option** in our spectrum, not a hypothetical

This is a materially more positive position than the one held on May 14 before the nested-virt announcement was fully factored in. The remainder of the brief explains the reasoning and names the specific use cases that justify the investment.

## The Honest Starting Point

We are not Fly.io. We are not Northflank. We are not a hyperscaler. We are a small team building a zero-knowledge encrypted vault platform with an agentic substrate layered on top. The fact that Firecracker is technically interesting and powers Lambda is **not** by itself a reason for us to operate it.

But two things have shifted since this question was last considered:

1. **AWS nested virtualization on C8i/M8i/R8i (February 2026)** means a Firecracker host now costs under $0.09/hour on-demand, not $3+/hour for `.metal`. The substrate-cost barrier has collapsed.
2. **AI agent code execution sandboxes** have emerged as a major 2026 category. Many of them are built on Firecracker. If we want to compete in or adjacent to that space (relevant to several of our resell products from earlier today), direct Firecracker understanding is no longer optional.

The question is operational: do the workloads we currently have or plausibly will have within 18 months benefit from direct Firecracker control in ways the managed services do not deliver, and is that benefit worth the engineering investment given the now-affordable substrate?

The answer is more interesting than "no."

## What We Already Get From Firecracker Indirectly

Most of Firecracker's benefits are already in our stack via managed services:

| Benefit | How We Get It Today |
|---------|---------------------|
| Per-tenant isolation | AWS Lambda (per-function microVM), Fargate (per-task microVM), AgentCore Runtime (per-session microVM) |
| Fast cold starts | Lambda SnapStart, Fargate task startup, AgentCore Runtime's I/O-wait-free pricing model |
| Dense packing | Lambda's per-host concurrency, Fargate's container density, AgentCore's session density |
| Security isolation | All three services use Firecracker's strong isolation by default |
| Per-second / per-millisecond billing | Lambda, Fargate, AgentCore all bill at sub-second granularity |
| Snapshot-based restore | Lambda SnapStart explicitly; the others use it internally |

This is significant: **we already use Firecracker; we just do not operate it ourselves**. AWS operates it on our behalf and charges us a premium for the management layer.

The question is whether that premium is worth paying. For most of our workloads today: yes, clearly. The premium is meaningfully less than the engineering cost of building and operating a Firecracker fleet at our scale.

## When Direct Firecracker Use Wins

There are specific scenarios where managed services do not deliver what direct Firecracker would. Worth being honest about which apply to us:

### Scenario 1: Pricing At High Scale

At sufficient scale, the per-invocation premium of Lambda or Fargate adds up. A platform doing **billions** of invocations per month may find direct Firecracker on bare-metal cheaper at the unit level, even after accounting for engineering and operational cost.

**Applies to us?** Not in 2026. We are not at this scale. Even with the daily band experiment, infographic generator, and customer POCs, we are firmly in the "managed services are cheaper" regime. This scenario becomes worth revisiting if a specific customer or product surface generates millions of invocations per month.

### Scenario 2: Snapshot-Based Pre-Warmed Pools

Direct Firecracker access lets you control the snapshot lifecycle precisely: snapshot at exactly the right moment, restore on demand sub-millisecond, hold many snapshots ready. Lambda SnapStart is the AWS-managed version but is opinionated about when snapshots are taken and how they restore.

**Applies to us?** Yes, worth prototyping. The container host primitive (today's brief) wants fast startup. Firecracker snapshots are now a candidate solution we should actually measure. The Fargate experiment (today's earlier brief) will tell us how Fargate compares; a Firecracker snapshot prototype on a cheap C8i instance would tell us how Firecracker compares. With substrate cost at ~$0.09/hour, this is a reasonable experiment to run.

### Scenario 3: Custom Network Topologies

Direct Firecracker lets you build whatever network topology fits the workload: per-microVM ENIs, custom routing, internal-only networks, encrypted overlays. Managed services have specific network models you cannot deviate from.

**Applies to us?** Not currently. Our networking is straightforward.

### Scenario 4: Genuinely Untrusted Code Execution

If we host customer-uploaded code that we cannot review, per-execution microVM isolation matters. AgentCore Code Interpreter already provides this (and uses Firecracker internally). For our cases (vault apps written by customers) we have not yet decided whether to allow truly arbitrary code execution.

**Applies to us?** Increasingly yes. The AI agent code execution sandbox category exploded in 2026. Many of the leading sandboxes are built on Firecracker. If we expand the SG/App framework to let customers run arbitrary code server-side, or if any of our resell products (especially Vault-Grounded Agent Hosting and the agentic newsroom/band production team) need to run untrusted code, microVM isolation is the standard. AgentCore Code Interpreter is sufficient for the simplest cases; for anything more (custom execution environments, customer-provided container images, faster startup than Code Interpreter provides), direct Firecracker is the right substrate. **This is the strongest near-term reason to invest in direct Firecracker capability.**

### Scenario 5: Confidential Compute With Density

Nitro Enclaves give us confidential computing but at full EC2 cost per workload. If we wanted **dense** confidential computing (many small enclaves per host with strong isolation), we would need a different substrate. Future Firecracker-on-SEV-SNP or similar combinations could be that substrate, but they are not turnkey today.

**Applies to us?** Not now. May become interesting in 2027+ if confidential-compute density becomes a real product requirement.

### Scenario 6: Multi-Cloud Or Cloud-Agnostic

Firecracker runs on any bare-metal Linux host. If we wanted to operate the same substrate on AWS, Azure, GCP, and on-premise, Firecracker plus a thin abstraction is a path. Managed services lock us to one cloud.

**Applies to us?** Not in 2026. Our customers want AWS-native compliance posture and AWS-native integrations. Cloud-agnosticism is a 2027+ consideration if specific customers require it.

## The Substrate Spectrum, Now With Firecracker

Today's strategy brief on serverless-SG-workflows-beyond-Lambda named five substrates (laptop / Lambda / Fargate / EC2 container hosts / dedicated EC2). Adding Firecracker explicitly:

| Substrate | Best For | Operational Cost |
|-----------|----------|------------------|
| Laptop | Interactive development | Free, but no capture / reproducibility |
| Lambda | Short, low-frequency, stateless | Pay per invocation; AWS operates |
| AgentCore Runtime | Agent workloads with I/O wait | Pay per second; AWS operates Firecracker for you |
| Fargate | Container-shaped moderate-duration workloads | Pay per second; AWS operates Firecracker for you |
| EC2 container hosts (our brief) | Many small workloads on shared hosts | We pay for EC2; we operate the routing layer |
| Dedicated EC2 | Long-running, isolation-sensitive | We pay for EC2; we operate |
| Nitro Enclaves | Confidential computing | We pay for EC2; we operate enclave lifecycle |
| **Direct Firecracker on C8i/M8i/R8i** | **Custom isolation with bin-packing on virtual EC2 via nested virtualisation** | **We pay for C8i-family EC2 (from ~$0.09/hr); we operate everything; engineering investment in orchestration** |
| **Direct Firecracker on `.metal`** | **Higher density, better performance, no nested-virt overhead** | **We pay for `.metal` EC2 ($3+/hr); only worth it at meaningful scale** |

Direct Firecracker sits at the most-control-most-operational-burden end of the spectrum. It is meaningfully harder to operate than any other option.

The cost story has shifted. Until February 2026, "direct Firecracker" meant `.metal` EC2 at $3+/hour, which made it unviable for anything but high-scale platforms. With nested virtualization on C8i/M8i/R8i, the entry point is now a c8i-flex.large at ~$0.089/hour (about $65/month on-demand, or ~$32/month spot). That is **cheaper than running our own container hosts** of comparable size, and certainly cheaper than the engineering effort gap between managed services and direct control.

The operational-effort gap (the engineering work to build the orchestration around Firecracker) has not changed. That work is still real, and it is still meaningful. But the substrate cost no longer dominates the decision.

## What We Should Do

Four concrete decisions, in priority order:

### Decision 1: Run A Small Firecracker Proof-Of-Concept Now

Spin up a `c8i-flex.large` instance with `NestedVirtualization=enabled`. Install Firecracker via the documented build process. Boot a microVM. Take a snapshot. Restore from snapshot. Measure cold-start time and snapshot-restore time. Document the experience.

This is now a one-to-two-day exercise costing less than $5 (or under $2 on spot). The cost-benefit calculation is overwhelmingly positive for the operational understanding it provides.

Concrete acceptance: a working Firecracker microVM running our SG vault app's container image, with measured startup time, snapshot save/restore time, and a documented operating model.

### Decision 2: Continue Consuming Firecracker Via Managed Services For Most Production Workloads

Lambda, Fargate, and AgentCore Runtime stay our primary Firecracker consumption paths for the bulk of production work. The managed-service premium is meaningfully less than the engineering cost of building production-grade orchestration around direct Firecracker for general-purpose workloads.

This decision does not change with the cost shift. What changes is that we now know what we are paying for.

### Decision 3: Prototype Direct Firecracker For Specific Use Cases Where It Materially Wins

Three candidate use cases worth prototyping properly (each gets a separate dev brief if we pursue it):

| Candidate | Why Direct Firecracker May Win |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| **Vault-attached compute (the next density mode)** | A vault could be associated with a Firecracker microVM that opens fast from a snapshot when the vault is accessed and closes when idle. Tighter loop than container hosts; cheaper than dedicated EC2; more isolation than multi-vault-per-app. |
| **AI agent code execution sandboxes** | If we host customer-uploaded code (LLM-generated or otherwise), per-execution microVM isolation is the standard. AgentCore Code Interpreter covers the basics, but a self-hosted version would be cheaper at volume and gives us more control over the execution environment. |
| **Multi-region Playwright fleet** | The container hosts primitive (today's earlier brief) wants fast startup. Firecracker snapshots may deliver sub-second startup for a pre-warmed Playwright base image, which would be a meaningful improvement over container startup. |

The pattern: each candidate has a property the managed services do not deliver (vault-attached lifecycle, custom sandbox control, snapshot-fast startup) where the value is large enough to justify the engineering investment.

### Decision 4: Make Direct Firecracker A First-Class Substrate In Our Spectrum

Our `sg-compute container run` primitive (today's container hosts brief) should treat Firecracker as one of the substrates it can route to, not as a future hypothetical. Concretely:

- The placement Lambda can route a workload to Fargate, our container hosts, or a Firecracker microVM based on workload classification
- The vault collection format supports a "preferred substrate" hint that includes Firecracker
- Cost accounting handles Firecracker line items the same way as Fargate line items
- The unified observability session can query Firecracker microVM metrics and logs alongside Fargate task data

This is no longer architectural insurance for a future case; this is preparing the platform for substrates we will actually use.

## The Honest Risks

Two risks worth being explicit about, now that the position has shifted:

**Risk 1: Over-investment.** Now that direct Firecracker is operationally accessible, it is easy to over-invest in operating our own substrate when the managed services would have served us. Discipline: only build direct Firecracker capability for use cases where we can articulate a specific advantage over Lambda/Fargate/AgentCore. "We could run it ourselves cheaper" is not a sufficient reason if we cannot articulate the customer or product benefit.

**Risk 2: Under-investment.** The opposite failure: complacency that says "managed services are enough" when a product surface emerges where direct Firecracker meaningfully changes the unit economics or customer experience. AI agent code execution sandboxes are the most likely such surface in 2026.

The discipline: pick one or two use cases to prototype properly. Do not boil the ocean. Do not skip the boil entirely.

## The Build-Vs-Buy Calculus

A useful framing: the gap between "what AWS managed services give us" and "what direct Firecracker would give us" has two components: substrate cost and engineering effort. Both have shifted.

**Substrate cost gap has collapsed.** Before February 2026, direct Firecracker required $3+/hour bare-metal; the cost gap made the question moot for small teams. Now it is $0.09/hour. The substrate-cost premium of managed services over direct Firecracker is now meaningful: a Lambda function running 24/7 at moderate load is significantly more expensive than the equivalent on a c8i-flex.large; a Fargate task running continuously is comparable but only if we exclude the per-second pricing advantages and management benefits.

**Engineering effort gap has not collapsed.** Building production-grade orchestration around Firecracker (networking, scheduling, snapshots, lifecycle, monitoring, recovery) is still real work. AWS continues to add capabilities (SnapStart, AgentCore's pricing model) that close the *capability* gap.

The combined calculus today:

- For **most production workloads**, managed services are still the right answer because the engineering effort to replicate them is large
- For **specific workloads where direct Firecracker delivers properties the managed services do not** (vault-attached compute, AI agent sandboxes, snapshot-fast startup), the engineering investment is now worth making
- For **experimentation and learning**, the new substrate cost makes hands-on Firecracker work a no-brainer

The calculus shifts further if:

- A specific customer or product surface generates enough volume to make the unit-cost gap dominate (currently unlikely; possible 2027+)
- AWS does something that materially worsens its managed-service offering (unlikely)
- The AI-sandbox category grows into a major revenue line for us (plausible 2026-2027)

This is no longer a "watch and wait" position. It is a "start prototyping selectively" position.

These are not small annotations; they are real new work items. But the unit cost of investigation is now low enough that the work is plainly worth doing.

## The Position In One Sentence

We use Firecracker today indirectly via the AWS managed services that wrap it; we are about to start using it directly on cheap nested-virt instances for specific use cases where it materially outperforms the managed alternatives.
