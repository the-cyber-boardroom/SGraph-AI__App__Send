# How to Find Your Dream Job in 2026: Make It Easy for Others to Help You

The single most important principle for finding work in 2026: **make it easy for others to help you.**

This is the same principle that runs through open source software, the same principle that makes good documentation actually useful, the same principle that separates the entrepreneurs who ship from the ones who do not. The asymmetry is identical: a small amount of work on your side saves a much larger amount of work on the other person's side. When you have done that work, people refer you. When you have not, they cannot.

This article is for someone in May 2026 who is already deep into AI, who is not trying to start a company, who is not looking for consulting gigs, and who wants a reasonable full-time job at the right place. Someone who wants to cut through the current recruitment pipeline, not navigate it.

## Your Dream Job Is Two References Away

The right opportunity for you almost certainly exists inside the network you already have. Someone in your LinkedIn, your past colleagues, your community, your conference contacts knows the role that fits you. They have not connected you to it because they cannot. They do not know exactly what you want. They do not have a clean way to send your information forward. They cannot vouch for what you can do without writing the case themselves.

So they do nothing. Not because they do not care; because the cost of helping is too high.

Your job is to lower that cost to almost zero. When you do, the referrals happen. The conversation becomes "I know someone, let me send you their link" instead of "I might know someone, let me dig around and figure out if they are still looking."

## Why a Website (and Why Not Just a CV)

A CV is a static document that captures one moment in time. It optimises for the recruiter who has to read 200 of them this week. It compresses you into one page of bullet points. It does not tell anyone why you are looking, what you want next, or why you would be good at the role they are thinking of.

A website does all of those things. It is alive. You change it every week. It captures who you are, not just what you have done. The bar is now raised: a single-page Netlify site assembled in an afternoon with ChatGPT is no longer sufficient. The bar is now a multi-page, well-structured, well-architected site with depth. Anyone reading this article can build that site this week. Lovable, Cursor, Claude, and a dozen other tools make it trivial. The constraint is no longer technical. It is whether you do the thinking work first.

That thinking work is the rest of this article.

## Multiple Pages, Multiple Audiences

The mistake is to think of your job-search website as "the place where I describe myself." That framing leads to a single long page that does not serve anyone particularly well.

Instead, think of it as **multiple pages for multiple audiences**:

- **Recruiters** want to know: what role, what industry, what salary band, what location, what stage of company. They need to filter you in or out in under a minute. Give them that page. Make it punchy. Make it clear.
- **Friends and former colleagues who might refer you** want to know: what kind of opportunity should I send them? They need a sentence they can paste into a message: "I think you might want to talk to Sara, she is looking for X at companies like Y." Give them that page. Write that sentence for them.
- **Hiring managers at companies you would want to work for** want to know: what specifically have you done that maps to what they need? They need depth, evidence, and the case for why you. Give them that page. Show your work.
- **Your potential future boss** wants to know: how do you think, how do you operate, will I enjoy working with you? They need narrative, judgement, perspective. Give them that page. Let them see you think.
- **Your potential future peers** want to know: is this someone I will respect? Will they make us better? Give them that page. Show the technical or domain depth that earns respect.
- **The people you might manage** want to know: what will it be like to work for you, what do you stand for, what would change about my day? Give them that page. Show how you treat people, what you have built, how you think about leading.

Each of these is a different message. Each compresses your past experience differently. The hiring manager does not need the same story as the peer; the peer does not need the same story as the future direct report.

This is the same idea as designing SKILL.md for a software package: write the documentation for the audience that will read it, not for yourself.

## Don't Make Them Think

This is the principle that anchors everything else.

When someone lands on your website (a recruiter, a former colleague, a hiring manager), they should not have to figure out what you want. They should not have to scroll, parse, or guess. They should see, within five seconds:

- What role you are looking for
- What industry or kind of company
- What you are particularly good at
- Why that combination makes sense

If they have to think, they leave. If they leave, they cannot help you.

The right way to test this: send your website link to three people who do not know you well. Ask them: "What does this person want?" If they cannot answer in one sentence, the website is failing. Fix the framing until they can.

What this rules out:

- A long list of past roles with no narrative connecting them
- A "Skills" section with thirty bullet points
- A philosophical statement about your career journey
- A homepage that requires scrolling to understand the basic ask

What this requires:

- A single clear sentence above the fold: "I am looking for X role at Y kind of company, where I can do Z."
- Supporting links to the deeper pages (audience-specific, role-specific)
- Evidence that you can do what you claim

## Change It Every Week

Your website is not a final artefact. It is a living surface. The advantage you have over a CV is that you can iterate.

Try a different framing this week. See who clicks. See what conversations come back. Test which roles attract you and which attract hiring managers. The bar is now low enough that updating your site takes minutes; the benefit of running ten variations is huge.

This also signals something important to the people who land on the site: this person is active, this person is thinking, this person has not given up and put a static CV online and hoped for the best.

## Hyperlink Everything

Your past experiences are not a list. They are a graph. The job you had three years ago led to the project two years ago which led to the talk you gave last year which led to the open source contribution you are still maintaining. Make those connections explicit.

Every page on your website should link to other pages on your website. Every job should link to projects from that job; every project should link to the technologies or domains involved; every domain should link back to the roles where you used it. The reader can follow whichever thread interests them.

This is the same property that makes Wikipedia useful: a non-linear, connected, hyperlinked structure that respects the reader's curiosity. Apply it to your own history.

A side effect: the graph also serves you. When you start writing the connections out, you discover narratives in your own work that you had not noticed. The thread that runs through everything you have done becomes visible. That thread is often the strongest pitch you can make about yourself.

## Show Your Work, Publicly

The single most underused move in 2026: publish what you are working on, while you are working on it.

This article is an example. The voice memo that produced it took twelve minutes to record; the article that resulted is now a permanent piece of content that demonstrates how I think about open source, how I think about helping others, how I think about communication itself. Anyone who reads it learns something true about me that no CV bullet could communicate.

You can do the same. Pick something you are doing this week (a project, a problem you are solving, a tool you are evaluating) and write about it publicly. Put it on your site. Link to the code, the issues, the decisions you made and why. Add narrative: why does this matter, who is it for, what did you learn?

A few of these accumulate into a portfolio that is qualitatively different from a CV. Anyone reading them can see:

- How you think
- What you care about
- The depth of your technical or domain skill
- Your communication ability
- The kind of work you produce when nobody is supervising you

That last one matters more than people realise. Many candidates are good when watched and mediocre when not. Public work is unsupervised work. If yours is good, that is a strong signal.

## Tell People What You Will Give Them

The instinct in a job search is to talk about yourself: your skills, your experience, your aspirations. This is backwards.

The better instinct is to talk about what you will give to the person you work for. What problems will you solve? What capabilities will you bring to the team? What will be different in their world six months after they hire you?

This is especially powerful for the audiences who might manage you or hire you. They want to know what they will get, not what you have done. Translate your past into future value, explicitly, in their language.

It also helps the people who might refer you. "Sara is great" is not useful to send to a hiring manager. "Sara is the person who turned around our security posture in nine months and would do the same for you" is something they can paste into an email.

Make that sentence for them. Put it on your website. Let them paste it.

## The Larger Pattern

This whole approach is a specific case of a general principle: **the asymmetric value of generosity in a connected world.**

When you make it easy for others to help you, they help you. When you make it easy for others to refer you, they refer you. When you make it easy for hiring managers to understand you, they hire you. The work you do upfront is small. The return is disproportionate, because it taps into the much larger amount of energy that other people are willing to spend on your behalf, if you make it cheap for them to do so.

This is also the principle behind every good open source project, every clear technical document, every effective marketing site, every well-designed product. The producer absorbs the cognitive load so the consumer does not have to.

The 2026 job market rewards this pattern more than any previous job market did. The tools to build the surface are free. The audience is reachable in a click. The competition is doing the old thing (sending CVs to recruiters and hoping). You do not have to outwork them. You have to think differently about who you are designing for.

Design for the person who might help you. Make their job easy. The opportunities will find you.
