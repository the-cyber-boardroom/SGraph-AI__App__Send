# How Vibe Coded Apps Actually Ship to Production: The Explorer, Villager, Town Planner Workflow

There is a pattern playing out in every company that has tried to build something with AI dev tools. The business stakeholder vibe codes an MVP. It demos beautifully. The first real users hit it. Things break. The team that built the MVP cannot fix the production problems. The team that could fix them was never brought in. The project either limps along or quietly dies.

The "GenAI horror stories" you see every week are almost all this story.

The fix is not to stop letting business stakeholders vibe code. The fix is to recognise that vibe coding produces something specific (a great MVP, an excellent brief), and to hand that something to teams structured to take it forward.

This article is about the workflow that gets vibe coded apps into production successfully. The framing is Wardley mapping. The roles are Explorer, Villager, and Town Planner.

## What Has Actually Changed

For the first time, business owners can build a working representation of their vision themselves. Not a wireframe, not a slide deck, not a spreadsheet pretending to model the app. An actual interactive thing that responds, that takes input, that produces output.

This is the feedback loop they have been missing for decades. Previously, the closest a business owner could get to validating their idea was a spreadsheet, a slide deck, or a written brief handed to a dev team that would come back six months later with something subtly different from what was asked for.

Now the business owner can iterate themselves. They can show ten versions to customers in a week. They can throw away nine of them. They can refine the one that works. By the time they hand anything over to a development team, the brief has been validated against reality.

That is genuinely new. It is also genuinely valuable. The mistake is in what comes next.

## The Mistake: Treating the MVP as a Product

The vibe coded MVP works on the demo machine. It handles the happy path. It looks polished. The business stakeholder shows it to investors, to partners, to potential customers. Everyone is impressed.

Then real users start using it.

The app crashes on edge cases the demo never hit. The data layer cannot handle concurrent writes. The auth flow falls apart on mobile browsers. The third-party integration times out under load. The logging is non-existent so nobody knows why things are broken. The security model assumed everyone is the developer.

This is not a flaw in vibe coding. This is the entire point of the next two stages of the workflow.

## Explorer, Villager, Town Planner

The Wardley mapping framing names three stages, each with different work, different teams, and different success criteria.

### Explorer

The Explorer builds the MVP. They are looking for what works. They optimise for speed of learning, not for production readiness. The output is a working representation that proves the idea.

In 2026, the Explorer is often the business stakeholder themselves, using Lovable, Cursor, Claude, or one of the dozens of AI dev tools available. They are not engineers. They do not need to be. They are validating whether the idea is right.

The Explorer's output is the MVP. The Explorer's job is done when the MVP shows the idea works.

### Villager

The Villager takes the Explorer's MVP and turns it into something that can actually be shipped. They read the vibe coded source. They often rewrite chunks of it from scratch, because the vibe coded version was not built for maintenance. They add tests. They separate concerns. They introduce frameworks where the MVP had ad-hoc code.

This work is best suited to junior and mid-level engineers. It is structured, well-bounded, and benefits from the kind of careful pattern-following that experienced engineers can find tedious. The MVP is the brief; the Villager produces the productionable version.

The Villager's output is an application that handles real users at modest scale, with reasonable reliability and maintainability. The Villager's job is done when the application is signed off by the business stakeholder and is being used by paying customers.

### Town Planner

The Town Planner takes the Villager's working application and hardens it for scale, security, resilience, and long-term operational maturity. They focus on the Non-Functional Requirements (NFRs) that were intentionally deferred at the earlier stages.

This work is best suited to the most experienced engineers on the team. It requires deep understanding of distributed systems, security, observability, operational practice, and the trade-offs each entails. Town Planners often do further rewriting and optimisation; they really read the code, and they care about properties that do not show up in feature lists.

The Town Planner's output is an application that can run reliably at scale, recover from failures, withstand attacks, and be maintained for years. The Town Planner's job is never quite "done"; it evolves with the application.

## The Vibe Coded MVP Is the Best Brief You Could Get

This is the key insight that makes the workflow work: the Villager team does not need the business stakeholder to write a specification. The vibe coded MVP **is** the specification. It is more precise, more concrete, and more usable than any document the business stakeholder could have written.

The Villager team reads the MVP. They see exactly which screens exist, exactly which fields are captured, exactly which user paths are supported. They see what the business stakeholder built, which tells them what the business stakeholder actually wanted (not what they would have written they wanted in a document).

This collapses the requirements-gathering phase that traditionally consumes weeks or months. The brief is already there, in working form. The Villager team's job is to rebuild it properly.

## Why the Old Workflow Was So Broken

Before AI dev tools, the same three stages existed but were collapsed into one team, and the stages were not named, so they were not managed.

A single engineering team had to do all of it: prototype, productionise, harden. The business stakeholder handed them a brief and expected production-ready software back. There was no separate phase where the business validated the idea before engineers committed to building it. There was no clean handover between exploration and hardening.

What we ended up with was a constant negotiation about NFRs. The engineers wanted to invest in security, observability, resilience, scalability. The business stakeholders did not see the value. The customers using the end product never knew to ask for any of it. So NFRs were chronically under-funded.

Coming from one of those NFRs (security), my own experience was that the challenge was never "what are the vulnerabilities to fix?" It was always "how do we fund this work, and how do we get the team time to do it?"

When the business stakeholders cannot see something, they will not pay for it. When the customers cannot perceive it, they will not demand it. When both of those things are true, the work does not happen, no matter how badly it needs to.

This produced the now-familiar frustration on both sides:

The business side: "These engineers are complicating everything. The thing is working from where I sit. Why can't we just ship it? Why does it take so long to make changes and push new features?"

The engineering side: "These executives have no idea how to actually ship a product. We keep adding more and more tech debt, and we never have the time or budget to focus on doing it properly."

Both sides were right, given their information. Neither side had the framework to discuss it. Wardley mapping gives you the framework: you have an Explorer/Villager/Town Planner problem, and you are trying to solve it with one undifferentiated team.

## Then AI Came Along

For a moment, the business stakeholders thought they had won. The AI dev tools made it so they could build the apps themselves. The narrative was clear: fire the engineers, save the budget, ship faster, win the market.

The first month went brilliantly. The vibe coded MVPs were impressive. The demos to investors and customers landed. The business stakeholders felt validated for the years of frustration with engineering teams that did not understand the urgency.

Then production hit. The apps that worked perfectly in the demo broke on the first real customer. The data got corrupted. The auth flow failed for one in three users. The third-party API rate-limited them. The site went down on the day they had press coverage. Recovery took days because nobody knew how to operate the system that the AI had built.

Some of these projects never made it to production at all. Others made it briefly and were quietly retired. The ones that survived are the ones whose business stakeholders eventually said: "Actually, those NFRs are really important. Let's bring back the engineers to push our apps into production."

That moment is the win. Not because engineers are vindicated, but because the workflow becomes legible. The business stakeholder is no longer fighting against the engineering work; they are sponsoring it, because they now understand what it produces.

## The Workflow That Actually Works

Here is the loop, named and ordered:

1. **The business stakeholder vibe codes the brief.** They are the Explorer. They iterate as much as they want. They show it to potential users, get feedback, refine. This phase takes days or weeks, not months.

2. **The vibe coded version is handed to the Villager team.** The team is staffed with junior and mid-level engineers. They read the vibe coded source as a specification. They rewrite the app properly, with tests, framework structure, separation of concerns. This is where real code-reading and code-writing happens.

3. **The Villager's app is shown back to the business stakeholder and given to real users.** Sign-off happens here. The business stakeholder confirms that the Villager team built what they actually meant. Real customers start using the app at modest scale.

4. **Once Villager features are signed off, they pass to the Town Planner team.** The Town Planner team is the most experienced engineers in the organisation. They harden the application for the NFRs: security, observability, resilience, scalability, operational maturity. They genuinely read the code, deeply. They make changes that are invisible to users but essential for the application to run reliably for years.

5. **The Town Planner team feeds back into the loop.** They surface issues that need to be addressed in the Villager phase of future features. They define standards. They are the people who set the bar for what production looks like in this organisation.

Critically: these are three different teams with three different skill sets, three different priorities, and three different success criteria. You do not staff them with the same people. You do not measure them on the same metrics.

## Why This Workflow Captures the Best of Both Worlds

The business stakeholder gets the speed and agency they wanted from vibe coding. They iterate on the idea themselves, without engineering being a bottleneck. They validate against real users before committing to building anything for real.

The engineering team gets the NFRs they wanted. The Villager team has a precise brief (the vibe coded MVP) and the time to do their work properly. The Town Planner team has signed-off Villager work to harden, without having to also negotiate requirements.

Both sides get the funding alignment they needed. The business stakeholder, having gone through the loop once, now understands viscerally why the Villager and Town Planner stages are necessary. They watched their MVP fail in production. They asked for help. They are sponsoring the work because they can see what it produces.

The customers get a product that actually works in production, was built with real user feedback, and will not break on edge cases. They never had to know about NFRs to benefit from them.

## The Examples Are All Around You

The "GenAI horror stories" published every week are almost entirely examples of what happens when the workflow above is not followed. The Explorer ships the MVP straight to production. The Villager and Town Planner stages are skipped. The application fails predictably, and the failure modes are documented for the rest of us to learn from.

The success stories, conversely, are the ones where someone in the organisation understood the workflow. They let the business stakeholder vibe code. They brought in engineers to productionise. They invested in NFRs after the MVP proved the idea. They shipped something that actually worked.

The pattern is repeatable. It is not about which AI dev tool you use, or which language you write the final code in, or which cloud you deploy on. It is about the structural decision to treat MVPs as MVPs, productions as productions, and the work of getting from one to the other as worth doing properly.

## What This Means for You

If you are a business stakeholder: vibe code aggressively. Build ten versions. Throw away nine. Show the surviving one to ten real users. By the time you hand it to engineers, you will have a brief that is worth their time to implement properly. Plan and fund the Villager and Town Planner phases from day one. The MVP is not the product.

If you are an engineer: do not fight vibe coded MVPs. They are the best brief you will ever get. Read them as specifications, not as competition. Your Villager and Town Planner work is what makes them real. The business stakeholder who went through one bad production deployment will become your strongest advocate for NFRs.

If you are leading a team: name the three stages. Staff them differently. Measure them differently. Make the handovers explicit. Stop trying to do all three with one team and one budget; that is what produced the last two decades of frustration on both sides.

The workflow is not new. The Wardley mapping framing has been around for years. What is new is that the Explorer stage can now be done by the people who actually have the business idea. That removes the worst part of the old workflow (the engineers guessing at requirements) and lets every stage do what it is good at.

Build it that way. Watch what happens.
