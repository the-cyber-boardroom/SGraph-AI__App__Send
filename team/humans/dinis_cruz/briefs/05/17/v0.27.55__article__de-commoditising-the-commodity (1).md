# De-Commoditising The Commodity: When Attrition Hides Inside The Cloud You Are Using

**version** v0.27.55
**date** 17 May 2026
**author** Dinis Cruz
**type** Article

---

## Summary

A pattern that Wardley Mapping touches but does not name cleanly: **a thing can be commoditised at one phase of evolution while still being meaningfully custom-built at the next phase**. The standard reading of a Wardley Map is that once something reaches commodity, you stop building and start consuming. This is usually right, but not always. Sometimes the commodity you are consuming creates enough attrition in your next phase that the rational move is to **build a custom layer that absorbs the attrition**: a custom layer that, in turn, makes the commodity feel like a commodity again to the rest of your system.

I am going to call this pattern **de-commoditisation**, with the caveat that the term is doing some work. We are not un-commoditising the underlying commodity; we are building a custom shield around it so that the experience of using it remains commodity-like for our specific case. The shield is custom-built code that handles the gotchas, the cold-starts, the rate limits, the inconsistencies, the operational quirks that the abstract commodity does not fix because they are not in scope for the commodity provider.

In the age of AI-augmented development, the economics of building this custom shield have changed substantially. Cases that would have been irrational to build five years ago (because the engineering cost would dwarf the operational savings) are increasingly rational now (because AI-assisted development collapses the build cost). This makes the de-commoditisation pattern relevant in places where it previously was not.

The article works through the concept, gives the canonical example (AWS Lambda), generalises the pattern, and ends with the practical decision framework: **when should you wait for the commodity to improve, and when should you build the shield?**

## The Standard Wardley Mapping Reading

The Wardley evolution scale runs left to right: Genesis → Custom Built → Product → Commodity / Utility. The implicit decision rule is "build at the leftward stages; consume at the rightward stages." Once something reaches commodity, the work has been done by someone else; your job is to use it well.

This reading is correct in the median case. Electricity is a commodity; you do not generate your own. Cloud object storage is a commodity; you do not run your own data centre. DNS is a commodity; you do not run your own root servers. For most workloads, accepting the commodity is the right call.

But the median case is not the only case. The Wardley framing names the evolution dimension cleanly but is less precise about what "commodity" means in operational terms. Specifically: **commodity for whom?** A thing can be commodity-shaped for the explorer (someone trying it for the first time), commodity-shaped for the villager (someone running their first production workload), and still not commodity-shaped for the town planner (someone running it at scale across thousands of workloads with stringent operational requirements).

This matters because attrition compounds at scale. A small inconvenience in a commodity that does not matter for a single workload becomes a significant cost at a thousand workloads, and a dominant cost at a million.

## The Attrition Question

The right question to ask about any commodity in your stack is not "is this commoditised?" but **"does using this introduce attrition into my next phase?"**

By attrition I mean the operational friction, the workarounds, the gotchas, the special-case code, the limits you have to design around, the rate limits you have to back off from, the configurations you have to remember. None of these things stop the commodity from being useful. All of them accumulate cost over time, and the cost is paid by your team rather than by the commodity provider.

Attrition is invisible in the Wardley Map because the map shows components, not their operational shape. Two services can both be "commodity" on the map; one introduces no attrition for your use case; the other introduces a great deal. The map does not tell you which is which.

The pattern of de-commoditisation starts with naming attrition: where is your stack chewing up time, attention, and cost in ways that are not delivering proportionate value? Those are the places where the commodity you are consuming may not actually be commodity-shaped for your operational context, even if the Wardley Map says it is.

## The Canonical Example: AWS Lambda

AWS Lambda is the cleanest example of a commodity that is genuinely commoditised at one phase and meaningfully custom-built at another. As a function-execution primitive, it is commodity-shaped. You write a function; you upload it; AWS runs it when traffic arrives; you are billed per invocation. The Wardley Map places it firmly on the right.

But everyone who has run Lambda at scale knows that the right side of the map hides a substantial amount of operational reality. Cold starts that vary by runtime and package size. Hot pool behaviour that you cannot directly control. Reuse of execution contexts that breaks the "stateless function" abstraction in subtle ways. Concurrent execution limits that bite when traffic spikes. Pricing models that become expensive faster than developers expect when workloads are bursty or parallel. Difficulty inspecting a running function. Difficulty stopping a misbehaving function without modifying code and redeploying.

None of these things stop Lambda from being commoditised. All of them mean that running real workloads on Lambda involves writing a layer of custom code that absorbs the gotchas. Warmer functions to mitigate cold starts. Reservation strategies to handle concurrency limits. Custom observability because the built-in observability does not show you what you need to see. Wrapper code that handles the cold-start state-reset case explicitly. Cost-optimisation logic that picks the right memory tier per function based on actual usage.

This custom code is not commoditised. It is custom-built per organisation, often duplicated across teams within the same organisation, with no shared substrate or vendor. **The commodity is Lambda; the de-commoditisation layer is everything you build around Lambda to make it usable for your specific case.**

If the de-commoditisation layer becomes large enough (and for teams running serious Lambda workloads, it often does), the question is no longer "are we using a commodity?" but "are we building enough custom infrastructure around the commodity that we might as well build the whole thing ourselves?"

Increasingly, the answer is yes. Companies that went all-in on AWS in the late 2010s are pulling workloads back to dedicated infrastructure (their own data centres, alternative clouds, or a hybrid) not because the AWS services stopped working but because the de-commoditisation layer they had built around AWS had grown large enough that owning the whole stack was cheaper, faster, and more controllable. The "37signals leaving the cloud" story is the most visible example; many others are happening quietly.

This is the de-commoditisation pattern in action: **the custom layer around the commodity grew large enough that the commodity stopped being the rational choice for that workload**.

## Generalising The Pattern

Lambda is not the only place this happens. The pattern shows up wherever a commodity's abstract simplicity hides operational complexity for specific use cases:

| Commodity | Abstract Promise | What Real Attrition Looks Like |
|-----------|------------------|-------------------------------|
| Managed Kubernetes (EKS, GKE) | "Run containers; cluster managed for you" | Network policy debugging; CRD compatibility; upgrade cycles; node-group sizing |
| Managed databases (RDS, Cloud SQL) | "Database; we run it" | Connection pooling at scale; instance sizing for spiky loads; backup strategies; replication lag |
| Email infrastructure (SES, SendGrid) | "Send email; high deliverability" | IP reputation warming; bounce handling; DMARC alignment; complaint feedback loops |
| LLM APIs (OpenAI, Anthropic) | "Send prompt; get completion" | Rate limit handling; cost spikes; output validation; prompt versioning; fallback routing |
| Object storage (S3, GCS) | "Store objects; durable; cheap" | Eventual consistency edge cases; multipart upload edge cases; pricing tier selection; access pattern optimisation |
| CDN (CloudFront, Cloudflare) | "Cache content; fast globally" | Cache invalidation patterns; origin shield configuration; pricing tier selection; debugging cache misses |
| Authentication (Auth0, Cognito) | "Identity; we handle it" | Custom claim mapping; M2M auth pricing; migration complexity; multi-tenant patterns |

In each case the commodity is real. In each case the attrition is also real. The question is whether the attrition is small enough to absorb (most teams) or large enough to warrant building a layer around (some teams).

The pattern is more visible in 2026 than it was five years ago because two things changed. First, the cloud commodity providers have stopped innovating as fast as they did from 2015-2020; their incremental improvements have slowed, leaving gaps that are now stable enough to design around. Second, AI-augmented development has dropped the cost of building infrastructure code by enough that previously-irrational custom builds are now rational. A two-engineer team can now build the kind of de-commoditisation layer that previously required a five-engineer team-quarter.

## What The Custom Layer Looks Like

The custom layer around a commodity is rarely a complete replacement of the commodity. It is more typically a shield that absorbs the attrition while still using the commodity underneath. Specifically:

| Layer Role | What It Does |
|------------|--------------|
| Adapter | Makes the commodity's API look like the API your application actually wants |
| Buffer | Absorbs rate limits, retries, transient failures so your application code does not see them |
| Optimiser | Picks the right commodity-tier or configuration based on workload patterns |
| Observability | Adds the visibility the commodity does not provide |
| Cost-shaper | Routes work between commodity tiers (or providers) to optimise cost vs latency |
| Fallback | Switches to an alternative when the commodity is degraded or unavailable |

Each layer role is non-trivial to build. Each is also bounded in scope: the custom layer does not need to do what the commodity does; it only needs to handle the specific attrition the commodity introduces for the specific case. This bounded scope is what makes the de-commoditisation pattern tractable. We are not rebuilding Lambda; we are building the specific wrapper that makes Lambda usable for our use case.

In Wardley terms, the custom layer is at the Product or Custom-Built stage of evolution, sitting on top of a Commodity. The map shows the dependency clearly. What the map does not show is whether the custom layer is large enough to dominate the value chain. Sometimes it is.

## The Decision Framework

Practically, the question for any team using a commodity that introduces attrition is: **wait for the commodity to improve, or build the shield?**

The right answer depends on five factors:

| Factor | Argues For Waiting | Argues For Building |
|--------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Rate of commodity improvement** | Fast (provider is actively closing the gaps) | Slow or stagnant |
| **Severity of attrition** | Manageable; team can work around it | Painful; eating significant engineering time |
| **Cost of building the shield** | High (would dominate roadmap) | Low (especially with AI augmentation) |
| **Specificity of your use case** | Generic (the commodity will eventually fit) | Highly specific (the commodity will never fit cleanly) |
| **Strategic value of the shield** | None (commodity work, no differentiation) | High (the shield itself is differentiating) |

When most factors point right, build the shield. When most point left, wait. When they are mixed, the deciding question is usually the fifth: **is the shield itself a strategic asset?**

This is where the de-commoditisation pattern becomes interesting strategically. The custom layer you build to absorb commodity attrition can become a moat in its own right. It encodes your specific operational knowledge. It is hard for competitors to replicate because they would have to make the same mistakes you made and learn the same gotchas. It is differentiated because it is shaped to your workload, not to the lowest common denominator.

In effect, **the shield is where your specific operational expertise lives, and where commodity providers cannot reach because their scope is different**.

## The AI-Era Reframe

The most consequential update to this pattern in 2026 is the change in build economics. Building a custom layer around a commodity historically required substantial engineering time. The break-even point, where the shield costs less than the attrition over its lifetime, was high enough that most teams correctly decided to absorb the attrition.

AI-augmented development changes this. The shield that took six engineer-months in 2020 might take six engineer-weeks in 2026. The break-even point moves leftward. Cases that were irrational to build before are rational now.

This matters for product strategy because it means **the surface area where de-commoditisation is the right answer is growing**. Teams that look at their stack honestly will find more places where building a custom shield is justified than they would have found two years ago. The Wardley Map has not moved; the economics of acting on it have.

The implication for platform-builders is similar but inverted: if you are building a platform that competes with a hyperscaler commodity, the de-commoditisation pattern is your wedge. You do not need to compete head-on with the commodity. You need to address the attrition that the commodity creates for a specific class of customer. The custom layer becomes your product.

This is the framing my own platform work has been converging on. We are not trying to be AWS Lambda. We are building the specific layer that absorbs Lambda's attrition for agent-shaped workloads: parallel-request handling that does not penalise concurrency, vault-grounded state instead of vendor databases, control of lifecycle that Lambda does not allow, optional confidential compute via Nitro Enclaves. The commodity (AWS compute primitives, Bedrock, S3) is still underneath. The custom layer is what makes the experience commodity-shaped for our specific case.

In Wardley terms: we are sitting on commodity, building product, and the value of the product is the attrition it absorbs.

## When De-Commoditisation Goes Wrong

The pattern is not always the right call. Three failure modes are worth naming.

**Failure mode one: building the shield because you do not understand the commodity.** Teams sometimes build elaborate wrappers around services they have not actually learned. The wrapper duplicates capabilities the service already has; the team ends up maintaining a layer that exists because of their own knowledge gap, not because of real attrition. The discipline here is to verify the attrition is real before building around it.

**Failure mode two: the shield grows faster than the commodity it shields.** Sometimes a custom layer starts reasonable and ends up so large that it has its own attrition, which a meta-shield then has to absorb. This is how organisations end up with bespoke platforms that are more painful than the commodities they were meant to improve on. The discipline here is to periodically re-check whether the commodity has improved enough to retire the shield.

**Failure mode three: building the shield in isolation when a community shield exists.** For many common attrition patterns, open-source layers already exist. Teams that build their own from scratch are duplicating work that has been generalised by others. The discipline here is to look for existing shields (LangChain for LLM attrition, K8s operators for Kubernetes attrition, Pulumi or Terraform for infra attrition) before writing your own.

When done well, de-commoditisation produces a layer that is small, focused, well-understood by the team that owns it, and clearly necessary because removing it would re-expose the attrition. When done poorly, it produces a parallel system that is hard to justify and hard to remove.

## The Practical Question

If you take one thing from this piece, take this: **the next time you look at a commodity in your stack, ask not "is this commoditised?" but "is this introducing attrition that I am working around?"**

If the answer is no, the commodity is doing its job. Use it; do not over-engineer around it.

If the answer is yes, the next question is whether the attrition is small enough to absorb or large enough to shield. The factor framework above gives you a structured way to answer.

And if you decide to build the shield, treat it explicitly. Name it. Give it a scope. Maintain it as a deliberate piece of your architecture rather than something that grew up around the commodity by accident. The shield, done right, is where your operational expertise lives. That expertise is worth taking seriously.

In an era where the underlying commodities are powerful but generic, **the shields are where the value is increasingly created**. The Wardley Map shows the commodity. The shield is what the map does not show. Both matter.
