# The SME Opportunity In GenAI: Resistance Reframed, And Why Workflows Are The Missing Piece

**version** v0.27.55
**date** 17 May 2026
**author** Dinis Cruz
**type** Article

---

## Summary

A piece on where the focus should be when working with small and medium enterprises (SMEs) on GenAI adoption, particularly in service industries like accounting, law, healthcare, and other specialist trades. The argument runs against two prevailing narratives.

First, **the narrative that AI will replace these professionals** is wrong about the work but right about the noise it creates. The replacement framing is the single biggest obstacle to adoption, and dismantling it is necessary before the conversation can move forward.

Second, **the narrative that SMEs are technology-resistant** is also wrong, just in a different way. These companies have already adopted four major technology transitions in living memory: paper to email, paper-and-post to internet, desktop to mobile, basic computing to apps. The GenAI transition is structurally a smaller jump than any of those, but it has been positioned as a larger one because of the replacement narrative.

The opportunity, when these two narratives are corrected, is sharper than it appears. **The barrier to adoption is not technological; it is the absence of workflows that fit how these professionals actually work.** Building those workflows, with respect for the decades of craft embedded in existing practice, is where the real opportunity lives.

## The Replacement Narrative Is Costing Adoption

When you talk to a partner at a small accountancy firm, or a senior solicitor at a regional law practice, or a doctor running a small clinic, the conversation about AI almost always opens with some version of "they say it is going to replace us." Not as a question; as an observation about the surrounding discourse.

This is genuinely a problem for adoption, and it is not the professional's fault. The technology press, the consulting class, the investor decks, and the AI-vendor marketing have spent two years framing the change as one of substitution: better automation will replace human work. The framing is not entirely wrong (it applies in some narrow contexts), but it is wrong as the dominant narrative for the kind of expert services SMEs provide.

The problem with the replacement narrative is not that it makes professionals feel insecure (though it does). The deeper problem is that it makes adoption look like an act of self-harm. If the tool's purpose is to replace you, why would you adopt it enthusiastically? The rational response is exactly what we see: cautious engagement, slow integration, and a quiet "wait and see" posture that puts the firm 6-12 months behind the curve.

The replacement narrative also misses what these professionals actually do. **A senior accountant is not paid to add columns of figures; they are paid to know which figures to add, why those, and what to do with the result.** A senior lawyer is not paid to read documents; they are paid to know which precedents apply, where the risks are, and how to negotiate. A doctor is not paid to type into a patient record; they are paid to recognise patterns across years of patient interactions and judge what intervention is appropriate. These are judgement-heavy, context-rich, relationship-grounded activities that do not commoditise the way the replacement narrative implies.

The right reframe is **augmentation**, but even that word does not land well because it has been overused. What actually happens in practice when these professionals start using AI well is that they spend less time on the mechanical parts of their work and more time on the parts where their judgement matters most. The work becomes more itself, not less.

## SMEs Have Already Done Four Tech Transitions

A separate narrative that needs correcting: the framing of SMEs as technology-resistant or technology-laggards. This is almost universally how SMEs in service industries get described in vendor pitch decks and consulting reports, and it is not accurate.

Consider the technology transitions a typical small professional services firm has lived through in the last 30-40 years:

| Transition | What Changed | When |
|------------|--------------|------|
| **Paper to email** | All client communication shifted from posted letters and faxes to email | 1995-2005 |
| **Paper/post to internet research** | Reference materials, regulatory updates, market data moved online | 1998-2008 |
| **Desktop to mobile** | Communication, document review, calendar management became mobile-first | 2008-2015 |
| **Manual to apps** | Specialist software replaced general-purpose tools for accounting, legal research, medical records | 2010-present |

Every one of these transitions changed the day-to-day operation of these firms profoundly. None of them happened without resistance. All of them got adopted, in the end, because the firms that adopted them outperformed those that did not.

A firm that today says "we are not very technology-focused" is using a self-description that does not match the reality. They use email constantly. They have firm-wide accounting software, practice management software, or electronic health records. They do client work on laptops. They check messages on phones. They use cloud services (often without realising they are using cloud services, because the marketing has moved past that term). The technology stack of a "non-technical" small accountancy firm in 2026 would have been considered cutting-edge in 2005.

**The honest description is not "technology-resistant" but "technology-conservative."** They adopt when the case is clear, the workflow is mature, and the risk profile is understood. They do not adopt because someone published an excited blog post. This is a feature, not a bug; it is part of why these firms have survived as long as they have.

## The Positive Function Of Resistance

The voice memo I am working from named something worth being explicit about: **the resistance to change in these firms has a positive function**. It is not pure inertia. It is the embodied judgement of people who have learned, often the hard way, that not every new thing is worth adopting.

These professionals have seen waves of technology come and go. They have seen consultants pitch transformations that did not pan out. They have seen colleagues invest in systems that became maintenance burdens. Their caution is calibrated.

This matters because it suggests the right adoption strategy is not "convince them faster." It is "give them something demonstrably worth adopting." When the case is strong, these firms adopt. When the case is weak, they correctly hold off. The job of someone building tools for this audience is to make the case strong, not to overcome the resistance.

The corollary: **tools that are demonstrably useful spread inside this audience faster than people think**. The accountant who finds something genuinely good shows it to their colleagues at the next CPD event. The lawyer mentions it in a peer network. The doctor brings it up in a clinical meeting. This is slower than viral consumer adoption, but it is structural and durable. A tool that earns its way in stays in.

## The GenAI Transition Is Smaller, Not Larger

Here is the surprising claim, and the one that takes most work to make stick: **for these audiences, the GenAI transition is structurally smaller than the previous four transitions, not larger**.

This sounds wrong because the AI marketing positions it as transformative, world-changing, paradigm-shifting. But for the practical day-to-day experience of an accountant, lawyer, or doctor adopting tools, what does GenAI actually require?

| Earlier Transition | What Adoption Required |
|--------------------|------------------------|
| Paper to email | Learn a new communication medium; change client expectations; new filing system |
| Internet research | New tools; new search behaviour; new ways of verifying sources |
| Desktop to mobile | New devices; new operating systems; new app paradigms; new security models |
| Specialist apps | Learn new vendor-specific software; migrate years of data; train teams |
| **GenAI** | **Type questions or paste documents into a chat box; read the answer; check it** |

The GenAI interaction model is genuinely simpler than the apps that preceded it. There is no new device. The interface is conversational, not menu-driven. The learning curve to get useful output is hours, not weeks. Most professionals who have tried Claude or ChatGPT or similar tools were producing useful output within a single session.

What is harder about GenAI is not the technical adoption. It is two adjacent things:

**First**, working out where it is reliable enough to trust and where it is not. This is a real skill and takes time to develop, but it is not a barrier to starting; it is something that gets refined through use.

**Second**, integrating it into actual work patterns. This is the workflow question, which is the heart of this article.

## Workflows Are The Missing Piece

When you watch SME professionals try to use GenAI, the failure mode is rarely "they could not figure out the chat interface." The failure mode is "they tried it once, did not see how to fit it into how they actually work, and stopped."

A solo accountant has a workflow that has been refined over twenty years. Client onboarding goes a certain way. Year-end goes a certain way. VAT returns go a certain way. The workflow is mostly tacit, embedded in habits and templates and the specific software the firm has standardised on. A new tool that does not fit the workflow gets used in isolation, briefly, and then abandoned.

This is exactly the same pattern that delayed earlier technology adoption. Email did not spread because the technology improved; it spread because email-aware workflows emerged. The internet did not spread because browsers got better; it spread because business processes that assumed internet access became the norm. Apps did not spread because mobile got faster; they spread because workflows formed around them.

**The bottleneck for GenAI adoption in SME service industries is workflows that fit how the professionals actually work**, not the technology itself.

This is the opportunity. The professional knows the work intimately. The professional has the relationships, the regulatory understanding, the judgement. What the professional does not have is the time or technical inclination to design GenAI-augmented workflows from scratch. **Someone needs to build those workflows in collaboration with the professionals, and then make the workflows portable and accessible enough that other professionals in the same trade can adopt them too.**

This is not a software product, exactly. It is closer to a methodology with software components. The product is the workflow; the software is what makes the workflow tractable.

## What Good Workflows Look Like

Concretely, what does a GenAI-augmented workflow for, say, a small accountancy firm look like? Some properties:

- **Starts from existing practice, not a blank page.** The workflow respects how the firm currently handles year-end, VAT, client onboarding, management accounts. GenAI fits into specific steps rather than replacing the whole flow.
- **Preserves the audit trail.** Every AI-touched step has a record of what was done, by whom or by what, and is reviewable. This matters professionally and legally.
- **Surfaces uncertainty.** When the AI is confident, it says so. When it is not, the workflow flags the step for human review. Professionals trust tools that know what they do not know.
- **Keeps the professional in the loop.** AI does the first pass; the professional validates and adjusts. The professional remains accountable, which they would insist on regardless.
- **Saves time, demonstrably.** Vague productivity claims do not move adoption. "This step used to take 40 minutes; it now takes 8" does.
- **Is file-friendly.** SMEs in these industries work in files (spreadsheets, PDFs, Word docs). Workflows that require everything to live in a SaaS database fail; workflows that ingest files, process them, and emit files succeed.
- **Allows for local context.** The accountant in Manchester serves different clients than the one in Madrid; the workflow needs to be adaptable, not rigidly templated.

Each of these properties is achievable today with current GenAI tools. None of them are happening at scale, because nobody has prioritised the workflow design work over the technology marketing.

## The Democratising Effect, Done Right

A point worth being explicit about. The voice memo named this directly: **the GenAI transition, if positioned correctly, is more democratising than the previous transitions were**. The earlier transitions tended to advantage the technically inclined. Email rewarded the people who set up filtering systems first. Mobile rewarded those who configured their devices well. Apps rewarded those who could navigate complex software UIs.

GenAI inverts some of this. The interface is conversational. The tool meets the user where they are linguistically. A senior partner who has not learned a new software application in fifteen years can be productive in Claude or Chat GPT within an hour. A doctor who finds technology frustrating can use AI to dictate notes, summarise patient histories, draft letters, without learning a new UI paradigm.

This means the population of professionals who can benefit from GenAI is wider than the population who benefited from the previous transitions. It is not the under-30s with technical aptitude; it is **anyone with deep domain expertise and the willingness to integrate a conversational tool into their work**. The age and technical-background spread of beneficiaries is wider than for any previous tech transition.

The corollary is important for how we should approach SME partnerships: **the target user is not the technology-forward partner at the firm; it is the experienced professional who has the most context to apply judgement well**. The senior accountant with thirty years of experience is precisely the person who gets the most leverage from GenAI, because they have the most judgement to apply to a faster information layer.

This is the opposite of how most software-for-professionals products are pitched (typically to the technology-forward partner who then has to drag the rest of the firm along). The right pitch for GenAI-augmented workflows is to the most experienced people, not the most technical.

## What This Means For Building Tools

Pulling the threads together, what does this analysis imply for how to build GenAI products for SME service industries?

1. **Lead with workflows, not features.** Show what changes in the day-to-day, not what the underlying model can do.
2. **Respect existing practice.** The workflow integrates with, rather than replaces, what already works.
3. **Be honest about reliability boundaries.** Tools that overclaim get abandoned the first time they fail; tools that scope their claims correctly get trusted.
4. **Surface the audit trail.** Professionals operate under regulatory scrutiny. The trail of what was done matters.
5. **Embrace the file-driven reality.** Files in, files out. The professional sees familiar artefacts.
6. **Price for SMEs.** This is a fragmented market of small firms; pricing models designed for enterprise sales fail here. Per-active-client or per-active-professional pricing fits the market shape.
7. **Build for the senior professional first.** They have the judgement, the relationships, and the influence within the firm.
8. **Make adoption reversible.** If someone tries the tool and decides not to keep using it, they should not have lost their data or their workflow. Trust is built on reversibility.
9. **Be patient about scaling.** This market adopts slowly and structurally. Trying to force viral adoption usually fails; building solid relationships with a small number of firms creates compounding referrals.
10. **Distribute through professional networks.** CPD events, peer associations, sector-specific conferences. These are where adoption happens, not on social media.

None of these is a technology recommendation. All of them are about how to build a product that fits the audience.

## The Bigger Picture

What I am describing is something like a counter-narrative to most of how GenAI is being sold today. The dominant pitch is "AI does the work; humans get out of the way." The counter-narrative is **"AI augments the expert; experts do better work and serve more clients."** The first pitch sells subscriptions to vendors; the second pitch transforms small businesses.

The market opportunity for the second pitch is enormous, and structurally different from the first. The buyer is the SME firm, not the individual employee. The value is delivered through workflow design and ongoing support, not through API access. The pricing model is per-firm or per-active-client, not per-seat. The competition is not other AI tools; it is the inertia of established practice plus the noise of bad marketing.

The platforms that build credibly for this audience, and the consultants who help these firms adopt, will compound. Each successful adoption creates a reference customer in a peer-networked market where references travel fast. Each well-designed workflow becomes a template that adapts to similar firms in adjacent contexts. The flywheel exists; it just rotates more slowly than the consumer-tech version everyone is used to.

The accountants, lawyers, doctors, surveyors, consultants, and other professionals are not going to be replaced. They are going to be more productive, more strategic, and more valuable to their clients than they have been in a generation. The job for those of us building the tools is to make that easier and more achievable than the current narrative allows.

If you are reading this and you are one of these professionals: the technology is genuinely usable; the workflows are starting to exist; the resistance is a feature, not a problem; and the experienced senior person in the firm is exactly who has the most to gain from getting started.

If you are reading this and you build tools for this audience: stop pitching the AI; start pitching the workflows; respect the expertise; price for the market structure; and be patient about adoption that will, in the end, be structural and durable.

The opportunity is real. The framing has been wrong. Both can be fixed.
