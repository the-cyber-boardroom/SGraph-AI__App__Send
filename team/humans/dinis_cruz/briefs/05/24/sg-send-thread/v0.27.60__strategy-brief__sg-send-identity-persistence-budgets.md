# SG/Send Identity, Persistence, And Budgets: No Anonymous Users, Ephemeral By Default, Everything Has A Price

**version** v0.27.60
**date** 24 May 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Strategy, Architect, Product, Security, @Dev
**type** Strategy / arch brief

## What This Is

A follow-up to the SG/Send open-evaluation briefing, developing the mechanisms that make the tiered open-evaluation model work in practice. Where the first briefing established the strategic transformation (free/non-private, paid/non-tracking, ephemeral vaults, security as a business enabler), this brief develops three mechanisms underneath it:

- **No anonymous users**: every user who hits the website, even a bot, gets an identity, an account, and a fingerprint, created by an SG/Sentinel rule, using fingerprinting (no cookies, but local storage is fair game).
- **Ephemeral by default**: the public cloud shares and vaults are not designed to hold data for more than a couple of days; persistence is restricted, charged for, and initially custom.
- **Everything has a budget**: every account gets a budget (say a pound) to spend on capabilities, which educates users that everything is paid and lets us control access via rules.

These build directly on the SG/Sentinel capabilities (the fingerprint as the spine, the rules engine, the ephemeral instances) and on the first SG/Send briefing, without repeating either. They also surface a genuinely new SG/Sentinel capability: an identity-creation rule.

## The Direction: Sensitive Data Needs Dedicated Protection

The framing context the voice memo set: **as SG/Send opens up, the direction of travel is that more sensitive data needs dedicated servers and additional layers of security.** Opening evaluation does not mean weakening protection for the data that matters; it means tiering it.

The voice memo: **"the direction of travel is to have data that is more sensitive, for some of the data I store, protected by dedicated servers and other layers of security. The vault server should be protected by an API key. We should have different deployment servers, and when I use vaults that have confidential data, I should be using the ephemeral stuff, EC2, where the data gets loaded and protected and exists in specific buckets, not the general one."**

So the architecture separates:

| Data Sensitivity | Protection |
|------------------|------------|
| **Confidential data** | Dedicated, API-key-protected vault servers; ephemeral EC2; specific (not general) buckets |
| **Public / evaluation data** | The general cloud shares, with the persistence restrictions below |

This sets up the rest of the brief: the public cloud shares are deliberately ephemeral and restricted (they are for evaluation, not storage), while confidential data goes to dedicated, isolated, API-key-protected, ephemeral environments. The first briefing's ephemeral-vault-and-per-customer-EC2 capability is where confidential data lives; the public shares are something different, and they need restrictions.

## Ephemeral By Default: Restricting Persistence On The Public Shares

The first mechanism: **the public cloud shares and vaults are not designed to hold data for long, and persistence is deliberately restricted.**

The voice memo: **"I want to put a series of restrictions on the main cloud-based shares and vaults that take into account that this instance is not designed to hold data for more than a couple of days. It allows us to clean it up and not have to worry too much about the data."**

The persistence policy:

| Tier | Max Persistence | Notes |
|------|-----------------|-------|
| **Public free vaults** | Two or three days max expiry | Cleaned up fast; not for storage |
| **Paid vaults (for now)** | A different workflow needed for data lasting more than a week | SLA work to define |
| **Persistent vaults** | Pay, contact the project lead, custom setup | Manual first; automate later |

The voice memo was clear on the premise: **"even the vaults we do here should not have long-term persistence, especially on a public server. I want to clean them up fast, and we don't want people to start to depend on it."** The key principle: **do not let people depend on the public shares for storage.** They are for evaluation and transfer, not persistence. Cleaning up fast keeps the public service light, cheap, and low-risk, and it sets correct expectations (this is not your storage).

For data that needs to last (more than a week), a different workflow is required, including how deletion and archiving work. The voice memo: **"we'll archive it, sort of archive the delete; we can think about how we actually do deletion. If somebody wants a persistent vault, they have to pay, contact me, and there has to be a different setup."** Persistent vaults become custom solutions initially (pay, reach out, bespoke setup), automated later. The voice memo's sequencing: **"let's first get the normal ephemeral vaults working before we implement that; they become more custom solutions; let's take it step by step."**

There is also a hint at a save-key mechanism: **"maybe you need a key to save things to a vault. You can open vaults, but you need a key to be able to use it."** The idea (still being thought through, the voice memo said "let me think about this better") is that opening a vault is one thing, but saving to it requires a key, adding a control point on persistence. This connects to the per-deployment PKI and the save-control direction (cross-ref: the SG/Sentinel deployment-modes brief).

## No Anonymous Users: An Identity For Everyone

The central and most important mechanism: **the platform should have no anonymous users. Every user who hits the website, even a bot, gets an ID, an account, and a fingerprint.**

The voice memo: **"the way we should do this is that we should not have anonymous users on this platform. Every user we have, every user that hits the website, should have an ID, an account, and a fingerprint. Even if it's a bot."**

This is a strong, clarifying principle. It does not mean every user is known by name; it means every user is *identified* (given a stable identity) so that the platform can reason about them, apply rules to them, budget them, and tell humans from bots. The voice memo connected it directly to the hostile-to-bad-traffic goal: **"this goes back to wanting to make the site ridiculously hostile to bots and non-human and non-valid users. We want to make sure that only valid users are available, and we should make this quite visible, because it's a good way to generate traffic and things interesting to talk about."**

So no-anonymous-users serves three purposes at once: it enables the hostile-to-bad-traffic capability (you cannot be hostile to what you cannot identify), it enables the budget model (below), and it is itself an interesting, talkable demonstration of the platform's capabilities.

### Identity Via Fingerprint, No Cookies, Local Storage Fair Game

The mechanism for identity respects the privacy stance: **identity is created from a fingerprint, with no cookies (no tracking), but local storage is fair game.**

The voice memo worked through this carefully: **"we create an identity based on the fingerprint we generate for the user, still relying on the fact that we are not going to store any cookies. The interesting question is local storage. I think local storage is fair game, because it's not cookies, it's not tracking. So we have no tracking, but we have local storage, which we use anyway."**

The distinction matters: cookies are associated with tracking and are avoided (consistent with the privacy stance); local storage is a client-side store the platform already uses and is not tracking in the cookie sense, so it is acceptable. The identity is a fingerprint hash computed from available signals:

| Signal | Contributes To The Fingerprint |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| **Local storage** | A stored identifier the platform places (not a cookie) |
| **Browser characteristics** | Browser fingerprint signals |
| **Environment** | Especially for anonymous users, the environment they are in |

The voice memo: **"we need to calculate a hash that lets us identify a user: a combination of what's in local storage, the browser, and the environment. We need to fingerprint the user, fingerprint the browser, respecting privacy but capturing this."**

This is the fingerprint-is-the-spine principle (cross-ref: the SG/Sentinel addendum, Observation 5) realised concretely for SG/Send: the fingerprint is the identity, computed from local storage plus browser plus environment, and it is the anchor for everything (rules, budget, human-vs-bot classification). Everyone who hits the website is effectively logged in, as a (possibly pre-generated, possibly random) user derived from these signals. The voice memo: **"everybody that hits the website will be logged in as a user, which could be a random user pre-generated from the signals we get, and we can even show that to the user."**

The privacy angle differs by whether you authenticate with a key: **"the privacy angle will be different whether you are logging in with a key or not. If you log in as a user with a key, you have different capabilities than if you do not."** So the fingerprint identity is the baseline for everyone; logging in with a key (the paid/authenticated path) layers on more capability and a different privacy posture (cross-ref: the first briefing's paid/non-tracking mode).

### The SG/Sentinel Extension: An Identity-Creation Rule

A genuinely new SG/Sentinel capability surfaced here, which the voice memo recognised as such: **an SG/Sentinel rule that creates an identity for every user who hits the website.**

The voice memo: **"one interesting concept is how we add this on the back end, and that's an interesting extension of SG/Sentinel. We could use SG/Sentinel, because we can have a rule to map a user. We want to create an identity for every user that hits the website, whether anonymous or paid."**

This is a notable addition to SG/Sentinel's rule capabilities. The rule types in the day-68 corpus were about allowing, blocking, logging, and transforming; an identity-creation rule is a new kind: a rule that, on every request, computes the fingerprint and creates or looks up the user's identity. It connects to the rule-as-transformation idea (cross-ref: the SG/Sentinel portable-spec brief: a rule can transform, not just block) and the fingerprint spine. For the main SG/Send website, this means adding a bit of logic (an SG/Sentinel rule) that runs on every request, capturing and registering the user and their activity.

The voice memo: **"for the main website, we're adding code that runs every time, storing the user and the user activity, logging and registering. We add a bit of logic to our rules that captures the user and who it is, for all anonymous users."** This is the identity-creation rule, running on every request, building the no-anonymous-users reality.

This SG/Sentinel extension should be captured in the SG/Sentinel rule work as a new rule type (identity-creation), since it is broadly useful beyond SG/Send.

### Showing Users Who We Think They Are

The educational feature from the first briefing deepens here: **classify and show each user what we think they are: human, bot, agent, or no-capability.**

The voice memo: **"this is accounts we believe are humans, versus accounts we know are bots, or accounts that have no capability. We should show the user 'this is what we think you are': we think you are a bot, or not. From what we gather from you, this is who we think you are."**

| Classification | Meaning |
|----------------|---------|
| **Human** | We believe a live human user |
| **Bot** | We believe automated, non-human |
| **Agent** | An AI agent (a distinct and growing category) |
| **No capability** | An account with no meaningful capability |

This classification serves the hostile-to-bad-traffic goal (treat bots and non-valid users differently, cross-ref: the SG/Sentinel time and rules briefs) and the educational goal (showing users our read of them is striking and memorable, cross-ref: the first briefing's education angle). The agent category is forward-looking and connects to the agent-governance work (cross-ref: the SG/Sentinel agent-governance brief): agents are a recognised, distinct class of user.

### The Anonymous-To-Live Transition

A pattern borrowed from e-commerce, which the no-anonymous-users model enables: **transform an anonymous (fingerprint) session into a live (authenticated) session on login, or discard it.**

The voice memo: **"we do the typical thing you do in e-commerce: if you log in, we transform an anonymous session into a live session, or we just delete everything, or we say this is a transition from this type of thing."** Because every user already has a fingerprint identity, logging in is a transition from the anonymous identity to the authenticated one, carrying over (or discarding) the session. This is a well-understood pattern (e-commerce carts work this way) and it fits the fingerprint-identity model cleanly.

## Everything Has A Budget

The third mechanism, and a powerful one: **because every user has an account, every account gets a budget, which educates users that everything is paid and lets us control access via rules.**

The voice memo: **"because we make every user an account, we can give every account a budget. Every account gets, say, a pound to spend on the website. That pound can be used, and it has certain properties. We can enable different capabilities you get with that account, and as you top up, you get more."**

The budget model:

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Every account gets a budget** | E.g. a pound to start |
| **Capabilities cost** | Different functions cost different amounts (infographics, vaults, ephemeral EC2) |
| **Higher-cost capabilities need a paid account** | Creating vaults, spinning up ephemeral EC2 instances cost more |
| **Top up for more** | Adding budget unlocks more usage |
| **Enforced via rules** | SG/Sentinel rules, rates, and controls enforce the budget |

The voice memo gave examples: **"creating vaults, creating ephemeral EC2 instances, that's a good example where you'd need a more paid account to spin that up. If every user that hits the website has a budget, we can enforce the budget on what they can use."**

The strategic value is the education the budget provides: **it teaches users from the first interaction that everything is paid, that everything costs something, and how it all fits together.** The voice memo: **"we are already educating the user that all of this is paid from a usability point of view, and all the functionality they access we can control using rules or rates."** This is a gentle, built-in education of the value model: rather than a hard paywall, every user gets a small budget, spends it on real capabilities, sees what things cost, and tops up when they want more. It makes the paid model intuitive and fair from the first click.

This connects directly to the cost-attribution work (cross-ref: the SG/Sentinel cost-attribution brief): because every action has an attributed cost (the accountant agent's per-action cost), the budget can be spent against real costs. The cost-attribution machinery is what makes the budget model precise: a capability costs what it actually costs to provide, and the budget is spent against that. The budget model is the user-facing expression of the cost-attribution capability.

## What This Asks For

Concrete next steps:

1. **Separate confidential data onto dedicated, API-key-protected, ephemeral, isolated-bucket servers** (vs the public general shares).
2. **Restrict persistence on the public shares** (two-to-three-day max expiry on free vaults; clean up fast; do not let people depend on it).
3. **Define the >1-week persistence workflow** (paid, custom, contact-the-project-lead first; archive-and-delete; automate later).
4. **Consider the need-a-key-to-save-to-a-vault control** (open vs save separation).
5. **Implement no-anonymous-users** (every user gets an ID, account, fingerprint).
6. **Build the fingerprint identity** (local storage + browser + environment; no cookies; privacy-respecting).
7. **Build the SG/Sentinel identity-creation rule** (runs on every request; creates/looks up the user; a new rule type for the SG/Sentinel corpus).
8. **Classify and show users** (human/bot/agent/no-capability; the educational "who we think you are").
9. **Build the anonymous-to-live session transition** (the e-commerce pattern).
10. **Build the per-account budget model** (every account gets a budget; capabilities cost; enforced via rules; spent against real per-action costs).

Estimated effort: the persistence restrictions are near-term and straightforward (expiry policy plus cleanup); the no-anonymous-users and fingerprint identity build on the SG/Sentinel fingerprint and need the new identity-creation rule; the budget model builds on the cost-attribution machinery and is the user-facing layer over it. Persistent vaults stay custom/manual first, automated later.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a redesign of the first briefing.** It develops the mechanisms underneath it.
- **Not long-term storage.** The public shares are deliberately ephemeral; persistence is paid and custom first.
- **Not tracking.** Identity uses fingerprint and local storage, not cookies; the privacy stance holds.
- **Not a hard paywall.** The budget model educates gently; everyone gets a starting budget.
- **Not the final SG/Sentinel identity-rule design.** It surfaces the new rule type; the detailed design belongs in the SG/Sentinel rule work.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Fingerprinting, even without cookies, has privacy implications.** Browser fingerprinting can be privacy-sensitive. Mitigation: full transparency (we show users what we compute and who we think they are); local storage not cookies; the privacy-by-payment path for those who want it; legal review.

**Risk 2: No-anonymous-users could be perceived as surveillance.** Identifying everyone, even bots, could feel heavy. Mitigation: the identity is a fingerprint, not personal data; it is shown to the user transparently; it serves abuse-prevention and budgeting, not tracking; the paid tier removes it.

**Risk 3: The budget model adds complexity to the user experience.** Metering everything could feel fiddly. Mitigation: the starting budget makes it gentle; the education is the point (users learn the value model); the designer (cross-ref: the SG/Sentinel UX brief) ensures it is intuitive.

**Risk 4: Aggressive cleanup could delete data users wanted.** Two-to-three-day expiry on free vaults could surprise users. Mitigation: clear communication (this is ephemeral, not storage); the paid/persistent path for data that must last; set expectations from the first interaction.
