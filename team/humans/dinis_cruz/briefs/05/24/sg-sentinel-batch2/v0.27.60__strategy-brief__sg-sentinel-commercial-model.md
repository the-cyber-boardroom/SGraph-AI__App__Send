## What This Is

The thirteenth brief of the SG/Sentinel implementation batch, mapping the commercial model for SG/Sentinel. The project lead was explicit that this is a sketch of how it could be done, for someone else to take forward, not a commitment to build a company: **"we should map the commercial model, although I don't want to do it, because somebody else should do it. We should just show how it could be done, in case somebody wants to build a company on top of this."**

This develops a question the day-68 path-to-MVP brief flagged as open (product vs internal tool). It is genuinely new: the corpus established the technology and the value (cheaper than Firehose+WAF, business-logic enforcement, visibility); this brief sketches how that value could be packaged and sold.

## The Stance: A Sketch For Someone Else

Before the model, the framing matters: this is a deliberate sketch, not a roadmap. SG/Sentinel is open source and the focus is the technology; the commercial model is documented so that someone (a startup, a partner) could build a company on top of it, the way the project lead's other startups are structured. It is shown, not committed to.

## The SaaS Model

The core commercial shape, consistent with the project lead's other startups: **a SaaS model, selling custom versions, with hosted options.**

The voice memo: **"it should be the SaaS model, like my other startups. You should sell the custom versions. You can host a number of these online, so you can protect a website, you can have this running as a service, as a reverse proxy, but again integrate with all the other key providers. You can deploy in passive mode."**

The commercial components:

| Component | What It Is |
|-----------|------------|
| **SaaS** | The core delivery model, as with the project lead's other startups |
| **Custom versions** | Sell tailored versions (the pack spectrum, cross-ref: the rule-packs brief, is the customisation axis) |
| **Hosted service** | Host SG/Sentinel online to protect a website, run as a service / reverse proxy |
| **Integration with key providers** | The interoperability (cross-ref: the interoperability brief) as a commercial feature |
| **Passive-mode entry** | Deploy in passive mode for instant visibility (the low-friction on-ramp, below) |

The pack spectrum (cross-ref: the rule-packs brief) is the natural commercial axis: super-light to super-full packs map to commercial tiers, and "what they're paying" was named as a pack-selection factor. The commercial model sells access to richer packs and hosted/managed options.

## Passive Mode As The Commercial On-Ramp

The most commercially astute point the voice memo made: **passive mode is a brilliant commercial on-ramp, because it delivers instant visibility with zero risk.**

The voice memo, speaking from direct experience: **"passive mode is really cool, because it allows you to immediately get visibility and immediately get traffic. I would have deployed this in passive mode if that was an option, when I was trying to understand what my CloudFront does. Even just the path to say 'deploy this and gain visibility into CloudFront' would be a cool solution."**

This is a strong go-to-market insight grounded in the project lead's own unmet need. Passive mode (cross-ref: the deployment-modes brief: logging only, zero side effects) is the perfect first product because:

- **Zero risk**: it only observes, so a customer can deploy it without fear of breaking anything.
- **Instant value**: it delivers visibility immediately (into CloudFront, into traffic, into what is actually happening), which currently does not exist (cross-ref: the over-index-on-visibility emphasis).
- **A natural upgrade path**: once a customer has visibility and trusts the tool, enabling rules (blocking, business logic) is the natural next step (the progressive lock-down, cross-ref: the delegation brief).

The voice memo's own experience is the proof: faced with not understanding what their CloudFront was doing, they would have paid for a deploy-this-and-get-visibility solution. **The commercial entry point is "deploy in passive mode, gain visibility into your CloudFront/traffic," and the upsell is everything else.**

## Mini Packages With Cost Comparison

A concrete packaging idea: **mini packages that combine SG/Sentinel with specific tools to deliver visibility, sold with an explicit cost comparison against the alternatives.**

The voice memo: **"we probably should look at those mini packages, where you do this plus this tool plus that tool, you get visibility, and here's the cost comparison, for example with the Firehose pipelines."**

This connects directly to the cost-attribution work (cross-ref: the cost-attribution brief: cost-as-benchmark). The mini packages are sold on a quantified cost-comparison basis: "this package gives you the visibility you currently get from Firehose pipelines, at this lower cost." The cost-as-benchmark capability is the commercial proof: SG/Sentinel can show, with real numbers, that it delivers the same or better visibility and protection more cheaply than the Firehose+WAF baseline.

The voice memo extended the comparison beyond Firehose: **"a lot of companies spend a lot of money on Google Analytics and logging and stuff like that."** The visibility SG/Sentinel provides overlaps with what companies pay for in analytics and logging, so the cost comparison extends to those spends too. The mini-package pitch is: get this visibility, at this cost, versus what you currently pay for Firehose / analytics / logging.

| Mini Package | Delivers | Compared Against |
|--------------|----------|------------------|
| Visibility package | CloudFront/traffic visibility (passive mode) | Firehose pipelines, CloudWatch costs |
| Analytics-overlap package | Traffic insight | Google Analytics, logging spend |
| Protection package | Visibility plus blocking | WAF subscriptions |

## Agentic-Native As A Commercial Differentiator

A point the voice memo stressed as very important: **the entire solution is agentic-native (agentic-friendly but not inline), which is a major commercial differentiator.**

The voice memo: **"remember that our entire solution is agentic-friendly, almost agentic-native. We are making this super easy for agents to develop and build on top, but not inline. That allows us to leverage the best of both worlds."**

The "not inline" qualifier is the key (cross-ref: the rules-engine brief: LLM never inline; the time brief: slow analysis, fast enforcement): agents help develop, build, customise, and analyse (the LLM-assisted rule development, the agentic red-team, the accountant agent, the designer), but they are not in the hot path of request processing, which stays fast and deterministic. This is the best of both worlds: the flexibility and power of agents for everything around the request path, with the speed and determinism of simple rules in the path.

Commercially, agentic-native is a differentiator because it makes SG/Sentinel uniquely easy to build on, customise, and operate with agents, at a time when agentic capabilities are a major axis of competition. A security tool that agents can develop rules for, analyse, and manage is more valuable in an agentic world than one that requires manual configuration. This connects to the acid test (cross-ref: the UX brief: teams adopting Sentinel via LLM-assisted primitives) as a commercial flywheel: agentic-native lowers the adoption barrier, which drives the acid-test adoption, which deepens the app-coupling, which improves the product.

## What This Asks For

This brief is a sketch, so it asks less for a build and more for documentation and optionality:

1. **Document the SaaS commercial model** (custom versions, hosted service, reverse-proxy hosting).
2. **Position passive mode as the commercial on-ramp** (deploy, gain instant visibility, zero risk).
3. **Define mini packages with cost comparisons** (vs Firehose, CloudWatch, analytics, logging spend).
4. **Use cost-as-benchmark as the sales proof** (real numbers, cross-ref: the cost-attribution brief).
5. **Lead with agentic-native as a differentiator** (easy for agents to build on, not inline).
6. **Keep it as a sketch for someone else** (show how it could be done; the focus stays on the technology).

Estimated effort: this is primarily a documentation and positioning exercise (the sketch), not a build; the underlying capabilities (passive mode, cost benchmarking, packs, integration, agentic-native) are built for their own sakes and the commercial model packages them.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a commitment to build a company.** It is a sketch for someone else.
- **Not a detailed business plan.** It shows the shape, not the financials.
- **Not a departure from open source.** The model sits on top of an open-source core.
- **Not the focus.** The technology is the focus; this documents the commercial optionality.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Documenting a commercial model could distract from the technology focus.** Mitigation: it is explicitly a sketch for someone else; the technology remains the priority; the model is documented once and parked.

**Risk 2: Cost-comparison claims must be honest and current.** Mitigation: ground them in the cost-as-benchmark real data (cross-ref: the cost-attribution brief); update as pricing changes; do not overclaim.

**Risk 3: A commercial framing could conflict with the open-source and collaborator stances.** Mitigation: the model sits on an open-source core and the collaborator principle (cross-ref: the interoperability brief) holds; commercial value is in hosting, custom versions, and integration, not in lock-in.
