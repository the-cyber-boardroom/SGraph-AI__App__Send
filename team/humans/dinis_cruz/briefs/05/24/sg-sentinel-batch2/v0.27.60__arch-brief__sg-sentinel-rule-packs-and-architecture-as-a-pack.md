## What This Is

The eleventh brief of the SG/Sentinel implementation batch, introducing the concept of **multiple versions and multiple rule packs, where not just the rules but the whole architecture becomes a pack.** A pack can range from a couple of rules running inside an Nginx, FastAPI, Node, or Go application, all the way up to a rich chain of SG/Sentinels protecting SG/Sentinels protecting an app. Which pack a deployment uses depends on the business case, the risk, what is being paid, the accepted scenario, and where it is being deployed.

The core idea: **the architecture itself is a pack.** This unifies several threads from the batch (the form-factor spectrum from the portable-spec brief, the SGS-in-sequence resilience from the MVA brief, location-agnostic rules) under one model: a deployment is a pack, packs range from super-light to super-full, and the choice of pack is a per-deployment decision driven by need and risk.

This is genuinely new as a framing, though it draws threads together. The portable-spec brief established the form-factor spectrum and location-agnostic rules; the rule-architecture brief (day-68) established the fractal rule graph; this brief unifies them: the pack is the unit that bundles a particular architecture and its rules, versioned under IFD (cross-ref: the IFD-versioning-correction addendum).

## The Pack Spectrum: Super-Light To Super-Full

The central concept: **packs range from super-light (a couple of rules) to super-full (a rich multi-layer deployment), and a deployment chooses where on that spectrum it sits.**

The voice memo: **"we should have these super-light packs that only have a couple of things, and you should be able to go from super-light to super-full SGS deployment. And it's not just the rules, it's the architecture."**

The spectrum, from lightest to fullest:

| Pack | Architecture | Example |
|------|--------------|---------|
| **Super-light** | A couple of rules embedded in an app | A few rules inside Nginx, FastAPI, Node, or Go (the baked-in firewall, cross-ref: the portable-spec brief) |
| **Light** | A simple embedded SGS with more rules | A richer FastAPI-embedded SGS |
| **Medium** | A FastAPI support layer upstream | An SGS in front of the app |
| **Full** | Chained SGS, multiple layers | SGS talking to SGS, connected to the outside world (cross-ref: the SGS-in-sequence model in the MVA brief) |
| **Super-full** | The complete fractal deployment | SGS protecting SGS protecting SGS protecting an app |

The voice memo: **"from the simplest couple of rules executed inside an Nginx, inside a FastAPI, inside a Node, inside whatever application, Go, whatever, to a rich little FastAPI support to an upstream set of SGS, who then talks to another SGS connected to the outside world, the multiple layers, each of those has a pack."**

## The Architecture Is The Pack

The key insight that makes this more than rule bundling: **the architecture, not just the rules, is what the pack defines.** A pack is not merely a set of rules; it is a complete specification of an architecture (which layers, which form factors, which rules at each) that can be deployed as a unit.

The voice memo: **"the whole architecture becomes almost a pack by definition."** This means choosing a pack is choosing an architecture: the super-light pack is the embedded-firewall architecture; the super-full pack is the chained-SGS architecture. The pack bundles the architectural decisions and the rules together.

This is fractal (cross-ref: the rule-architecture brief's fractal rule graph, the platform's vault-of-vaults pattern): **you can have an SGS that protects an SGS that protects an SGS that protects an app**, and each of those SGS instances has its own pack. The voice memo: **"this becomes this very fractal way of development. You can have an SGS that protects an SGS that protects an SGS that protects an app."** The packs nest: the architecture-as-a-pack composes fractally, the same way rules nest in the rule graph and vaults nest in vaults.

## Packs Differ From Development To Production

A property the voice memo reiterated (and which corrects-by-reference the fast-track inversion theme): **the pack can differ from development to production, and production may have fewer restrictions than dev.**

The voice memo: **"it becomes different from development to production. You can have more rules, more different ones. Production could have less restrictions than dev, because we probably want to make it as harsh as possible during dev so we pick up everything. On main and QA it should be exactly the same."**

This is the prod-more-relaxed-than-dev/QA inversion (cross-ref: the test-driving brief), now expressed as packs: dev runs a harsher pack (more rules, more aggressive, to catch everything during development), QA runs the same pack as main (parity), and production runs the pack appropriate to its real traffic (possibly fewer restrictions than the harsh dev pack). The pack is the unit that carries these per-environment differences, while the rules within remain location-agnostic (cross-ref: the portable-spec brief: a rule does not know where it runs; the pack decides which rules run where).

This connects to the deploy-parity principle (cross-ref: the delegation brief): main and QA run the identical pack, so QA tests exactly what production-candidate main will run. Dev can be harsher; main equals QA; production is what main releases.

## Packs Are Chosen By Business Case, Risk, And Context

The selection model: **which pack a deployment uses depends on the business case, the risk, what is being paid, the accepted scenario, and where it is deployed.**

The voice memo: **"each of these rule packs, it's almost like it depends on the business case, depends on the risk, depends on what they're paying, depends on the accepted scenario, depends on where they're being deployed."**

| Selection Factor | Influence On Pack |
|------------------|-------------------|
| **Business case** | What the deployment needs to do |
| **Risk** | Higher risk warrants a fuller, harsher pack |
| **What is being paid** | Commercial tier (cross-ref: the commercial-model brief) maps to pack richness |
| **Accepted scenario** | The residual risk the deployment accepts (cross-ref: the MVA brief's accepted baseline risk) |
| **Where deployed** | Edge, embedded, bare-EC2, behind another WAF, etc. |

This makes the pack the unit of commercial and risk differentiation: a customer paying for more, or running higher-risk workloads, gets a fuller pack; a simple low-risk deployment gets a super-light pack. The pack spectrum is also the commercial spectrum (cross-ref: the commercial-model brief), and the deployment unit (cross-ref below).

## The Deployment Contains Everything To Deploy From Zero

A practical requirement the voice memo stressed: **the deployment (the pack) must contain everything needed to deploy an entire solution, or at least the SG/Sentinel components, into a fresh, different environment, always starting from zero.**

The voice memo: **"the deployment itself needs to contain everything to deploy an entire solution, or at least the SGS components, in a fresh, different environment. You always need to start from zero and then deploy on top of it, which is why it's important that you work very effectively."**

This is the start-from-zero discipline (cross-ref: the demo-discipline and provisioning work from day 66, and the CLI create/destroy/teardown from the architecture-and-data-flows brief): a pack is self-contained and reproducible, deploying a complete SG/Sentinel solution into a fresh environment from nothing. This matters for the ephemeral-instance reality (cross-ref: the test-driving brief's bare-EC2 instances) and for reliable, repeatable deployment: a pack is a complete, versioned, self-contained deployment artefact.

## What This Asks For

Concrete next steps:

1. **Define the pack concept** (a versioned bundle of an architecture and its rules).
2. **Build the pack spectrum** (super-light embedded to super-full chained).
3. **Make the architecture part of the pack** (the pack specifies layers and form factors, not just rules).
4. **Support fractal pack nesting** (SGS protecting SGS, each with its pack).
5. **Carry per-environment differences in the pack** (dev harsher; main equals QA; prod appropriate).
6. **Version packs under IFD** (cross-ref: the IFD-versioning-correction addendum).
7. **Make packs self-contained deployment artefacts** (deploy from zero into a fresh environment).
8. **Map pack selection to business case, risk, payment, scenario, and location.**

Estimated effort: the pack concept formalises and bundles work already designed (form factors, the rule graph, the layers); the self-contained-deployment-from-zero requirement builds on the CLI and provisioning work; the commercial-tier mapping connects to the commercial-model brief.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a new rule format.** Packs bundle the existing rules and architecture.
- **Not a fixed set of packs.** The spectrum is continuous; deployments choose where they sit.
- **Not separate from the location-agnostic-rules principle.** Rules stay location-agnostic; the pack decides which run where.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: The pack spectrum could proliferate into unmanageable variety.** Mitigation: a small set of well-defined reference packs (super-light, light, medium, full, super-full); customisation within them; IFD versioning tracks variants.

**Risk 2: Architecture-as-a-pack could couple architecture and rules too tightly.** Mitigation: rules remain location-agnostic; the pack references which rules run where, it does not embed location into the rules.

**Risk 3: Self-contained-from-zero deployment is demanding.** Mitigation: it builds on the proven provisioning and CLI work; the start-from-zero discipline is already practised elsewhere in the project.
