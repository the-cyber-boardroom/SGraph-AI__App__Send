## What This Is

The seventh brief of the SG/Sentinel implementation batch, capturing a topic the project lead has written about extensively: **surrogate dependencies and traffic replay.** A surrogate dependency is a web service that replaces traffic and looks just like the real traffic, which unlocks a range of new capabilities for development, testing, and experimentation, especially in agentic workflows.

The core idea: **once SG/Sentinel is deployed as a reverse proxy, we can simulate traffic back, inject content, and create a different reality from what actually exists on the other side.** This lets us not just replay a third-party service we depend on, but simulate it entirely, record live traffic and replay it, run variations, and ask "what happens if?" questions, all without touching the real dependency.

This is genuinely new relative to the corpus. The tabletop simulations (day-68) were paper exercises; the replay-from-S3 idea (the addendum) was specifically for testing rules against captured traffic. Surrogate dependencies are a broader capability: a general mechanism for substituting a controlled reality for a real dependency, built on the reverse-proxy form factor (cross-ref: the test-driving and deployment-modes briefs). A later memo extended the idea into chaos engineering (simulating outages and incidents on the response side), which this brief now incorporates.

## What A Surrogate Dependency Is

The definition: **a surrogate dependency is a web service that replaces traffic and looks just like the real traffic.** Where a real dependency (a third-party API, another app, an external service) would respond, the surrogate responds instead, indistinguishably from the real thing, but under our control.

The voice memo: **"the surrogate dependency is fundamentally a web service that replaces traffic and looks just like the real traffic, which allows a lot of very interesting new capabilities, new developments, especially on the agentic workflows."**

The reverse-proxy form factor (cross-ref: the deployment-modes brief, where SG/Sentinel sees outbound as well as inbound traffic) is what makes this possible. When a system behind SG/Sentinel reaches out to a dependency, that outbound request goes through SG/Sentinel. Instead of forwarding it to the real dependency, SG/Sentinel can answer with a surrogate: a controlled response that looks exactly like the real dependency's response.

The voice memo: **"once we have an SG/Sentinel as a reverse proxy, we can simulate traffic back, we can inject stuff on it, we can create a different reality than what exists on the other side."**

## Creating A Different Reality

The conceptual power: **the surrogate lets us create a different reality from what actually exists on the other side of the dependency.** The system under development or test sees a reality we control, not the real one.

This unlocks several distinct capabilities:

| Capability | What It Does |
|------------|--------------|
| **Replay a real dependency** | Record the real dependency's traffic, replay it later (no live dependency needed) |
| **Simulate a dependency** | Stand in for a third-party service or another app entirely |
| **Inject content** | Insert specific responses to test how the system handles them |
| **Run variations** | Change the surrogate's responses to test different scenarios |
| **Ask "what happens if?"** | Simulate conditions that are hard to produce with the real dependency |

The voice memo: **"we can not just replay a third-party service we depend on, we can even simulate that third-party service or this other app we're working on. It is a nice way to do not just development but also experimentation, and a much better workflow in development and testing."**

This is valuable because real dependencies are awkward to develop and test against: they have rate limits, costs, side effects, and they cannot easily be made to produce rare or error conditions on demand. A surrogate has none of those constraints: it is under our control, free to call, side-effect-free, and able to produce any condition we want to test.

## Record And Replay

A specific and important use the voice memo emphasised: **using surrogate dependencies with live systems to record the traffic and then replay it.**

The voice memo: **"one of the ideas of surrogate dependencies, especially when using live systems, is to use it to record the traffic and then replay it, which makes a massive difference in a lot of these workflows."**

The record-and-replay workflow:

1. **Record**: with SG/Sentinel as a reverse proxy in front of a real dependency, capture the real traffic (requests and responses) as the system interacts with it.
2. **Replay**: later, switch the surrogate to replay mode, answering from the recorded traffic instead of calling the real dependency.

This gives a faithful, controlled reproduction of how a real dependency behaved, replayable as many times as needed, without the real dependency. It is invaluable for development (work against a realistic surrogate offline), testing (deterministic, repeatable tests against recorded reality), and debugging (replay the exact traffic that caused a problem).

This connects to the replay-from-S3 idea (cross-ref: the addendum's Observation 2) and the passive-mode capture (cross-ref: the deployment-modes brief): the traffic SG/Sentinel captures (in passive mode, or as a recording reverse proxy) becomes the corpus for replay. The captured traffic serves rule-testing (the addendum) and now also surrogate-dependency replay (this brief). **The same captured traffic is both the rule-test corpus and the surrogate-replay source.**

## The Paper-Replay Connection

The voice memo drew a direct line from this capability to the tabletop work done in this very project: **the tabletop simulations were a paper replay, used to find gaps; surrogate dependencies take that to the next level by doing it in running code.**

The voice memo: **"I already do this sometimes; in fact, we did it here, this idea of you replay it all on paper, all of the capabilities, and you use it to find gaps. Imagine we take it to the next level."**

This is a nice continuity: the day-68 tabletop simulations replayed requests on paper to surface gaps (and found 25 of them). The surrogate-dependency capability is the running-code version: replay real or simulated traffic through the actual system to find gaps in the implementation, not just the design. Where the tabletop was the design-time gap-finder, surrogate-dependency replay is the implementation-time gap-finder. It is also the natural partner to the agentic red-team (cross-ref: the test-driving brief): the red-team generates adversarial traffic; surrogate dependencies let us replay and vary it to probe the system thoroughly.

## Especially Powerful For Agentic Workflows

The voice memo singled out agentic workflows as where surrogate dependencies shine: **they allow a lot of interesting replays in agentic workflows.**

Agents are difficult to develop and test against precisely because they interact with many external dependencies (APIs, tools, other agents), and those interactions are expensive, non-deterministic, and side-effectful. Surrogate dependencies address this directly: an agent under development can run against surrogate versions of all its dependencies, replaying recorded interactions or simulated ones, deterministically and without cost or side effects.

This connects to the agent-to-agent and outbound-traffic direction (cross-ref: the deployment-modes brief): if agents talk to the world and to each other through SG/Sentinel reverse proxies, then those same reverse proxies can serve surrogates, letting an entire agent workflow be developed and tested against a controlled reality. **The reverse proxy that governs agent traffic in production is the same one that serves surrogates in development.** This is a strong, coherent story for developing and testing agentic systems.

## Simulating Outages And Incidents: Chaos Engineering On The Response Side

A later memo extended the surrogate-dependency idea into a powerful variation worth capturing here, since it is the same capability: **simulating not just bad traffic, but outages and incidents, the way Chaos Monkey and chaos engineering do, by controlling the responses a system receives.**

The voice memo: **"this is a variation of the first concept: to simulate bad traffic, but also outages and incidents. We can now really do what people used to do with Chaos Monkey, recreating scenarios that let us test and push to the limit a FastAPI application, not just in terms of requests, but also the response you get from services."**

The key extension is the response side. Surrogate dependencies already control what a system receives from its dependencies; chaos engineering uses that control to inject failure conditions:

| Failure To Simulate | What It Tests |
|---------------------|---------------|
| **A whole service goes down** | How the system handles a dependency being entirely unavailable |
| **A service stops working** | Partial failure of a dependency |
| **A service takes too long to respond** | Timeout and slow-dependency handling |
| **A service gets overwhelmed** | Behaviour under dependency overload |

The voice memo: **"we can simulate catastrophic failures: what happens if this whole service goes down, what happens if that stops working, what happens if the service takes too long to respond, what happens if the service gets overwhelmed."**

This is chaos engineering delivered through the surrogate-dependency mechanism: because SG/Sentinel as a reverse proxy controls the responses a system receives from its dependencies, it can inject any failure mode (unavailable, slow, error, overwhelmed) and observe how the system copes. Where classic Chaos Monkey kills infrastructure, this simulates dependency failure at the traffic layer, which is more controllable and more granular.

This connects directly to the Minimal Viable Application work (cross-ref: the resilience and minimal-viable-application brief): the chaos simulations are how we drill graceful degradation. We simulate a part failing, and we verify the system degrades gracefully rather than collapsing. The surrogate-dependency capability is the tool; the MVA is the resilience property it tests. **Chaos engineering via surrogates is how we practise and prove the Minimal Viable Application.**

This is a development/testing capability, valuable throughout but not on the critical path for the first protective MVP.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a production-traffic manipulation tool.** Surrogates are for development, testing, and experimentation, not for altering real production traffic.
- **Not a replacement for real integration testing.** It complements testing against real dependencies; some tests still need the real thing.
- **Not a new proxy.** It is a mode of the existing reverse-proxy form factor.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: A surrogate could diverge from the real dependency and give false confidence.** If the surrogate stops matching the real dependency's behaviour, tests pass against a fiction. Mitigation: record from the real dependency regularly; validate surrogates against the real thing periodically; flag staleness.

**Risk 2: Creating a different reality could be misused or confused with production.** Mitigation: surrogates are clearly scoped to dev/test/experimentation; never in the production path; clear labelling of surrogate vs real.

**Risk 3: Recorded traffic could contain sensitive data.** Mitigation: the zero-knowledge and IP-escrow principles (cross-ref: the tabletop resolutions) apply; recorded traffic is handled with the same privacy care as logs.
