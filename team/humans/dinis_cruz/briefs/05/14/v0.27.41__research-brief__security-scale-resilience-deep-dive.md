## Summary

Both models can be made secure, scalable and resilient in theory, but in *real-world* conditions the serverless per-tenant VM orchestration tends to have safer defaults and simpler failure modes, while Kubernetes offers more powerful knobs at the price of much higher operational risk and complexity.[1][2][3][4]

## Security

**Serverless per-tenant VM orchestration**

- Each tenant gets its own VM: OS kernel, process space and network stack are not shared across tenants, so a compromise is naturally confined to that VM unless your own control plane or storage layer is hit.
- Attack surface is narrower: the main "shared" surfaces are your serverless control plane, image/AMI pipeline, storage system and DNS automation; there is no shared K8s API server, etcd, CNI or admission webhooks to harden.
- Tenant isolation story is simple: "if you break into your VM, you get your VM" is easier to reason about than container escapes, namespace breaks, or CNI bugs in a shared cluster.[1][5][6]

**Kubernetes multi-tenant in the wild**

- Kubernetes' documented multi-tenancy model stresses that you are relying on namespaces, RBAC, network policies and Pod Security rather than hard isolation. Misconfigurations in any of those layers are common.[7][8][1]
- Real-world failure stories include cryptominers via exposed dashboards, RBAC mistakes, insecure defaults on managed services, and ingress misconfig causing full-cluster impact.[4][9]
- Large multi-tenant clusters concentrate risk: multiple tenants share nodes, control plane, CNI, CSI and often monitoring/ingress, so a single weak point or bug can expose many workloads.[3][10]
- Well-run multi-tenant K8s can be secure, but it depends on strong platform engineering, least-privilege design and continuous hardening; many teams never get there.

**Net effect:** for the median team, the per-tenant VM model usually lands in a better security posture by default, simply because it removes several shared layers that are hard to secure correctly.

## Scale

**Serverless per-tenant VM orchestration**

- Horizontal scale is dominated by control-plane throughput (how fast your serverless functions can create/destroy VMs and update DNS) and cloud API limits, not by a central cluster.
- Scaling tenants is embarrassingly parallel: each tenant's VM lifecycle is independent, so per-tenant churn does not create a "big shared cluster" scaling ceiling; you hit provider limits and your own design limits instead.
- Resource efficiency is lower for busy tenants (no bin-packing), but for many mostly-idle or spiky tenants the ability to drop to near-zero when idle is a scale advantage economically.

**Kubernetes in practice**

- Kubernetes can scale very well in capable hands, but large multi-tenant clusters show clear operational risks: they concentrate control-plane and network load, and amplify the impact of noisy neighbours.[2][3][7]
- Real-world data: a Komodor report on production clusters found teams spend the majority of their Kubernetes time on troubleshooting and change-related incidents, with substantial over-provisioning (over 80% of workloads).[11][2]
- Autoscaling is powerful but non-trivial: pod and node autoscaling depend on correct requests/limits, metrics, and network/IP planning; stories of autoscaling blocked by IP ceilings or mis-tuned limits are common.[4][9]

**Net effect:** Kubernetes can reach very high density and throughput at scale, but that scale is fragile without strong discipline; the per-tenant VM model gives up some density but avoids many scaling anti-patterns and shared-cluster ceilings.

## Resilience

**Serverless per-tenant VM orchestration**

- Failures are mostly per-tenant: bad images, bad startup scripts or resource exhaustion typically break that VM, not everyone else. The blast radius of runtime bugs is naturally constrained.
- Control-plane failures (e.g. provisioning logic, state store, DNS automation) are still systemic, but the surface is much smaller than a full K8s control plane.
- Because VMs are designed to be short-lived and recreated from images, you implicitly practice immutable infrastructure, which reduces configuration drift and "long-lived pet" issues.

**Kubernetes in practice**

- Kubernetes has excellent resilience mechanisms in design: self-healing pods, replica sets, rolling rollouts and HA clusters.[12][13]
- In the wild, a lot of outages are K8s-related: an enterprise report shows almost 80% of production incidents in studied clusters were tied to recent system changes, with median annual downtime in the hundreds of hours and very high cost per hour.[2][11]
- Failure stories highlight how a single misconfigured ingress, CPU limits, IP planning error or logging daemon can take down or severely degrade a whole cluster.[4][9]

**Net effect:** Kubernetes gives more resilience tools but also far more ways to cause cluster-wide failures; the per-tenant VM model has fewer resilience features baked in, yet fewer high-impact failure modes and a more predictable blast radius.

## Putting It Together

Given how Kubernetes is actually deployed and operated in many organisations, the comparison on the three axes looks like this:

| Dimension   | Serverless per-tenant VM orchestration | Kubernetes multi-tenant in the wild |
|------------|-----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Security   | Strong isolation by default; small shared surface | Powerful central policy, but many soft-isolation layers; frequent misconfig and shared-cluster vulnerabilities [1][10][9][4] |
| Scale      | Parallel tenant scaling, good economic scale for many idle/bursty tenants | High theoretical scale and density, but operationally hard; over-provisioning and noisy neighbours common [7][2][11][3] |
| Resilience | Simple, tenant-scoped failure modes; control plane small enough to reason about | Rich resilience mechanisms, but high rate of change-induced outages and complex cluster-wide failure modes [2][9][13][4] |

For a team that does not want to be in the business of running K8s as a product, the per-tenant VM model is arguably safer on security, easier to scale organisationally, and more predictably resilient, even if K8s can beat it on raw density and feature richness in the hands of a very mature platform team.[2][3][4][11]

The following statistics from this research are particularly useful for defensive citation in partner conversations, position pieces, and hiring discussions:

- **80% of production incidents in studied K8s clusters are tied to recent system changes** (Komodor 2025 Enterprise Kubernetes Report)

These data points materially support the strategic argument that Kubernetes is not the safe-default choice for teams without dedicated platform engineering capacity.
