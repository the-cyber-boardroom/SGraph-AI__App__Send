## What This Is

A companion analysis of the Perplexity-generated research brief (`v0.27.41__research-brief__serverless-per-tenant-vm-orchestration-vs-kubernetes.md`) that compares our DNS-event-driven, per-tenant ephemeral VM architecture against the conventional Kubernetes multi-tenant model.

The research brief is external and impartial. This brief is internal and contextual. It records what the comparison validates about our approach, where we agree, where we go further than the comparison framed, and where the comparison missed nuance that our actual implementation captures.

The framing matters: this is **not** "we read about an architecture and decided we like it." This is "we shipped the architecture over the past several weeks and an external research pass independently described it as a defensible platform strategy." The validation flows in the right direction.

## What The Research Got Right (And Validates)

Several points in the research line up cleanly with decisions we have made deliberately and recently:

### The Isolation Story

The research correctly identifies that **VM-per-tenant gives a stronger default isolation boundary** than namespace-per-tenant in Kubernetes. Our 11-12 May briefs (the medical partner cluster, the 2026 entrepreneur strategy, the production deployment for partner solutions) made this an explicit design pillar: regulated workloads get dedicated EC2 instances because the isolation is the product.

The research's caveat about "centralised security policy through VM-centric mechanisms" is exactly what our **CLI-first agent architecture** (13 May arch brief) provides at the operational layer. Each agent is bounded, each CLI has its own contract, security policy lives in the contracts rather than in a Kubernetes admission controller.

### The Operational Complexity Story

The strongest paragraph in the research is the one about "appliance-like units" replacing "API servers, schedulers, controllers, CNIs, admission policies, and cluster-wide configuration drift." This is what we have shipped this week.

The DNS-and-cert milestone (13 May) explicitly chose the appliance model: each ephemeral instance is its own appliance with its own DNS name, its own cert, its own vault app, its own lifecycle. We have **no** API server, scheduler, controller, or admission policy as part of the platform. Each instance is self-contained. The substrate is programmable DNS, serverless control logic, EC2 compute, and S3 storage. Four primitives, no shared cluster, no platform team needed to keep a cluster healthy.

### The Cost Model Story

The research nails the cost asymmetry: "**near-zero when no tenant VMs are running**" versus "ongoing cluster/control-plane and baseline worker cost." For our actual demand profile (many bursty workloads, many tenants idle most of the time, daily band experiment with one song per day, partner POCs that are active during business hours), this is exactly right.

We are not paying for a cluster sitting idle. We pay for the instances actually serving workloads. The warm pool brief from earlier today (`v0.27.41__dev-brief__ephemeral-ec2-boot-optimisation-warm-pools.md`) refines this further: a small warm pool gives us fast startup AND near-zero idle for the cases that need it.

### The "Failure Shape" Argument

This is the most underrated point in the research. The argument is that **VM-per-tenant changes the shape of failures rather than eliminating them.** A failure is local to one tenant; the blast radius is bounded.

This matters more than it sounds. In a Kubernetes cluster, a problem with the API server, etcd, CNI, or admission stack becomes systemic; every workload feels it. In our model, an instance failing affects one vault. The vault synchronizer brief (13 May) means recovery is one command: spin up a new instance, sync the vault state, repoint DNS. The blast radius is the wrong metric to optimise for in our setup; the recovery path is.

## Where Our Implementation Goes Further

The research describes "serverless per-tenant VM orchestration" as a category. Our implementation is more specific, with several refinements the research did not capture because it generalised.

### The Wildcard DNS + Lambda Spinup Pattern

The research treats DNS as a generic programmable substrate. We are more specific: a wildcard DNS record covers the entire tenant space; existing tenant DNS hits the fast path (direct A record → instance IP); new tenant DNS hits a Lambda that decides whether to spin up a new instance or place a new container on an existing instance.

This is in the **vault hosting density and scaling** brief (today). It gives us three density modes (dedicated EC2, multi-container per EC2, multi-vault per app) on the same DNS substrate. The Lambda evolves from provisioner to placement decision-maker. This is finer-grained than the research framed.

### The Vault Collection As Distribution Unit

The research talks about VMs holding tenant data. Our model is more specific: **vault collections** (today's vault-collections arch brief) are the unit of data, packaged as zips, mountable into VMs OR containers. The same vault collection can move between substrates. The data layer is portable in a way the research did not capture.

This matters because it decouples the data from the substrate. A vault can run on a dedicated EC2 today, move to a container tomorrow, render statically the day after (per the 12 May serverless static projection brief). The substrate is one decision; the data layer is another. The research conflated them; our architecture separates them.

### The Vaults-Of-Vaults Pattern

The research did not capture the **self-contained vault** model (today's vaults-of-vaults arch brief). In our model, each vault carries its own app, its own tools, its own UI. The platform is much smaller than even the research suggested because most of what a Kubernetes platform provides (the per-application configuration, the per-application UI, the per-application workflows) lives **in the vault**, not in the platform.

This is the structural reason our control plane stays small. The complexity has not moved to a different layer of the platform; it has moved into the user's vault, where the user (or the vault's author) owns it. Each tenant is responsible for their own vault's complexity, the same way a developer is responsible for their own application's complexity.

### The Agent-CLI Substrate

The research's "narrower control surface" point is even narrower in our architecture. Our control surface is a set of CLI commands (the CLI-first agent architecture from 13 May). Each operation is a JSON-in-JSON-out CLI. The "control plane" is largely orchestration scripts that string together CLI commands. There is no API server in the traditional sense.

This is a stronger statement than the research made. The research described a small control plane; we have a control plane that is just a collection of small CLIs. Different category of simple.

## Where The Research Was Conservative

A few points where the research undersold the model.

### "30-60 Second Bootstrap Is Acceptable"

The research framed this as a trade-off: we accept slower starts for lower idle cost. Today's warm-pool brief (the EC2 boot optimisation brief) shows the bootstrap can drop to **under 10 seconds** with a warm pool of 3-5 instances. For the demo and interactive cases that matter, the gap to Kubernetes-style hot response shrinks substantially.

The research's framing was correct at the category level. Our specific implementation tightens the trade-off meaningfully.

### Platform Maturity Required

The research implies that a successful Kubernetes deployment requires a "capable platform engineering function." It correctly notes that our approach reduces this requirement, but understates how much.

In our model, the platform is a handful of services that each individual engineer can fully understand: a Lambda spinup function, a wildcard DNS record, a warm pool manager, a routing table. There is nothing that requires a dedicated platform team. A small SaaS team, an SME, or a solo founder can operate this. The CV/portfolio market opportunity (12 May strategy brief) is feasible because the operational footprint is small enough that the same person who builds the product can operate it.

This is a strategic competitive advantage we did not lean on hard enough in our own briefs. The research surfaced it usefully.

### The Centre Of Gravity Question

The research framed the choice as "where you put the platform complexity." It implied the choice is small-bespoke-control-plane vs large-generic-control-plane. We have a third position: **most of the complexity lives in the vault, not in any control plane.**

This is the deeper architectural decision. We are not just choosing a smaller control plane; we are choosing to **push application-level concerns out of the platform entirely**, into the vault that carries its own app. The platform's job is substrate (encryption, storage, DNS, serving). The application's job is everything else.

This is more like serverless functions (each function has its own deployment, dependencies, runtime) than like Kubernetes (where many applications share a cluster and operators apply cluster-wide policy). The research framed our model as "smaller Kubernetes-alternative." It is more accurately "applications-carry-themselves alternative." The control plane is small because most things are not in the control plane at all.

## Where Kubernetes Genuinely Wins (And We Should Acknowledge)

The research is fair about where Kubernetes is the right choice. We should be too. Our model is **not** a universal replacement; it is the right choice for our specific demand profile.

| Scenario | Kubernetes Right Choice |
|----------|------------------------|
| High continuous load across many active tenants | Yes; bin-packing is meaningful |
| Always-hot response for thousands of tenants | Yes; cluster stays warm by default |
| Organisation has a dedicated platform team | Yes; the ecosystem becomes leverage |
| Heavy use of operators, GitOps, service meshes | Yes; the surrounding ecosystem is real value |

Our model is right for: many bursty tenants, low average duty cycle, isolation-sensitive workloads, small operational team, demand for platform-agnostic infrastructure, willingness to accept seconds-scale cold starts.

This is **most** of our actual customers (2026 entrepreneurs, partner POCs, the CV/portfolio market, the daily band experiment). Some future scenarios (a high-volume SaaS shipped on our platform with thousands of always-active sessions) might justify Kubernetes underneath, but they are not the current shape of demand.

## The Strategic Takeaway

The research's most useful contribution is its framing: this is not "Kubernetes vs a non-standard workaround." It is **two deliberate platform models optimised for different demand shapes.** For our shape (bursty, isolation-sensitive, small-team, multi-tenant-but-low-density, willing to accept seconds-scale cold starts), our model is the more disciplined architecture, not the less sophisticated one.

This matters because the platform world default-assumes Kubernetes. When we describe our architecture to investors, partners, or technical evaluators, we sometimes face the implicit question "why didn't you just use Kubernetes?" The answer is captured in this research and in our own briefs: we did not just use Kubernetes because for our workload shape, Kubernetes would be the wrong choice. We made a deliberate alternative platform choice, and the trade-offs work in our favour.

The research gives us a defensible third-party-validated framing for that conversation.

Three concrete uses for this material:

1. **In partner conversations.** When a technical evaluator asks why we are not on Kubernetes, this brief (plus the research) is the answer. Send both.
2. **In hiring conversations.** Engineers who have lived through Kubernetes-becomes-pet experience will respond to this framing. It is a recruiting asset.
3. **In our own strategic communication.** When we write public-facing content about the platform (the sgraph.ai library entries, the OWASP-style articles, the Portugal publication's tech briefs), this framing is the right one. We are not anti-Kubernetes; we are matched-to-our-actual-workload.

## What To Refine

Two follow-ups worth considering:

### Make Our Own Version Public

The research is Perplexity-generated and references citations we cannot fully audit. Useful as internal validation, less useful as public-facing material. We could write our own version (article-style, citable as an SGraph Send position paper) that captures the same arguments with our own evidence and own framings.

This would be a fifth article in our public-facing library (after the moat article, the job-search-in-2026 article, the Explorer/Villager/Town Planner article, and the OWASP pack). Working title: "Why We Chose A Per-Tenant VM Model Over Kubernetes (And When You Should Too)."

### Update The Hosting Density Brief With This Lens

Today's vault-hosting-density-and-scaling brief discussed three density modes (dedicated EC2, multi-container per EC2, multi-vault per app). The research's framing suggests adding a comparison to Kubernetes equivalents for each mode, so a reader from a Kubernetes background can map our concepts onto their existing mental model. This is a small edit, high educational value.
