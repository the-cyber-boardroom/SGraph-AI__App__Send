## What This Is

The substance layer for the evidence-driven CV workflow (cross-ref: the v0.31.5 evidence-driven-CV brief): **what a CV should actually surface, drawn from the project lead's own hiring philosophy.** The previous brief covered the mechanism (research, anchor, confirm, enforce consistency); this one defines the content the mechanism should be discovering and visualising, namely the qualities, stories, and signals that make a candidate genuinely stand out, which a conventional CV almost never captures.

This is explicitly the project lead's subjective view, formed over years of hiring (especially in cybersecurity), and it is offered as a guide to what the workflow should be able to surface, not as a universal scoring rubric. Different roles and different hirers will weight these differently (cross-ref: the per-role weighting in the evidence-CV brief). The messaging and multiple-CVs phase remains the subject of a separate, still-deferred memo; this brief is about what to capture, not how to message it.

## The Presentation: A Communication Competition, Not A Design Competition

The project lead's long-standing practice frames the whole brief: **interview candidates (senior especially, but juniors too) by having them present about themselves, because a CV is too easy to make abstract.**

The project lead: **"we would get them to do a presentation about themselves, because CVs are very easy to be abstract, and we would lose so many good candidates. We always said this is not a design competition, it's a communication competition."** The signal is not the polish; it is the choices: **"what screenshots do you put? What information do you choose to put? That I always find way more interesting than anything else."** And design is no longer an excuse: **"these days there's no excuse, because LLMs can do that for you."** So the workflow inherits this exactly: it removes the design barrier (the visualisations are generated), leaving the meaningful signal, what the person chooses to show and how they communicate it.

A note on the project lead's interview style, because it reveals what to look for: **"I would interview the person, give feedback on the CV, and then pay attention to how much they listened. I'd usually find amazing nuggets they already had, which were not on the CV, and I'd say, dude, that's your tagline, the most important thing."** Two signals here: how a person responds to feedback (do they listen, adapt?), and the buried nuggets, the genuinely distinctive things a person undersells. The workflow should be good at unearthing these buried nuggets, because they are often the most important thing and the candidate does not realise it.

## The Most Important Thing: How They Think, Behave, And Hold Values

The project lead was clear about what matters most, and it is not the technical list: **how they behave, how they think, what their values are, how they solve a problem, how they react under pressure and to change, and how holistically they understand the problem.**

The project lead: **"the most important thing I want to see is how they behave, how they think, what are their values, how they solve a problem, how they react under pressure, how they react to change, how they think about the problem, especially in cybersecurity, where it's so important to have a holistic view. Do you understand the problem? Do you understand what you're actually trying to solve? How curious are you?"**

| Signal | What It Reveals |
|--------|-----------------|
| **How they think** | Their reasoning, their approach |
| **Their values** | What they care about, how they operate |
| **Problem-solving** | How they break down and tackle a problem |
| **Reaction to pressure and change** | Resilience, adaptability |
| **Holistic understanding** | Do they grasp the whole problem, not just a task |
| **Curiosity** | How much they want to understand and learn |

A revealing probe the project lead used: **"a lot of what I'd ask is, you want to do this? Haven't you done it? Especially these days, what's preventing you from doing that?"** The signal is initiative: does the person act on their interests, or just list them? These are exactly the things a conventional CV hides and an evidence-anchored, story-driven workflow can surface.

## The Whole Person: Other Jobs, Hobbies, And Non-Linear Paths

A strong theme: **what a person did outside the obvious domain matters, and a non-linear career path is more interesting than a linear one.**

The project lead: **"I like to see what other jobs they've done, especially outside cybersecurity, or hobbies, or things they've done professionally or semi-professionally. Somebody who plays a sport at a high level tells you a lot about team building, being a team player, their values, and why they do it: just for winning, for money, or because they like the challenge, they like to learn."** The why behind an activity is itself a signal (intrinsic motivation, love of the challenge, love of learning).

And on career paths: **"I don't expect to see a linear path. I'm more interested in the things they've done that are different, that made them think, the stuff they learned."** The project lead's own cross-domain lessons illustrate why: **"I learned some of my best lessons in computing playing games, my best lessons in business from programming, my best lessons in finance from visualisation. The more I went into design, the more I understood how things work, change management, people management, all these intersections."** So the workflow should surface and celebrate cross-domain experience and the lessons drawn from it, not flatten a career into a linear title list. This is where the multiple visualisations matter (cross-ref: the visualisation-cornerstone brief): **"how do you visualise somebody's career path? There's not one, you need three, five, ten visualisations."**

## Trespasser Syndrome And Bringing People In From Other Industries

A specific, valuable insight the project lead named: **"trespasser syndrome,"** and the success of hiring people into cybersecurity from other industries.

The project lead: **"a lot of people who move outside technical roles would say programmer, not technical, and then you see what they do with a spreadsheet, how they use computing, how they think, and I'm like, dude, you're an engineer, you just don't like to call it that. I used to bring a lot of people to cybersecurity from other industries, and the hardest part was what I called trespasser syndrome: they kind of knew they were okay, they just didn't think they should be there."** This is distinct from imposter syndrome (which sits on top): trespasser syndrome is feeling you do not belong in a field you are actually good at. The project lead had **"a lot of success hiring people from other industries into cybersecurity, because they bring a whole level of skills."**

The workflow's job here is to **capture and visualise transferable skills the person undersells**, the engineering thinking in someone who calls themselves non-technical, the leadership in someone who ran a team in another field, so that genuine, transferable capability is seen rather than hidden behind the person's own under-labelling. This is both a fairness and a quality argument: it surfaces real talent that conventional CVs and self-labelling miss, and it supports diversity of thought and background.

## Role-Specific Attributes And How They Approach The Problem

Beyond the universal signals, the project lead noted **role-specific attributes (wanted in some roles, not others) and, increasingly, how a person approaches the actual problem.**

The project lead: **"there are some attributes you want in some roles and some you don't, depending on what we're trying to do. And these days, what's more interesting, I'd love to learn how they view the problem. How would you solve the problem? I don't expect solutions, but I expect them to go from what they understand: you're doing this, so this is how you'd solve it. You can see we hired for position X, so clearly there's a gap there. How do you show the gaps? How would you approach that? What would you do on day one?"**

So the workflow should support showing **how a candidate would approach the specific role**: their reading of the problem, the gaps they perceive, their day-one approach, not pretending to have the answers, but demonstrating understanding. And it should reflect that **the roles are brutal**: the project lead: **"a lot of these roles are brutal inside companies. You need to hire somebody who can handle the company, especially in cybersecurity, where you interact with all sorts of people, get stuff done, a lot of negotiation, creativity, communication."** So negotiation, creativity, communication, and resilience are role signals to surface, not just technical fit.

## Technical Depth: T, M, And Geeking Out

The technical signal still matters, and the project lead wants it deep: **how well they understand the problem, whether they are T-shaped or M-shaped, and whether they can geek out.**

The project lead: **"of course I want to see technical stuff, I want to go deep, understand how much they understand the problem. Are they a T, are they an M, do they go deep, can they geek out? We always used to ask: what's the most interesting thing you've done, the most technical, the most difficult? Because it's important to see where they stopped, how they kept going, how they resolved the problem, how they made it better, the nugget, the lessons learned, and the mistakes they made. Mistakes are as important as the successes; you learn a lot."**

| Technical Signal | What It Reveals |
|------------------|-----------------|
| **T-shaped vs M-shaped** | Breadth with one deep spike, or multiple deep areas |
| **Can they geek out** | Genuine depth and passion |
| **Most interesting/technical/difficult thing** | Where their real depth and drive are |
| **Where they stopped and how they kept going** | Persistence, problem-solving under difficulty |
| **Lessons learned and mistakes** | Reflection, growth (mistakes as valuable as successes) |

The three questions (most interesting / most technical / most difficult thing you have done) are a compact, powerful probe the workflow should build in: they surface depth, drive, persistence, and reflection in a way a skills list never does. And treating mistakes as signal (not something to hide) is part of the honesty the evidence workflow enables (cross-ref: the evidence-CV brief's consistency principle).

## The Sales Pivot: Show Them They Want Something Different

The project lead connected this to the customisation idea from the previous memo, articulating the why behind the pivot (the full multiple-CVs mechanism remains the deferred memo): **customise the message to what the other person thinks they want, and, even better, show them they actually want something different, which is exactly what you have.**

The project lead: **"when you try to be hired, you're selling yourself. You customise the message of you to what the other person thinks they want. But even better is when you can show them they actually want something different, which is exactly what you have. That's the best sales, because you're not just outsmarting your competition, you changed the rules of the game, you changed the metric. A lot of people hire for X because they feel X is the problem, but sometimes Y is the problem. So you say: I'm the perfect person to solve Y, which gives you X by proxy, and all this other stuff too."**

This is a sophisticated point and a signal the workflow should support surfacing: the candidate who understands the real problem (Y) behind the stated one (X) and can reframe the role. It is honest (not lying, reframing), and it is the highest form of fit, demonstrating exactly the holistic problem-understanding the project lead prizes most. The workflow, by building the evidence graph and the role understanding, is well-suited to help a candidate find and make this pivot.

## The Gen AI Mojo: This Workflow Is Itself The Signal

A closing, strategic point the project lead made: **knowing how to use Gen AI effectively is now itself a hiring signal, and doing this CV workflow demonstrates exactly that mojo.**

The project lead: **"if you know how to use Gen AI, and you've done this yourself, you have a mojo, and you'll be hired today, the same way people used to be hired because they could use the internet, the cloud, or mobile. Now you'll be hired in some industries because you know how to use Gen AI effectively. This role I'm describing is a great way to do that, and the vaults I'm developing, which are open source, facilitate this."**

So there is a reflexive benefit: a candidate who builds their CV through this evidence workflow is, by doing so, demonstrating the Gen-AI fluency that is itself increasingly a hiring criterion. The medium is part of the message. And the open-source vaults (cross-ref: the open-evaluation and the CC BY 4.0 stance across these briefs) are the enabler, which is both a product point and a way to make the capability broadly accessible.

## How This Guides The Workflow

Pulling it together, this brief tells the evidence-CV workflow what to surface:

| Capture And Visualise | Why It Matters |
|-----------------------|----------------|
| How they think, their values, problem-solving, reaction to pressure/change | The most important signals; usually hidden on CVs |
| Curiosity and initiative (have they acted on their interests?) | Drive and genuineness |
| Cross-domain experience, hobbies, sport, other jobs, and the why | The whole person; transferable skills; values |
| Non-linear paths and the lessons from them | More interesting than a linear title list |
| Transferable skills the person undersells (trespasser syndrome) | Surfaces real, hidden talent; supports diversity |
| Role-specific attributes and how they would approach the problem | Fit for the actual role; day-one thinking; the gaps |
| Technical depth (T/M, geeking out, the three questions, mistakes) | Real depth, persistence, reflection |
| The reframe/pivot (the real problem Y behind the stated X) | The highest form of fit; honest reframing |
| The buried nuggets (the tagline they undersell) | Often the most important thing |

The workflow's job is to discover these from evidence, help the user confirm and weight them, and visualise them compellingly, which is exactly the evidence feedback loop (cross-ref: the visualisation-cornerstone and evidence-CV briefs) applied to the things that actually make a person worth hiring.

## What This Asks For

1. **Treat these qualities as what the workflow surfaces** (not just titles and skills): thinking, values, problem-solving, curiosity, the whole person, transferable skills, role-fit, depth, the pivot, the buried nuggets.
2. **Remove the design barrier** (generate the visualisations, so the signal is the communication choices, not the design skill).
3. **Unearth buried nuggets** (the distinctive things a person undersells; often the tagline).
4. **Surface cross-domain and non-linear experience** (and the lessons and the why).
5. **Capture transferable skills the person undersells** (the trespasser-syndrome case; real hidden talent).
6. **Support role-specific framing** (how they would approach the problem, the gaps, day one; the brutal-role signals: negotiation, creativity, communication, resilience).
7. **Probe technical depth** (T/M, the three questions, lessons and mistakes).
8. **Support the honest reframe/pivot** (the real problem behind the stated one).
9. **Recognise the Gen-AI mojo** (the workflow itself demonstrates the signal; the open-source vaults enable it).
10. **Keep it subjective and weightable** (this is one hirer's view; different roles weight differently; cross-ref: per-role weighting).

Estimated effort: this is a content/criteria guide, not a separate build; it informs what the evidence-CV workflow (already specified) discovers and visualises. The work is ensuring the workflow can surface these softer, story-driven signals, not just hard facts, which is what the feedback loop and the multiple visualisations are for.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a universal scoring rubric.** It is one experienced hirer's subjective view; different roles weight differently.
- **Not just the technical list.** The most important signals are how a person thinks, their values, and the whole person.
- **Not a design competition.** A communication competition; the design is generated.
- **Not the messaging/multiple-CVs mechanism.** That remains the deferred memo; the pivot is captured here as philosophy.
- **Not a way to fabricate qualities.** It surfaces real, evidence-anchored signals (cross-ref: the consistency principle).

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Subjective criteria can encode bias.** Hiring signals, especially soft ones, can carry bias. Mitigation: present these as one hirer's view, weightable per role and hirer; anchor to evidence (cross-ref: the consistency principle); surface transferable skills to widen, not narrow, who is seen (the trespasser-syndrome and diversity point).

**Risk 2: Soft signals are hard to evidence.** Values and thinking are harder to anchor than facts. Mitigation: anchor to stories and examples (the three questions, the projects, the cross-domain lessons); the user confirms; distinguish strong from weak evidence.

**Risk 3: Over-surfacing could overwhelm.** Capturing this much could produce a sprawling output. Mitigation: the per-role weighting and the multiple visualisations (cross-ref); the packs scale from a one-pager to a deep evidence pack (cross-ref: the evidence-CV brief).

**Risk 4: The pivot could be misused to overreach.** Reframing the role could become overclaiming. Mitigation: the reframe must still be evidence-anchored and consistent; it is honest reframing, not fabrication.
