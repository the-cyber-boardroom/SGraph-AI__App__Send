## The Consulting Assessment: Three Sources Of Information

The core: **a security or compliance assessment (how good are you for GDPR?) that combines what the user provides, what the consultant provides, and what we discover by research.**

The project lead: **"I like that we combine information the user provides, information the consultant provides, and information we discovered using research (Perplexity, OpenAI, Google Search, Gemini)."**

| Source | What It Contributes |
|--------|---------------------|
| **The user** | What they know about their own posture (confirmed, not from scratch) |
| **The consultant** | The expert's knowledge and judgement (in the consultant-driven model) |
| **Research** | What we discover externally (research agents, search) |

The three sources combine into one evidence base, the same evidence-graph approach as the CV (cross-ref: the evidence-CV brief) and the GDPR assessment, here for a consulting engagement.

## Confirmation, Not Open-Ended Questions (And It Can Be Harsh)

The same inversion as the rest of the series, sharpened for consulting: **do not ask a lot of open-ended questions; ask for confirmations, and present the evidence, even harshly.**

The project lead: **"we don't ask a lot of open-ended questions, we just ask confirmations. We can say: at the moment you are not protecting this, or you don't have a good policy for X, or you're not doing this. And then we say: here's the evidence we have for this. Unless you have it and you can show it, then this is the bottom line."** So the workflow makes a claim about the company's posture (backed by discovered evidence), and the user confirms or rebuts it with their own evidence. The burden flips: rather than asking "do you have a policy for X?", it asserts "you do not appear to have a policy for X; here is the evidence; show us otherwise."

This can be deliberately harsh: **"some of this we can be quite harsh. You could even have a harder pact: if you don't do this, you should be compliant."** A consulting assessment that confronts the company with the evidence of its gaps is more useful (and more honest) than a soft questionnaire, and the evidence-anchoring (cross-ref: the evidence-CV brief's consistency principle) is what lets it be that direct: the harshness is backed by evidence, not opinion.

## Customised Graph-Based Standards

The key new architectural idea, and the one that ties back to SG/Sentinel: **create customised, graph-based versions of the standards (GDPR, ISO 27000), and map the company against them.**

The project lead: **"this is the beginning of the idea that we should have customised standards, customised graph-based standards, because fundamentally this is what we're mapping. If you go back to the data I was mapping for SG/Sentinel about compliance, it's the same thing here: this flow of how compliant am I. I like the idea of doing the work to create these graph-based versions of GDPR, ISO 27000, and there are already some out there we should be able to leverage."**

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Graph-based standards** | GDPR, ISO 27000, and others, represented as graphs (cross-ref: the GDPR brief's graph-based GDPR) |
| **Leverage existing ones** | Some graph versions exist; build on them rather than from scratch |
| **Customised** | Tailored to the company (its type, its context) |
| **Map the company against it** | The assessment is mapping the company's posture onto the standard graph |

This is the same compliance-as-a-living-graph machinery the project lead designed for SG/Sentinel (cross-ref: the day-68 SG/Sentinel compliance-as-living-graph brief): a standard expressed as a navigable graph, against which a posture is mapped and the gaps surfaced. The cross-product point is strong: the work done for SG/Sentinel's compliance graph is the same work these assessment vaults need, so the two should share the graph-based-standards foundation. Building (or leveraging existing) graph versions of GDPR and ISO 27000 serves both.

## Scoping By Company Type And Company Lookup

How the assessment scopes itself to the company: **ask what type of company you are (so we know what applies), or look the company up and process its public information.**

The project lead: **"in principle, GDPR, you can say what type of company are you, and immediately what applies to you. Or give me your company name or company number, and we go to Companies House, download that information, start processing it, do a search, and present to the user information about themselves, then allow the user to provide more details."**

| Scoping Input | What It Does |
|---------------|--------------|
| **Company type** | Determines which parts of the standard apply (the relevant subgraph) |
| **Company name/number** | Look up the company (Companies House), download and process its public information |
| **Research/search** | Discover more about the company to seed the assessment |

This is the present-information-about-themselves-then-confirm pattern (cross-ref: the evidence-CV brief): the assessment seeds itself from the company's type and public record, presents what it finds, and the user adds detail and confirms. The project lead noted the same applies to startups: **"for startups, what do you want to do, what problem are you solving, what's your TAM, your addressable market, your potential? Where's the evidence?"** The same discover-present-confirm-anchor loop serves a startup assessment (market, evidence) as serves a GDPR one.

## The Rounds / Passes Concept

The formalisation the project lead introduced: **the assessment runs in rounds (or passes), and you choose how many; each round follows the same flow.**

The project lead: **"maybe we have the concept of rounds or passes, how many rounds you want to do: two, five, twenty. For each round the process is always the same: you start with the evidence and data we have, use that to calculate the next question or set of questions, the user provides (the more visually the better), we analyse it, and we save. Then you continue."**

The per-round flow:

1. **Start with the evidence and data we have.**
2. **Calculate the next question(s)** (what we want the user to provide), from the current evidence.
3. **The user provides** (visually where possible; cross-ref: the visualisation-cornerstone brief).
4. **Analyse** the new input.
5. **Save** (the round is recorded; cross-ref: the version-controlled rounds).
6. **Continue** to the next round.

The number of rounds is the user's choice, which is the budget control (cross-ref: the visualisation-cornerstone brief's per-round budget): **"it becomes a question of how much money you want to spend, how many rounds you want to do."** Two rounds for a quick pass, twenty for a deep assessment.

And crucially it is **non-linear**: the project lead: **"you can always go back, consolidate, this should be a graph so you can go at different tangents. It's not a linear process. You can answer again, provide another answer, see what happens when you give a different answer, because sometimes people need to experiment."** So the rounds are not a fixed sequence: you can go back, consolidate, branch into tangents, re-answer, and experiment, all on the graph, with every round version-controlled and replayable (cross-ref: the visualisation-cornerstone brief's replay-go-back-experiment). This is the same feedback loop the whole series is built on, here formalised as the consulting assessment's engine.

## The Three Consulting Delivery Models

The project lead set out three ways a consulting assessment can be delivered:

| Model | How It Works |
|-------|--------------|
| **1. Self-service** | The person does it themselves, collects the data, and may send it for review (or keep it entirely private, or use an ephemeral instance) |
| **2. Send to a consultant** | The person collects the data, then sends it to a consultant or company to process and review |
| **3. Consultant-driven** | The expert drives the assessment (like a doctor collecting information from a patient) |

The project lead: **"there's the angle where the person does this by itself, collects the data, maybe sends it to somebody for review, and remember with the vaults you can do it privately and never share it, or use an ephemeral instance. There's the one where the data is sent to a consultant or company to process. And there's the one that is consulting-driven, where the expert drives this, which is where it connects to what a doctor would do with the information they collect."**

The privacy options matter for the self-service model (cross-ref: the identity-persistence-budgets brief's ephemeral instances and the zero-knowledge stance): a company can run a sensitive self-assessment entirely privately, never sharing it, or on an ephemeral instance, which is valuable for something as confidential as a compliance gap analysis. The consultant-driven model connects to the doctor analogy (cross-ref: the private knee-score MVP): an expert driving a structured, evidence-based information-gathering, augmented by the workflow.

## The Thesis: Augment The Human, Do Not Replace Them

The note the series closes on: **this does not replace human experts; it makes them far more effective, more interesting, and more relevant.**

The project lead: **"what I'm describing is not going to replace the humans, it's going to make the humans work way more effective, more interesting, and more relevant, because a lot of these analyses become cost-effective and scalable. The more you do this, the more we want this."** The consultant (or doctor, or assessor) is augmented, not automated away: the workflow does the research, the discovery, the evidence-anchoring, the visualisation, and the rounds, freeing the expert to apply judgement where it matters and to handle far more assessments than they could manually. The analyses become cost-effective and scalable, which (the project lead's nice closing observation) increases demand rather than reducing the human role: the more cost-effective and scalable the analysis, the more of it people want, and the more relevant the expert becomes.

This is the same human-augmentation thesis as the security-report and VC demos (cross-ref): the vault and the workflow do the heavy lifting; the human delivers the judgement and the relationship, now at far greater scale.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a replacement for human experts.** It augments them; the expert delivers judgement and relationship at scale.
- **Not a soft questionnaire.** It asserts the posture with evidence and asks for confirmation, directly.
- **Not a linear process.** Rounds are non-linear: go back, consolidate, branch, re-answer, experiment.
- **Not standard-from-scratch.** Leverage existing graph versions of GDPR/ISO 27000 and the SG/Sentinel compliance graph.
- **Not necessarily shared.** The self-service model can be entirely private or ephemeral.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Compliance assessments carry liability.** A wrong or harsh assessment could mislead or expose. Mitigation: evidence-anchor every claim (the user confirms or rebuts with their own evidence); the human expert in the consultant-driven model; be clear it is an assessment aid, not legal advice (cross-ref: the GDPR brief's caveat).

**Risk 2: Graph-based standards must be accurate.** A wrong standard graph produces wrong assessments. Mitigation: leverage existing, vetted graph versions; share and reuse the SG/Sentinel compliance-graph work; review the graphs carefully.

**Risk 3: Harshness could alienate or be wrong.** A harsh assessment that is mistaken is damaging. Mitigation: the harshness is evidence-backed; the user can rebut with evidence; the rounds allow correction; tune the tone per delivery model.

**Risk 4: Cost across many rounds.** Deep assessments (twenty rounds) cost. Mitigation: the rounds are the budget control (the user chooses how many); the cost-per-assessment measurement (cross-ref: the GDPR brief); cost-effective and scalable is the point.

**Risk 5: Sensitive company data.** A compliance assessment handles sensitive posture data. Mitigation: the private/ephemeral self-service options; zero-knowledge vaults; the data stays in the company's vault.
