## What This Is

The brief on what the project lead calls the cornerstone of the evidence-driven discovery work: **how to visualise what a user has provided, as a continuous feedback loop that connects the dots.** It builds toward the GDPR assessment and other real assessments (cross-ref: the v0.31.5 GDPR assessment vault brief), but it explores the underlying mechanism first, through two games that make the mechanism tangible and tunable: **Akinator and Whodunit.** The project lead is explicit about the stakes: **this visualisation is the cornerstone; until we get this right, the rest is not going to work.**

The next memo will describe how this works in a real business scenario; this brief establishes the feedback loop, the three visualisation primitives, the version-controlled rounds, and the budget discipline. It is the foundation the assessments sit on.

## The Power Of Connecting The Dots

The animating idea, which the project lead has long used the graph-and-whodunit analogy for: **the power of a graph is the ability to connect the dots, and once the dots are connected, the answer is obvious.**

The project lead: **"the power of a graph is that ability to connect the dots, and it also means the whole thing of when you have connected the dots, the answer is obvious, which is the same thing with design and a bunch of other things."** A whodunit is the perfect illustration: scattered clues mean nothing individually, but connected in a graph the culprit becomes obvious. The goal of the discovery work is to do this for a user: take what they provide, connect it into a graph, and make the answer obvious, visually.

## The Primitives We Now Have

The reason this is possible now is a combination of capabilities that did not exist before:

| Primitive | What It Does |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Extraction** | Take text and extract facts, hypotheses, opinions, and questions (cross-ref: the prompt-context and GDPR briefs) |
| **Diagram visualisation** | Visualise as Mermaid and other diagrams (cross-ref: the Wardley/Mermaid work) |
| **Infographics** | Generate infographics (cross-ref: the infographic tool) |
| **Latest image generation** | The newest image-generation models, which follow instructions well and can connect the dots into a visual |

The project lead named the latest image-generation models as a key enabler: **"the latest image generation tools, namely ChatGPT Image Two and Banana Banana ones, or the latest Google Image, where they are now very good at following instructions and connecting the dots and creating a visual representation."** (The exact models and their costs are something to evaluate; the point is that the newest image models are good enough at instruction-following to render a discovered structure visually.) The discovery the project lead wants is: **how to find the prompts and workflows that map this out**, combining extraction with these visualisation abilities.

## The Proving Ground: Akinator And Whodunit

The first two things to build, because they make the mechanism tangible and tunable: **Akinator and Whodunit.**

### Akinator (And Reverse Akinator)

Akinator is the game where, through a series of questions, the system guesses the character you are thinking of. It maps perfectly onto the discovery loop: each question adds evidence, narrows the graph, and converges on the answer. The project lead noted two directions:

| Direction | How |
|-----------|-----|
| **Classic** | The LLM picks a character; the system (or user) tries to guess it through questions |
| **Reverse** | The user picks a character; the LLM tries to guess it through questions |

Both exercise the same loop (ask, gather evidence, refine the graph, converge), which is why they are a good proving ground.

### Whodunit

The whodunit is the second: a scene or a crime, where the user asks questions ("what about this, what about that") and the evidence accumulates into a graph until the answer emerges. It adds something Akinator does not: **contradictions and messy truth.**

## Evidence, Contradictions, And Messy Truth

A subtle and important point: **the truth is sometimes messy, and things that look contradictory can both be true.**

The project lead: **"the truth sometimes is messy, or sometimes you have things that, like a riddle, look contradictory but they actually are true. Maybe somebody is alive or dead, and it depends on the definition."** So the visualisation must handle not just clean evidence but **contradictions**: evidence that appears to conflict, where the resolution is often a definition or a nuance rather than a falsehood. The graph should hold the evidence *and* the contradictions, surfacing them rather than hiding them, because in real assessments (GDPR, security, interviews) the apparent contradictions are often where the truth actually lives. This is what makes the discovery honest rather than glib.

## The Three Visualisation Primitives

The core of the brief: **three primitives to visualise, curate, and iterate, which together give a three-dimensional view of the discovery.**

| Primitive | What It Is | How It Is Made |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| **1. The semantic knowledge graph** | The facts, the evidence, what connects to what, relationships, ontologies | Elements extracted by an LLM |
| **2. The visualisation of visualisations** | Multiple graphs, multiple zoom levels, multiple altitudes, a mix of a Miro board and a whodunit board | Rendered by pure graph-based engines (LLM creates elements, engines render) |
| **3. Pure infographics** | A visual image representation to play with | The latest image-generation models, best engine at the best price |

The project lead: **"the three primitives are the graphs (the semantic knowledge graph containing the facts, the evidence, relationships, ontologies); the visualisation of visualisations, because this could be multiple graphs, multiple levels of zoom, a mix of a Miro board and a whodunit board, multiple levels of altitude; and the third is pure infographics, a visualisation image we can play with, to see what is the best engine at the best price."**

A key design separation: **the LLM creates the elements, but the visualisation is by pure graph-based engines.** The project lead: **"even the elements of this are created by an LLM, but the visualisation is then by pure graph-based engines, and you can iterate a couple of times to get it right."** This matters for cost and reliability: the LLM does the thinking (extracting facts, proposing connections), but the rendering is deterministic graph engines (not the LLM drawing), which is cheaper, faster, and consistent. Only the third primitive (pure infographics) uses the image models, and there the goal is to find the best engine at the best price.

The "visualisation of visualisations" is the richest idea: not one diagram but many, at different zoom levels and altitudes, navigable like a Miro board or a detective's evidence board. This is how a large, messy graph stays comprehensible: you zoom out for the shape, zoom in for the detail, and move around the board.

## The Feedback Loop: Every Round Improves The Graph

The key principle the project lead is introducing: **a feedback loop based on the evidence provided, where every round improves the graph and the visualisations.**

The project lead: **"the key principle is this idea of the feedback loop based on the evidence provided. Every time you ask a question, you improve your graph, improve your visualisations."** The loop, illustrated with Akinator:

1. Start blank (everything unknown).
2. Ask a question ("is it a real or fictional character?").
3. The answer adds evidence; link it to specific elements.
4. Ask the next question ("which country, what age, what job?"), driven by what is now known.
5. Each round improves the graph and the visualisations, converging on the answer.

So the discovery is iterative and visual: the graph grows and sharpens round by round, and the user watches it converge. This is the same loop the GDPR assessment runs (cross-ref: the GDPR brief's discovery workflow), here made visual and general.

## Version-Controlled Rounds: A Three-Dimensional, Replayable View

A capability the vault makes possible and the project lead wants: **every round is version-controlled and replayable, giving a three-dimensional view across time.**

The project lead: **"every round is version controlled, and every round I can replay. I can go back to the round, see what it looked like, see what we knew, what we thought we knew. You have a much more three-dimensional view of these maps."** Because the discovery runs on the vault (cross-ref: the vault version-control and commit-queue work), every round is a version: you can replay the discovery, go back to any round, and see not just what was known but **what we thought we knew at that point** (including hypotheses later revised). This adds a time dimension to the graph: not just the current state, but the whole trajectory of the discovery, navigable and replayable.

This enables animation: **"from an animation point of view, you can see the graph evolving, see the discovery evolving. From a learning point of view it should be quite visual."** The evolving graph can be animated (the dots connecting over the rounds), which is compelling for learning, for demos, and for understanding how the conclusion was reached.

## Everything Stored In The Vault (Including The Models' Reasoning)

Because it runs on the vault, **everything can be stored: the evidence, the rounds, and even the models' internal reasoning.** The project lead: **"we're building all of this on top of our vaults, which means we can store a lot of data, a lot of evidence, the whole thing, so we can replay it back. We could even store the internal reasoning of the models, and have multiple models trying to guess."** So the vault holds the complete discovery: the evidence graph, every round, the visualisations, and optionally the models' reasoning traces. This supports replay, audit, and **multiple models guessing in parallel** (different models running the same discovery, compared), which is both a quality technique (cross-ref: the prompt-context brief's multi-LLM consensus) and a way to study the models' capabilities.

## Budget Per Round: Controlling The Closed Loop

A discipline the project lead stressed hard, because the danger is real: **a closed-loop LLM discovery can burn enormous amounts of money, so every round should have a budget the user controls.**

The project lead: **"this is how people spend thousands or hundreds of thousands in tokens, especially in a closed loop, because it's very easy for an LLM to explore and go on all sorts of tangents. You want to control this, visualise it, see it happening in real time. On every round you get to choose how much money you spend on discovering, on data analysis, on visualisation."** The closed-loop risk is acute: an LLM left to explore in a loop can spend without bound (chasing tangents). The mitigation is per-round budget control:

| Per Round, The User Chooses The Spend On | Why |
|-------------------------------------------|-----|
| **Discovery** | The LLM reasoning to find the next move/question |
| **Data analysis** | Processing the evidence |
| **Visualisation** | Rendering graphs and infographics (especially the image models, which cost) |

This makes the discovery a controlled game rather than a runaway loop: the user decides how much to spend each round to connect more dots, sees the cost in real time, and stops when satisfied. It ties to the budget discipline (cross-ref: the prompt-context brief's budget awareness and the GDPR brief's cost-per-assessment) and is essential for any real, repeated use.

### Compression And Strategic Selection

Related, and reinforcing the budget point: **be strategic and selective about what is sent to the LLM for the next move.** The project lead: **"when you send to an LLM to get the next move or the next questions to ask, we can be much more selective, a lot more strategic on the questions to ask and the information we send."** Rather than sending the whole graph every round, send only what is needed to decide the next question. This is the compression/optimization discipline (cross-ref: the prompt-context brief's token management and self-pruning): a strategic, compressed context per round keeps both the cost and the focus right. The graph in the vault is the full record; the prompt is a curated slice.

## Replay, Go Back, Experiment

A property worth its own mention: **the workflow can replay, go back, and try again.** The project lead: **"in our workflow we can replay, go back, try again, experiment."** Because every round is version-controlled in the vault, the discovery is not a one-shot: you can branch, try a different line of questioning, compare outcomes, and learn what works. This is how the prompts and workflows get discovered (the project lead's stated goal): by experimenting on the games (Akinator, Whodunit) with replay and comparison, the team learns the prompts and loops that connect the dots well and cheaply, before applying them to real assessments.

## Where This Is Going

The project lead named the real targets this is for: **GDPR, consulting security reviews, interviews, assessments, data collection, and news and facts.** The games are the proving ground; the real uses are these evidence-driven discoveries:

| Real Use | The Discovery |
|----------|---------------|
| **GDPR** | The company's data-handling posture (cross-ref: the GDPR assessment brief) |
| **Consulting / security reviews** | The findings, the evidence, the exploit paths (cross-ref: the security-report demo) |
| **Interviews** | Extracting and connecting what an interviewee provides |
| **Assessments / data collection** | Any structured discovery from a user |
| **News and facts** | Large evidence graphs of news stories and facts |

The cornerstone is the same for all of them: the evidence-driven feedback loop, the three visualisation primitives, the version-controlled rounds, and the budget control. Get the visualisation right on the games, and the real assessments follow. The next memo will describe the real business scenario in detail.

## What This Asks For

1. **Build the evidence-driven feedback loop** (ask, extract, connect, refine; every round improves the graph).
2. **Build Akinator (and reverse) and Whodunit** as the proving ground (to discover the prompts and workflows).
3. **Build the three visualisation primitives**: the semantic knowledge graph; the visualisation-of-visualisations (multi-graph, multi-zoom, Miro/whodunit-board); pure infographics.
4. **Separate LLM-creates-elements from engine-renders** (the LLM thinks; graph engines render; only infographics use image models).
5. **Handle evidence and contradictions** (surface messy truth, not just clean facts).
6. **Version-control every round** (replay, go back, see what we knew vs thought we knew; animate the evolution).
7. **Store everything in the vault** (evidence, rounds, visualisations, optionally the models' reasoning; multiple models guessing).
8. **Budget per round** (the user chooses the spend on discovery, analysis, visualisation; control the closed loop; real-time visibility).
9. **Compress strategically** (send only what is needed for the next move).
10. **Evaluate image engines by capability and price** (for the infographic primitive).

Estimated effort: substantial but built on existing primitives (extraction, Mermaid, infographics, the vault version control, the LLM chat, multi-model). The new work is the feedback-loop logic, the visualisation-of-visualisations (the multi-altitude board), the per-round budget control, and the prompt/workflow discovery (which the games are for). The project lead frames this as the cornerstone, so it precedes the real assessments.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a one-shot answer.** It is an iterative, replayable feedback loop.
- **Not the LLM drawing.** The LLM creates elements; graph engines render; only infographics use image models.
- **Not an unbounded loop.** Per-round budget control; the closed-loop danger is explicit.
- **Not clean-evidence-only.** It surfaces contradictions and messy truth.
- **Not the business scenario.** That is the next memo; this is the cornerstone mechanism.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Closed-loop LLM cost can explode.** The project lead's central warning (thousands to hundreds of thousands in tokens). Mitigation: per-round budget control; strategic compression; real-time cost visibility; the user chooses the spend each round.

**Risk 2: The visualisation is hard and is the cornerstone.** If the visualisation does not work, nothing does. Mitigation: the games as a proving ground; LLM-creates/engine-renders separation; iterate; get it right before the real assessments.

**Risk 3: Image-model cost and quality vary.** The infographic primitive depends on image models that cost and vary. Mitigation: evaluate engines by capability and price; the budget covers it; infographics are one primitive, not the whole.

**Risk 4: Messy truth could mislead.** Contradictions handled badly could confuse or mislead. Mitigation: surface contradictions explicitly (do not hide or force-resolve them); the human confirms (cross-ref: the GDPR brief's confirm-not-answer); version control shows the reasoning.
