# The Evidence-Driven CV: A Personal Website Built From Discovered, Confirmed, Anchored Evidence

**version** v0.31.5
**date** 29 May 2026
**from** Human (project lead)
**to** Product, Strategy, Architect, Developer (lead), @Dev
**type** Strategy brief

---

## What This Is

The first practical business application of the evidence feedback loop (cross-ref: the v0.31.5 visualisation-cornerstone brief and the GDPR assessment brief): **helping a person build a personalised CV and personal website from discovered, confirmed, and anchored evidence.** Rather than asking the person to write their CV, the workflow researches them, shows them the evidence it found, asks them to confirm and weight it, and anchors every claim to its source. The output is a personalised, evidence-backed case for why a person fits a role (or, honestly, why they do not).

The project lead sees a strong intersection of commercial opportunity, ownership, and privacy here, and is the first user (updating the about-me pages on sgraph.ai). This brief covers the evidence and feedback-loop phases and the mechanisms that support them (skills, vault apps, feedback harvesting). The **messaging phase**, sending tailored CVs to the different people who review a candidate, is explicitly deferred to the next memo (the project lead said so directly); it is flagged here but not developed.

## The Insight: The Challenge Is Not Creating Content, It Is Curating It

The framing that motivates the whole application: **now that creating content is easy, the challenge is how to parse it, curate it, and anchor it.**

The project lead's own experience makes the point: **"I started to work on the page about me for sgraph.ai, but I did the typical thing: I went to Perplexity, ChatGPT, Claude and said do research about me, and suddenly it was too much. Not only did I have to process it, I had to put it together."** Asking a research agent about yourself produces a flood; the work is no longer generating the content, it is parsing, curating, and anchoring it into something true and usable. That is exactly what the evidence feedback loop does, which is why the CV is a natural first application.

| The Old Problem | The New Problem |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Creating content was hard | Creating content is easy (research agents) |
| (so CVs were thin and manual) | The flood must be parsed, curated, anchored |

## Start From Research, Not From The CV

The inversion, the same one as the assessment workflow (cross-ref: the GDPR brief's confirm-not-answer): **do not start by asking the person what they have done; discover it, then ask them to confirm.**

The project lead: **"instead of asking the person first what you've done, your CV and all that, let's say you don't start there. The interesting question is what can we figure out about the person. Leverage that we have access to deep research and research agents."** So the workflow:

1. **Research the person** (deep-research agents go out and find information; a folder of research, or the models do it).
2. **Build the graph and the visualisations** (the discovered facts, connected; cross-ref: the visualisation-cornerstone brief).
3. **Show the evidence to the user** (here is what we found, and where the evidence is).
4. **The user confirms, corrects, and weights** (is this correct? is it relevant? how important is it?).

This is a case of how much you want to spend, the project lead noted: **"this is simply a case of how much money you want to spend on a couple of models going out to map the data."** The research phase is budgeted (cross-ref: the visualisation-cornerstone brief's per-round budget), like the rest of the loop.

## The Feedback Loop: Confirm, Correct, Weight

The loop, applied to the CV: **show the user the evidence collected, then ask them about it.** The project lead: **"we show the user the evidence we collected, and we ask: is this correct, do you think this is relevant."** The user does three things:

| User Action | Example |
|-------------|---------|
| **Confirm or correct** | "I have two half degrees, not two degrees" (correct from evidence) |
| **Weight** | "This is more important, this is less important; I care about this, not that" |
| **Relate** | "That is a soft link" (the strength of a connection) |

And crucially, the weighting can be **per role**: **"later we can adjust and say, well, for this job I think this has more weight."** The same evidence base produces different emphases for different roles, by re-weighting, not by fabricating.

The visual element is whatever suits the user, the project lead stressed his own preference but allowed for others: **"different people prefer different things. I'm a visual person, I prefer graphs, connections, a visual element, and then say: is this true? The visual element could be a spreadsheet, could be a graph. You could even vibe-code on the spot what to show the user."** So the confirmation surface is flexible (graph, spreadsheet, an on-the-spot vibe-coded view), whatever best lets the user validate the evidence.

## Anchoring Every Claim To Evidence

The mechanism that makes this honest and powerful: **every claim is anchored to evidence, stored in the vault.**

The project lead: **"every claim on a CV, every phrase, almost every word combination, should be linked to evidence."** The workflow does not just assert; it shows where each claim comes from. And because it runs on the vault, the evidence is captured and stored, not merely referenced:

The project lead: **"we can have a couple of LLM loops: do a first Perplexity run, find some data, then follow that data, go to the website, grab the HTML, grab a screenshot, because of the vault we can store it. We could say: on this day he said this. Maybe in the future he doesn't, but at least on this day this person was featured here, or was part of this company. Here's the evidence the person was on somebody else's website, instead of relying on the Wayback Machine."** So the vault stores the actual evidence (the HTML, the screenshot, dated), becoming its own evidence archive, not dependent on the Wayback Machine or a live link that may change. And the workflow can grade evidence: **"we could show evidence we think is stronger, evidence we think is weaker, and present that to the user."** Strong and weak evidence are distinguished, so the user (and later the reader) knows how well-supported each claim is.

## The Most Important Point: Not A Way To Cheat, A Way To Be Seen

The principle the project lead called one of the most important: **this is not a way to cheat (as AI-generated CVs are), it is a way for genuinely talented people to be seen, because evidence-anchoring makes exaggeration fall apart.**

The project lead: **"this is not a way for people to cheat, like they can with CVs and AI-generated stuff. This is a way for people who are really talented, who deserve a particular job, to make sure they are seen, so the people making decisions about them decide on the right things. Once you create things like this, it becomes very hard to BS."**

The mechanism is **consistency**: because every claim is anchored to evidence, claims that do not fit the person's level do not hold up. The project lead's example:

| Consistent | Inconsistent |
|------------|--------------|
| A junior who says "Kubernetes experience" and the evidence shows them in a project deploying a simple EKS case | A junior who claims to deploy a 100-node Kubernetes cluster with no supporting evidence |

The project lead: **"a junior who says I have Kubernetes experience, and you see them deploying a simple EKS case, that's cool, it's consistent. What you don't want is exaggerations that are not consistent. You don't expect a junior to deploy a 100-node cluster; if they do, they need a whole raft of other capabilities to support it. We're creating an environment where the people who exaggerate, the maps don't hold up."** The evidence graph makes inconsistency visible: an inflated claim has no supporting evidence and contradicts the rest of the picture, so it stands out. This is the power: **if AIs or humans are making fast decisions about people (a CV gets two minutes), evidence-anchoring maximises the opportunity for the genuine candidate** and exposes the exaggerator. It is honesty-enforcing by construction.

It also produces honest verdicts: the output is **"why somebody should hire this person for this role, why this role is good for this person, and why someone should hire them, or honestly, this is not a good match, it's going to be a car crash, don't go there."** A non-match is as valid an answer as a match, which is part of what makes it trustworthy.

## Skills: Leveraging The Ecosystem

A mechanism the project lead wants to leverage: **the hundreds and thousands of skills that now exist.** The project lead: **"a skill is just something you add to an agent, or load dynamically. So it's a question of figuring out what skills to load, what apps, what analysis, and that can all be in a vault."** Skills (loadable agent capabilities) can do parts of the CV workflow (researching, extracting, analysing, visualising), and the question is which skills to load for a given step. These can live in a vault, developed there as a building block, then prioritised or commoditised: **"a lot of these things you develop in a vault as a building block, and eventually we prioritise or commoditise it, either as part of the vault or as a vault app that can be injected."** So the workflow is assembled from skills and apps, many of them reusable across assessments, not just CVs.

## The CV Workflow As An Importable, Versioned Vault App

A powerful structural idea, enabled by vault-of-vaults (cross-ref: the v0.27.64 VIV kernel-model brief and the v0.27.62 sub-vaults brief): **the whole CV workflow can be a vault app, imported like a Git submodule, version-controlled, customisable, and publishable.**

The project lead: **"when you think of vault-of-vaults, a lot of these workflows become more interesting, because like submodules in Git you can import or link a vault app, which could be the infographic generation, the semantic graph generation, or this whole CV workflow. That app can be version-controlled, you can customise it, but you want to grab the latest one. Somebody who wants to try it should grab the latest version of the app. And people should be able to publish their own."**

| Property | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **The CV workflow is a vault app** | The whole workflow packaged as an app |
| **Imported like a Git submodule** | Linked into the person's vault (cross-ref: the sub-vaults convention) |
| **Version-controlled** | Grab the latest version; customise your own |
| **Publishable** | People publish their own versions |

So a person's vault can hold **mini vault apps inside it** (the project lead: **"the vault of the person can have mini vault apps inside of it, which is really cool"**), the CV workflow, the graph generator, the infographic generator, each a versioned, importable app. Initially these are the ones the team develops; over time, people publish their own. This is the apps-in-the-vault model (cross-ref: the Wardley apps-in-the-vault map and the conventions brief) applied to workflows: the workflow is an app you import, update, and customise.

## Harvesting Feedback: Sharing Sub-Vaults For Opinions And Knowledge

A capability the project lead is excited about, built on sub-vaults and tokens: **share a sub-vault with a reviewer, an employer, or a friend to gather opinions, quotes, and references, while controlling exactly what they see.**

The project lead: **"when I want to ask for an opinion, is this good, what do you think, or even share with an employer, the employer can only see the sub-vault, not the parent vault. So we can harvest someone else's knowledge (what my friend calls the second brain). You can ask for opinions, quotes, references, and save them, because when you create the vault you create a read-only or read-write token to share with the person reviewing it."**

| Mechanism | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Share a sub-vault, not the parent** | The reviewer/employer sees only the child vault, never the parent (cross-ref: the VIV directional-trust model) |
| **A read-only token** | For reviewers/employers who should not change it |
| **A read-write token** | For those invited to contribute (opinions, quotes, references) |
| **Version-controlled** | Every contribution tracked (cross-ref: the commit-queue brief) |
| **Control by token and by content** | You control access both by the token you give and by what data you put in the child vault |

The project lead: **"you control the access by the token you give, but also by the data you share within these child vaults. It's a great way to get feedback, harvest knowledge, and share."** So feedback harvesting is precise: you decide who can see and edit, and you decide what is even in the shared sub-vault. This is the sub-vault sharing model (cross-ref: the sub-vaults and VIV briefs) turned into a knowledge-and-feedback-gathering tool: references, endorsements, and opinions collected into the candidate's own controlled, version-controlled vault. The "second brain" idea (harvesting others' knowledge) becomes concrete and controlled.

## The Output: Materials For Highly Personalised Packs

What the evidence and feedback phases produce: **the materials to create highly personalised CV packs, ranging from a one-pager to a 50-megabyte evidence pack.**

The project lead: **"we are creating the materials to create highly personalised vault CV packs that could be a one-pager, one infographic, or a 50-page, 50-megabyte evidence pack, for a particular person, for a particular company. It all depends what it is."** The same evidence base (the master set of confirmed, anchored evidence) feeds outputs of any size and form: a single infographic, a one-page summary, a full website, or a deep evidence pack. The form is chosen per audience and purpose. This is the create-once-render-many pattern (cross-ref: the security-report and VC demo briefs' audience views): a master vault of evidence, many tailored outputs.

The project lead noted the deeper point: **"a lot of people provide a uni-dimensional view of themselves, and they're not really answering the brief or providing evidence on why they're the right person, simply because it didn't scale. It was hard. But now it's not."** The reason CVs are thin and generic is that personalisation did not scale; this workflow makes it scale, so a candidate can finally present a multi-dimensional, evidence-backed, brief-answering case.

## Deferred To The Next Memo: The Messaging And Multiple CVs

The project lead explicitly deferred the messaging phase to a separate memo, and it is recorded here only as a pointer, not developed: **the idea that you should send not one CV but several, each tailored (and consistent) for a different person who reviews the candidate (the hiring manager, their boss, the peers, the reports, the CFO).** The project lead: **"I'm going to create a separate memo on this, because this becomes a variation of the different slides and graphs you want. When you send a CV, you should send at least five or ten: one for the person reviewing it, one for their boss, one for the peers, one for those they will manage, one for the CFO, each over-indexed on what that person is looking for, but all consistent with each other."** This messaging/multiple-CV phase, and the consistency-across-CVs requirement, is the subject of the next memo and is not developed here.

## The Commercial Angle: Recruitment Companies

A commercial direction the project lead noted: **recruitment companies could host this and offer it to candidates as a service.** The project lead: **"recruitment companies could be doing this, hosting it themselves, adding this service to what they provide to candidates to help them."** A recruiter that helps candidates present evidence-anchored, role-tailored, honest CVs adds real value (better matches, less BS, faster decisions), and it is a natural channel (recruiters have the candidates). This sits alongside the candidate-direct use; the workflow can be hosted and offered as a service. Pricing and packaging are follow-on (cross-ref: the open-evaluation and commercial-model briefs).

Estimated effort: the workflow composes existing capabilities (the evidence feedback loop, the vault chat, research agents, the vault version control, sub-vaults and tokens, vault apps, infographics, skills). The new work is the CV-specific workflow, the evidence-anchoring and grading, the consistency analysis, and packaging the workflow as a publishable vault app. The project lead is the first user.

## What This Does Not Try To Be

- **Not a CV-writing autopilot.** It discovers and the user confirms; the human stays in control.
- **Not a way to cheat.** It is honesty-enforcing; exaggerations do not hold up against the evidence.
- **Not uni-dimensional.** It produces multi-dimensional, evidence-backed, brief-answering materials.
- **Not the messaging phase.** The multiple-CVs/messaging idea is the next memo.
- **Not unbounded research.** The research and loop are budgeted.

## Honest Risks

**Risk 1: Research about a person can be wrong or stale.** Agents can surface incorrect or outdated information. Mitigation: the user confirms and corrects (the human in the loop); evidence is anchored and dated ("on this day"); strong vs weak evidence distinguished.

**Risk 2: Privacy of the person's data.** A CV vault holds a lot about a person. Mitigation: the self-contained, zero-knowledge vault; the person owns it; sub-vault sharing is controlled by token and content; ownership and privacy are explicit selling points.

**Risk 3: Could be misused to inflate rather than anchor.** Someone could try to game it. Mitigation: the consistency mechanism makes inflation visible (the maps do not hold up); evidence-anchoring is the core; honest non-match verdicts are valid outputs.

**Risk 4: Cost of research and loops.** Multiple research and LLM loops cost money. Mitigation: budgeted (the per-round budget); the user chooses how much to spend; strategic compression.
