## The Thought Experiment

If you were building a news organisation in 2026, with the primitives now available (vaults, agents, semantic knowledge graphs, multi-modal generation, cheap research, hyperlinked storage, public-private vault composition), what would it look like?

This brief is the answer to that question. It is upstream of the Portugal publication brief: that brief picked a specific topic and audience; this brief defines the principles that any vault-driven news organisation should follow.

## The Core Reframe

Traditional news is **article-centric**. An event happens; a journalist writes an article; the article is published; the reader reads it.

Vault-driven news is **story-centric**. An event happens; the event becomes a node in a knowledge graph; the event accumulates evidence, perspectives, context, and follow-on coverage; the reader accesses whatever cut of the story is most relevant to them.

The article is one possible projection of the story. Other projections include the infographic, the short version, the long version, the deep dive, the audience-customised version, the visual timeline, the evidence pack. All produced from the same underlying graph.

## Design Principles

### 1. Human Involvement Is Maximised, Not Reduced

Critical to state upfront: this is **not** an effort to replace journalists with agents. The opposite. Agents make it possible to involve **more** humans, in more roles, at more scale than was previously affordable.

The roles that become viable with this architecture:

| Role | What They Do | Why Agents Enable More Of Them |
|------|--------------|-------------------------------|
| Journalist | Original reporting, primary source interviews, editorial judgement | Agents handle the production grunt-work; journalists focus on judgement |
| Researcher | Deep background, fact verification, historical context | Agents surface candidate facts; researchers verify and weight them |
| Curator | Selection of what matters, sequencing, emphasis | Agents produce more candidate material than any single curator could read; curators direct the agents |
| Translator | Localisation, cultural adaptation | Agents do first drafts; translators handle nuance and voice |
| Personaliser | Tuning content for specific audience segments | Agents generate variants; humans calibrate |
| Verifier | Fact-checking with domain expertise | Domain experts validate specific claims; their credentials weight the validation |
| Reader-Contributor | Adding evidence, context, corrections | The platform makes contribution easy, with attribution and weighting |

Funding rather than employment is the right model for many of these. A news organisation that pays domain experts to verify claims in their area, pays translators per article, pays researchers per topic: this is journalism economics with the cost structure inverted from where it has been for decades.

### 2. Stories, Not Articles

Each significant event becomes a story node in the knowledge graph. The story has:

- A canonical topic (as objective as possible)
- Multiple possible headlines (different framings for different audiences)
- Multiple article projections (short, long, deep dive, primer, opinion)
- An evidence pack (sources, documents, primary materials)
- A perspectives section (different stakeholders, different angles)
- A timeline of related events
- Cross-references to entities (companies, people, places, policies)
- A confidence model (what is fact, what is opinion, what is speculation)

A reader arrives at the story. They pick the projection that suits their context. They navigate to related stories via the graph. They contribute evidence or corrections if they have them.

### 3. Customisation Per Audience

The same story renders differently for different readers:

- A short version for a busy commuter
- A long version for a weekend reader
- A technical version for an expert
- An accessible version for a newcomer to the topic
- An audio version for a driver
- An infographic version for a visual reader
- A version tuned to a specific reader's prior knowledge and interests

These are not separate articles written by separate writers. They are projections of the same underlying story, generated by agents working from the same knowledge graph and the same evidence. The customisation cost is low because the source material is structured.

### 4. Evidence-Driven by Default

Every claim links to evidence. Every fact has a source. Every opinion is marked as opinion (with the opinionator named). Every weighted claim shows its weighting and who weighted it.

This is what semantic knowledge graphs make possible. The graph stores not just "X happened" but "X happened (source: A, B, C), confirmed by (D, E), disputed by (F)."

Readers see this. The default view shows the article with citations inline. The deep view shows the full evidence chain. The skeptical reader can verify everything. The casual reader gets the journalism with confidence in its sourcing.

### 5. Reader Validation as First-Class

The voice memo's strongest point: readers contribute to the validation of facts. Reputable professionals in a domain confirming a claim about that domain carries weight beyond a generic upvote.

Example: an article about a cybersecurity vulnerability. The journalist reports the vulnerability. Reputable cybersecurity professionals (validated via their own credentials and reputation in the system) confirm or dispute the technical claims. The article displays this validation. A reader new to the topic sees not just "this is what was reported" but "this is what was reported, and these named experts have confirmed it."

This works at scale because:

- The contribution surface is structured (vault commits, with attribution)
- The reputation system is transparent (who validated what, what is their track record)
- The validation is auditable (a future reader can see when validation happened, by whom, with what reasoning)

This is the semantic knowledge graph thinking from prior briefs (ontologist, content connectivity) applied to news. The body of work gets validated by the community that follows it.

### 6. Multi-Modal Production

Each story generates not just text. Each story can produce:

- Articles (multiple lengths, multiple framings)
- Infographics (ecosystem maps, timelines, comparisons, flows)
- Cartoons or illustrations (when the story benefits from a visual angle)
- Audio summaries (for the commute)
- Video explainers (for complex topics)
- Interactive visualisations (when the story has data that benefits from exploration)
- Evidence packs (the underlying documents, organised)

The infographic skill exists. Audio generation is cheap. Video generation is improving rapidly. The cost of producing the multi-modal version of a story is dropping; the marginal cost per format is small.

The journalist does not produce all of these. The agents do, from the canonical story. The journalist directs.

### 7. Public and Private Vault Composition

Some content is public (the article, the summary, the citations). Some content is private (the journalist's notes, the unconfirmed source, the document received under embargo, the work-in-progress).

Vault architecture supports both natively. Public vaults serve the published content. Private vaults hold the work product. Cross-references between them are managed by the journalist (what to surface, what to keep private, when to flip something from private to public).

This matters for serious journalism. A news organisation that mixes public and private content needs the private side to be properly private. Vaults are the right primitive.

### 8. Cost Tracking Per Story

Each story records its own production cost: which agents ran, how many tokens, how many infographics generated, how much human time. The cost lives in the vault alongside the content.

Why this matters:

- The monetisation model needs to know what a story costs
- The editorial decisions need cost as one input (this story cost £X to produce; was it worth it?)
- The funding model (subscriptions, micropayments, tips, sponsorship) needs accurate cost data
- The reader transparency option becomes possible: "this story cost £12 to produce; here is what went into it"

Cost tracking is a side-effect of running the production workflow through the platform. It is free data that compounds in value.

## What Was Previously Expensive That Is Now Cheap

The brief flags this explicitly because it changes which trade-offs make sense:

| Capability | Previously | Now |
|-----------|-----------|-----|
| Multi-language publication | Expensive translation budget | Cheap, with human review |
| Multi-format production (article + infographic + audio + video) | One full team per format | One person directing agents that produce all formats |
| Per-audience customisation | Impossible at scale | Cheap; each variant is a regeneration |
| Deep evidence chains and cross-referencing | Expensive editorial time | Cheap; agents construct the chain |
| Historical context per story | Expensive research time | Cheap; agents pull from the graph |
| Real-time updating | Expensive editorial overhead | Cheap; the graph and the story regenerate |
| Reader contribution tooling | Expensive product investment | Mostly built; vaults provide the primitive |

This is what makes this design viable in 2026 that was not viable in 2020.

## The Funding and Revenue Model (Tentative)

The voice memo flagged: monetisation comes after usage. Build the usage first. But worth sketching the model so the architecture supports it.

Likely revenue sources:

| Source | What It Funds |
|--------|--------------|
| Reader subscriptions | Core operating costs, journalist salaries |
| Domain expert paid validation | Compensates verifiers per contribution |
| Sponsored deep dives | A specific story or topic underwritten transparently |
| Sponsored infographics | Visual content paid for (with disclosure) |
| Per-story tipping or micropayments | Direct support for stories that matter |
| Foundation grants | For investigative work, public-interest topics |
| Premium per-audience customisation | A reader pays for a deeply personalised feed |
| API access | Other publications licence the underlying graph |
| Training data licensing | The fact-checked graph has value as training material (with consent) |

The vault architecture means most of these revenue streams can be supported. Each story carries its own cost data; each reader carries their own access state; each contributor carries their own attribution. The platform does not need a separate payments and identity layer; the vault model already does this.

## Connection to the Portugal Publication

The Portugal brief picked a specific topic (Portuguese GenAI) and audience (PT + EN readers in that ecosystem). This brief defines the principles for any topic and any audience.

The Portugal publication is the first instance. If it works, the platform supports many instances:

- A sector publication (cybersecurity, fintech, healthcare AI, climate)
- A geography publication (Brazil, Spain, India, regional)
- A community publication (open source ecosystem, specific industry vertical)
- A topic publication (AI policy, AI safety, AI in education)

Each one runs on the same infrastructure with different content focus. The principles in this brief apply to all of them.

## What This Implies for the Platform

If we want to support news publications at scale, the platform needs:

| Capability | Status |
|-----------|--------|
| Vault-backed publishing | Built |
| Bilingual content management | Defined in the Portugal brief |
| Knowledge graph backbone | Built (ontologist) |
| Multi-modal content generation | Mostly built (infographic, content agents) |
| Cost tracking per piece of content | Easy add |
| Reader contribution surface | Defined in this brief; needs UI |
| Reputation and validation system | New; needs design |
| Public-private vault composition | Vaults support this naturally; UX needs work |
| Subscription and payment integration | Deferred to revenue phase |

Most of this exists. The remaining work is in surfaces (reader contribution UI, reputation system), not in fundamentals.

## Why This Matters Strategically

A news organisation is one of the most demanding possible use cases for a publishing platform. If the platform supports vault-backed agentic journalism well, it supports almost everything else by default:

- Corporate communications
- Industry publications
- Academic publishing
- Community newsletters
- Knowledge bases
- Documentation sites
- Educational content

Building for news pushes the platform to be excellent at the hard cases. The easier cases come for free.

This is why the Portugal publication matters beyond Portugal: it is the demonstration that the platform can support serious journalism. Once it can, everything else above it is easier to address.
